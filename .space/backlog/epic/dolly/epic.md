## Premise

A poet in New Delhi beholds Dolly as a living bridge between the present city and the sacred river-imagination of Radha at the Yamuna. Her beauty is not treated as ornament or conquest; it becomes a test of the poet's inner refinement. The central movement of the book is from dazzled attraction toward reverent seeing, from private longing toward a disciplined love that protects the beloved's dignity.

## Thematic Grounding

This poetry sequence draws its spiritual atmosphere from Radha-Krishna devotion, the remembered Yamuna, and the ordinary astonishment of seeing sacred grace inside modern life. New Delhi appears as traffic, dust, evening lamps, metro stations, balconies, markets, and restless speech; Braj appears as water, anklets, clay, flute-notes, kadamba shade, and the pitcher resting in the lap. The poems should keep both worlds alive without collapsing Dolly into a mythological figure. She may resemble Radha to the poet, but she remains Dolly: contemporary, self-possessed, and human.

The philosophical conflict is the ethics of adoration. Can beauty be praised without being possessed? Can a poet turn desire into prayer without hiding from desire's human heat? Can memory become a river rather than a cage? The sequence answers through image, restraint, and transformation rather than argument.

## Poetic Voice And Tradition

The voice should be lyrical, reverent, image-rich, and contemporary, with a devotional undercurrent. It may borrow the emotional grammar of bhakti poetry, but it should avoid archaic imitation. The poems should carry long, flowing sentences where appropriate, luminous images, and a serious cadence shaped by tenderness rather than melodrama.

## Quality Mandate

Each poem should preserve a three-part structure: Question, Oration, and Benediction. The imagery should remain concrete and culturally grounded: New Delhi streets, Yamuna water, clay pitchers, evening lamps, monsoon air, temple bells, metro hum, sari fabric, and remembered Braj landscapes. The poems should avoid flattery that feels possessive, generic, or merely decorative. Dolly's dignity and independence are central to the book's moral beauty.
