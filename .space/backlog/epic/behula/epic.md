# Behula: The River of Devotion

- **Gist:** Behula, the Bengali heroine of the Manasamangal, sacrifices her social life and braves the river of death to revive her dead husband Lakhindar, her devotion so great that Lord Indra himself honours her character and the bhat flower weeps upon her foot.

## Premise

This poetry sequence gives Behula a living voice as the woman who refused to accept death as the end of love. Drawn from the Manasamangal, the medieval Bengali epic of the snake-goddess Manasa, the book follows Behula from her wedding night — when her husband Lakhindar is bitten by a serpent and dies — through her long, solitary voyage down the river with his body, to the court of the gods where her devotion moves heaven itself. She is not merely a mourner; she is a pilgrim who turns grief into a journey, a wife who turns love into a force that can argue with fate. The dramatic engine is the contradiction between a society that would bury a woman's life with her husband and a woman who refuses that burial. Her transformation is from sheltered bride to defiant lover, and finally to a figure whose character is honoured by Lord Indra himself, while the bhat flower — the humble flower of the riverbank — weeps upon her foot in witness to her sacrifice.

## Setting & Era

The book moves through the world of medieval Bengal and its mythic geography: the merchant town of Champaknagar, the house of the merchant Chand Sadagar, the wedding chamber where the serpent strikes, the river that carries Behula's raft, the villages and ghats she passes, the storms and hunger of the voyage, and finally the celestial court where gods and goddesses weigh a mortal woman's devotion. The geography is both real and symbolic: the river as the road between life and death, the raft as a floating shrine, the bhat flower as the earth's own tears, and the court of Indra as the place where human love is finally heard.

## Form & Voice

The form is poetry. Each poem should be a philosophical oration in three movements: a question, an embodied meditation, and a benediction or unresolved blessing. Behula may speak in first person, but the voice can also shift into invocation, lament, witness testimony, and moral address. The register should be serious, image-rich, contemporary, and mythically aware: sacred without becoming ornamental, devotional without losing force, elegiac without surrendering to despair.

## Thematic Threads

- Devotion versus fate: Behula's love is not passive acceptance but an active argument with destiny.
- Sacrifice and social death: she gives up her social life, her standing, and her safety to follow a corpse down a river.
- The river as threshold: water is the road between the living and the dead, the place where grief becomes pilgrimage.
- Womanhood and defiance: a society that would silence a widow is met by a woman who will not be silenced.
- Divine recognition: her character is not rewarded by chance but honoured by Indra, the king of the gods.
- The weeping flower: the bhat flower's tears are the earth's own witness, nature itself moved by human devotion.

## Topical Structure

1. **The Wedding Night** - Behula remembers the night Lakhindar was bitten, the serpent's shadow, the sudden silence, and the moment her world ended. The poem turns on the question of whether love can survive the body's death.
2. **The Widow's Silence** - Society arrives with its rituals, its pity, its expectation that she will now be buried alive in mourning. Behula refuses the silence and begins to plan her defiance.
3. **The Raft** - She builds the raft and lays her husband's body upon it, turning a funeral into a voyage. The poem turns on the image of a woman steering a corpse down a river as if it were a living passenger.
4. **The River's Road** - The long voyage begins: storms, hunger, the mockery of villages, the loneliness of the water. Behula speaks to the river as both enemy and road.
5. **The Mockery of the World** - People on the banks call her mad, a woman chasing a dead man. The poem turns on her answer: that love is not measured by what the world can see.
6. **The Court of the Gods** - Behula reaches the celestial court and pleads her case before the gods. The poem turns on the moment a mortal woman's devotion is weighed against divine decree.
7. **Indra's Honour** - Lord Indra, moved by her character, grants Lakhindar his life back. The poem turns on the recognition that her sacrifice was not for reward but for love, and yet heaven itself bowed.
8. **The Bhat Flower Weeps** - The bhat flower, the humble flower of the riverbank, is said to have wept upon her foot. The poem turns on the image of the earth itself crying in witness to her devotion.
9. **The Return** - Behula returns with her husband restored, but she is no longer the sheltered bride who left. The poem turns on what the journey has made of her.
10. **The Question Behula Leaves** - The concluding poem gathers the wedding night, the raft, the river, the mockery, the court, and the weeping flower into one final address. Behula does not boast; she asks what devotion is worth when it is not witnessed.

## World-Building / Atmosphere

The master metaphor is Behula as a woman carrying love itself across the water of death, a living soul steering a dead body toward resurrection. Recurrent images should include the serpent's shadow, the raft as a floating shrine, the river as a road of tears, the bhat flower as the earth's weeping, the wedding garland turned funeral shroud, and the court of the gods as a place where a mortal's devotion can be heard. The atmosphere should move between the intimacy of the wedding chamber, the vast loneliness of the river, the cruelty of the watching world, and the strange tenderness of heaven bending toward a woman's love.

## Conclusion

The emotional destination is not triumph alone. The book should end with Behula's question still alive: if devotion can move heaven, why must it first be tested by so much suffering? The final movement should leave the reader with awe rather than comfort, a sense that her sacrifice was not a transaction but a truth — and that the bhat flower's tears are the earth's own answer to a love that refused to end.
