## Premise

Meera Rathore, a hydrologist in Jaipur, comes home to the ridge-town of Mandera because her mother is dying and the mineral company has made an offer on the family house that would end three generations of stubborn tenancy. She intends to stay a week: sign, grieve, leave. Instead she finds a dry well that was never dry in her childhood, a strike over water in the marble quarry, and an old ledger in her mother's cupboard that proves the company has been borrowing the hill's groundwater for forty years and paying for it in a column that no one alive could read. As the monsoon breaks late and wrong, Meera must decide whether to finish the sale that would free her mother from debt — or to stand against the mine that fed her, educated her, and is now drinking the last of the ridge's water. The mountains, older than every empire that claimed them, watch both choices without comment.

## Historical Grounding

The Aravalli Range is among the oldest mountain systems on Earth, folded roughly 2.5 billion years before the present and worn by aeons into low, thorn-scrubbed ridges that run southwest across Rajasthan and into Gujarat and Haryana. Civilizations have quarried it since at least the Mauryan era: marble for temples, copper for coinage, and a reddish stone that appears in ramparts from Chittorgarh to Delhi. In the twentieth century the extraction industrialised — marble and sandstone quarries, zinc and copper mines, and a slow haemorrhage of open pits that have made the Aravallis one of the most mined ranges on the planet. Mining laws written in one generation have been reinterpreted by the next: what was a village common in the 1960s is a mineral lease today, and water that fell on a ridge legally belongs to whoever drills deepest.

Mandera is a fictional composite of such towns, set in the southern fold of the range near the confluence of the Banas and its tributaries. The ridge holds a marble quarry, a copper tailings pond, a single government school, and a temple to a folk goddess of the mountain pass whose priests have always claimed the spring above the town belongs to her. The town's prosperity and its ruin share one source, and the novel is the story of that arithmetic.

## Setting

Primary locations: Meera's ancestral house above the bazaar; the marble quarry and its tailings dam; the school where Amina teaches; the temple spring on the ridge; the district collector's office; the railway halt town of Kol; and the dry channel of the river below the ridge. The action unfolds across one late-summer-to-early-monsoon season, roughly six weeks, as the town inches from a water shortage toward a water emergency.

## Main Characters

- **Meera Rathore** — Hydrologist in Jaipur, back in Mandera to sell the family house. Professional, guarded, fluent in data and awkward in confession. The novel's eyes and its conscience, though she would refuse the word.
- **Bhuri Rathore** — Meera's mother, dying of a wasting illness, keeper of the family ledger and of the silence around the dry well. She has one secret she means to carry into the ground, and one debt she means to settle before she goes.
- **Bhanwar Singh** — The oldest living miner on the ridge, retired after forty years in the quarry, now the night-watchman of the temple spring. He believes the hill remembers every pick-stroke and every promise broken at the pit mouth.
- **Amina Sheikh** — Schoolteacher and volunteer archivist of Mandera. She has taught three generations of the town's children, including the mine manager's, and keeps the register of what each generation gave away.
- **Devan Chauhan** — Contract manager for the mining company, Meera's childhood friend, raised in the same bazaar. He respects the hill the way a farmer respects a field — as a thing that must produce. He is not a villain; he is the system's most reasonable voice.
- **Rasu** — A young migrant worker from the plains, new to the quarry, who digs the deepest cut of the season and falls ill on water nobody trusts. He is the price the ridge charges, collected and quietly unremarked.
- **The Priest of the Pass** — Nameless, elderly, appointed by the spring itself as far as the town is concerned. He speaks of the goddess and the water in the same breath, and refuses to pick a side he insists was always one side.

## Thematic Threads

- The oldest things are the most vulnerable; age is not strength but how long a wound has had to deepen.
- The distance between what the land gives and what the ledger records — ownership versus stewardship.
- Water as inheritance, as weapon, and as final arbiter.
- The children leave so the hills can hollow out, and return only when the hills can no longer hold them.
- What a place owes its dead, and what the living are allowed to keep.

## Chapter Outline

1. **The Dry Well** — Meera returns to Mandera to find the well behind the house empty for the first time in her memory and her mother propped by the window that looks toward the quarry.
2. **The Ledger** — Bhuri gives her the book of accounts; Meera finds a forty-year column of groundwater withdrawals signed by men who never lived in the town.
3. **The Tailings Pond** — Bhanwar walks the ridge with her and shows her the second landscape: the pond above the quarry where the mountain's water is stored, garish green, unclaimed.
4. **The Offer** — Devan presents the company's final terms; the offer is generous enough to be an insult and too generous to refuse.
5. **The School Register** — Amina reads her archive: every drought year, every failed petition, every name the town traded for a season of wages.
6. **The Migrant's Fever** — Rasu collapses; the doctor's guess is the water; the quarry denies it, and the town's two truths — its money and its illness — collide at the dispensary.
7. **The Spring** — The priest of the pass takes Meera to the temple spring at dark and asks her, as a woman who reads water, whether a spring can be owned by those who did not make it.
8. **The Hearing** — The district collector convenes a hearing on the quarry's groundwater licence; the ledger is entered as evidence; Devan argues for jobs, Meera for the column's missing zeroes.
9. **The Rain That Came Late** — The monsoon arrives as a violent, short squall; the tailings dam holds, but the dry well refills with water nobody will drink, and the town must decide what it believes.
10. **The Receipt** — Meera makes her choice: the house is not sold; the ledger is copied into the school register; and she leaves the ridge with the season's first true river running green and brown below her, knowing she has won a delay, not a victory.

## Scenes and Key Turns

- The childhood memory of drawing water with her mother, threaded through the returns of the present drought.
- The scene at the pit mouth where Bhanwar refuses to let the new cut touch the spring line, a confrontation of two old men and one young contract manager.
- The archive scene where Amina and Meera cross-index the school register against the company payroll — the same family names appear in both.
- The night of Rasu's fever, when the town splits between those who drink from the tanker and those who drink from the quarry's taps.
- The hearing, where a colonel-era revenue map is unrolled and the spring is marked "common," and the company's lawyer smiles because "common" never meant "yours."

## Tone and Style Notes

The prose should carry the register of the place: dry, patient, and sudden — the ridge's own rhythm. Sentences may run long through description of the hills and then stop short at a fact, the way the land itself stops at a quarry edge. The dialect of Mandera — Rajasthani-inflected Hindi in the mouths of Bhanwar, the priest, and the older women of the bazaar — should be rendered lightly in English, with key terms (paani, jharna, kuan) preserved where they anchor the emotional world. Water is the master metaphor: as ledger, as wound, as god, as debt. Each chapter should end with the sensation of a level falling — water, patience, or money.
