## Premise

The Aravalli Range is among the oldest mountain systems on Earth — older than the Alps, older than the Rockies, older than the Himalayas. What remains in Delhi NCR is a series of broken, battered, but stubbornly alive forest patches: the Mangar Bani sacred grove in Faridabad, the Jasola Bhatti reserved forest south of the Yamuna, Buddh Jayanti Park beside Chanakyapuri, the forested campus of Jawaharlal Nehru University, and the Okhla Bird Sanctuary at the confluence of the Yamuna and the Eastern Peripheral Expressway.

Together these fragments form an unofficial green corridor — a lung, a flyway, a watershed, a refuge. Around them, Delhi expands at speed: highways, metro lines, residential towers, data centres, shopping malls. Inside them, leopards still pad through the dark, neelgai graze at dawn, hundreds of migratory species stop over on their journey from Siberia to the Deccan, and a small number of human communities — forest guards, bird watchers, tribal memory-keepers, urban ecologists, Sunday walkers — hold their own kind of faith in the ridge's survival.

This book is not a lament. It is an act of witness. It asks: what does it cost a city to keep its forest alive? What does the forest cost the city in return — in restraint, in silence, in the slow discipline of co-existence? And what is the price of losing it — not in carbon metrics alone, but in the erosion of the imagination, the severing of the ancestral, the forgetting of how to be patient with something that grows on its own time?

## The Five Sites

### 1. Mangar Bani, Faridabad
The most intact forest patch on the ridge. Sacred to the Gujjar and Meo communities, whose tradition of reverence — no cutting, no hunting, no clearing — has preserved what state machinery and conservation law alone could not. The forest here is dense, dark, and genuinely wild. Leopards are regularly sighted. It is a living demonstration that sacrality is the oldest and most durable form of conservation.

### 2. Jasola Bhatti Reserved Forest
A patchwork of scrub forest, rocky outcrops, and reclaimed land south of Delhi, between the Yamuna floodplain and the urban edge. Less celebrated than Mangar Bani, more contested. Under constant pressure from real estate. The birding here — especially at dawn — is extraordinary, but most Delhiites do not know it exists.

### 3. Buddh Jayanti Park (Central Ridge Reserved Forest)
The most visited fragment. A curated edge between the diplomatic enclave and the forest interior. Walkers, joggers, families with children on weekends. And behind the manicured paths, a genuine forest with its own logic — sambar deer, rhesus macaques, monitor lizards, the occasional leopard pugmark. The tension between recreation and habitat is played out here every morning.

### 4. JNU Campus Forest
A university that accidentally became a wildlife sanctuary. The forested ravines of the JNU campus shelter more biodiversity than many notified reserves. Students argue politics under trees that are home to owlets and flying squirrels. The campus is also a site of memory: the Emergency, the student movements, the long argument about what a public university is for. The forest and the institution are both, in their way, acts of resistance.

### 5. Okhla Bird Sanctuary
Where the ridge meets the river and the city meets migration. Each winter, thousands of birds arrive — bar-headed geese, common pochards, painted storks, black-necked storks, spot-billed pelicans — from breeding grounds in Central Asia, Tibet, Siberia. They land on a water body flanked by a highway, a residential colony, and an industrial zone. And yet they come. The sanctuary is proof that wildness does not require wilderness — only water, and the smallest margin of safety.

## Themes

1. **Continuity vs. Rupture** — the ridge is 1.5 billion years old; the city is 800 years old at most. Which has the longer claim?
2. **The Sacred as Conservation** — Mangar Bani survived because it was holy, not because it was gazetted. What does this say about the limits of the legal?
3. **The Rat Race and the Ridgeline** — the book's central contrast: human urgency measured against geological time, financial quarter against migratory season.
4. **Urban Ecology as Ethics** — the question is not whether Delhi can afford to keep its forests, but whether it can afford not to.
5. **Memory, Indigeneity, and Belonging** — who has the right to speak for the ridge? The ecologist, the tribal elder, the bird watcher, the builder, the politician?
6. **Fragmentation and the Corridor** — what is lost when a forest becomes a series of islands? What survives, and how?
7. **The Non-Human City** — leopards in the ridge, vultures on the rooftops, kites over Connaught Place. Delhi is not a human city that tolerates wildlife; it is a shared city that humans have partially taken over.
8. **Sustainability as Ordinary Practice** — not a summit declaration, but the daily discipline of the forest guard who patrols at 5 a.m., the birder who counts, the community that does not cut.

## Voice and Register

The book is written in the first person of a witnessing narrator — part naturalist, part urban historian, part reluctant citizen. The prose is lyrical but grounded in observed detail: the specific bird, the specific tree, the specific hour of morning. It does not preach. It watches, and invites the reader to watch with it. The tone is urgent without being shrill, elegiac without being resigned.

## Key Characters / Voices

- **The Forest Guard** — a Gujjar man who has patrolled Mangar Bani since his father did. His presence is the thread of continuity.
- **The Birder** — a retired civil servant who counts at Okhla every winter morning. His notebooks are the closest thing the sanctuary has to a memory.
- **The Ecologist** — a researcher at JNU whose work on leopard corridors is the scientific backbone of the corridor dream.
- **The Developer** — not a villain, but a man with his own logic. His arguments are heard.
- **The Student** — a first-generation college student at JNU who has never seen a forest and finds one outside her hostel window.
- **The Ridge Itself** — present in every chapter as a structural and moral anchor. The oldest character.
