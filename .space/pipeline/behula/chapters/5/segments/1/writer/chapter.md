# The Mockery of the World

They stood on the banks and they laughed at her, and their laughter followed her down the river like a flock of birds that would not scatter. Look at the madwoman, they said, chasing a dead man down the water. Look at the widow who will not accept what the gods have written. And they threw their words at her as if words could sink a raft, as if mockery were a stone heavy enough to drag her under.

But Behula did not answer them with anger, for she had learned that anger is a weight, and her raft was already heavy enough. She answered them with the only thing she had left: the steady pull of her oar, the unbroken line of her course, the quiet certainty of a woman who has already chosen her road and will not be turned from it. And in that silence she was speaking louder than any of them.

For she understood that the world measures love by what it can see, and what it can see is very little. It can see a corpse and a widow and a few planks of wood, and it calls the whole thing madness. But it cannot see the vow that was made in the dark, or the fortress that was built in the heart, or the road that runs beneath the water where no eye can follow. And because it cannot see these things, it mocks them, the way the blind mock the sighted for walking toward a light they cannot perceive.

Let them laugh, she thought. Let them call me mad. For the love that carries me is not a thing that can be weighed on their scales or counted in their ledgers. It is not measured by what the world can see; it is measured by what the heart can bear. And the heart can bear more than the world knows.

And so she passed them, village after village, and their laughter grew fainter behind her, until at last it was only the sound of the water and the sound of her own breathing, and the long silence of a woman who had stopped listening to the world and begun listening to something deeper. For the world's mockery is a small thing, and it passes. But a woman's devotion is a great thing, and it remains.
