# The Return

She came back down the river with her husband beside her, and the water that had fought her now carried her gently, as if it too had been changed by what it had witnessed. But Behula was not the woman who had set out. The bride who had pushed the raft from the bank was gone, and in her place was a woman who had walked the road between the living and the dead and returned with something the world could not take from her.

She had left as a daughter-in-law, a wife, a woman whose worth was borrowed from the men around her. She returned as something else — a woman who had argued with fate and won, who had carried love across the water of death and brought it back alive. And the world, which had mocked her, did not know how to receive her, for she no longer fit the shape it had made for her.

The journey had not only restored her husband; it had remade her. The storms had stripped away her fear. The hunger had burned away her dependence. The mockery had taught her that the world's opinion is a small thing, and the silence of the river had taught her to listen to her own heart. And so she returned not as a woman who had been saved, but as a woman who had saved herself, and who had saved the one she loved.

And in that return there was a truth the world was slow to learn: that a woman's devotion is not a weakness but a power, and that the woman who will not be buried with her husband is the woman who cannot be buried at all. She had refused the silence, and the silence had broken. She had refused the grave, and the grave had opened and given back its dead.

And so she walked back into the world that had tried to bury her, and she walked as one who had already died and been reborn, and who therefore feared nothing. The river had been her road, and the road had made her. And the woman who returned was not the woman who had left, and the world would never be the same for having seen her.
