# The Police and the Curfew

The city had closed itself against the sickness, and the sickness had closed the city against her. The roads were barred, the checkpoints were manned, and the men in uniform stood at every crossing with the power to say who may pass and who may not. And Behula, who had to reach the hospital, who had to reach her husband, stood before them with her papers and her fear and her love, and she learned that the law is a wall that a woman must learn to climb.

They stopped her, and they asked her where she was going, and she told them, and they shook their heads. The curfew, they said. The rules, they said. Go home, they said, and the word home fell on her like a stone, because home was the one place she could not go. Her home was behind the glass of the ICU, and the road to it ran through their checkpoint, and they were telling her to turn back.

But Behula did not turn back. She stood her ground, and she spoke, and her voice was quiet but it did not break. She told them of the man who was dying, of the medicine that would not wait, of the blood that had to be given, of the love that would not let her stay home. And the men in uniform, who had seen so many and heard so little, looked at this woman and saw something they had not expected: a will that would not be moved.

And so she defied them, not with violence but with the unbreakable persistence of a woman who has nothing left to lose. She came back, day after day, to the same checkpoint, with the same papers, and the same quiet refusal to be turned away. And in the end, the wall of the law gave way before the wall of her love, because there is no law that can stand forever against a woman who will not abandon the one she loves.

For the world builds its walls to keep order, and the world is right to build them. But love is older than order, and deeper than law, and it will find its way through any wall, past any checkpoint, into any ward, to stand beside the one it will not leave. And Behula, defying the police to reach the hospital every day, was not breaking the law. She was obeying a higher one.
