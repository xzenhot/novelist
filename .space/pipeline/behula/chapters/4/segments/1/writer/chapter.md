# The River's Road

The river was not kind to her, and she did not ask it to be. It rose against her in storms that tore at the raft and soaked the body she guarded; it fell away in shallows that left her dragging the wood over stones; it carried her past villages where no one would feed her, and past nights when the cold came up out of the water and wrapped itself around her like a second shroud. And through all of it she spoke to the river as one speaks to a road that is also an enemy, and also a friend.

She learned that the water is the threshold between the living and the dead, and that to walk it is to belong to neither side. The living looked at her from the banks and saw a ghost; the dead lay beside her and did not answer. And so she became a creature of the between, a woman who had left the world of the living but had not yet been received by the world of the dead, and who carried her love through the middle of both.

Hunger hollowed her. The sun burned her. The rain fell on her and on the body of her husband alike, and she could not always tell which of them was the more alive. But she did not turn back, because turning back would have meant returning to the silence she had refused, and the silence was worse than any storm. The river could break her body, but it could not break the vow she had made in the dark of the wedding night.

And so she went on, day after day, a woman and a corpse and a few planks of wood, moving down the long water toward a court she had never seen and could not be sure existed. She had no map but her love, and no compass but her grief, and no companion but the river that carried her and fought her in the same current.

And in the long loneliness of that road, she began to understand that the river was not only a road. It was a test. It was the water that separates the woman who loves from the woman who merely mourns, and it would not let her pass until she had proven which one she was.
