# Indra's Honour

And then the king of the gods rose from his seat, and the hall fell silent, for when Indra rises, even the thunder holds its breath. He looked upon the woman who had carried a corpse across the water, and he saw in her something he had not expected to find in a mortal: a devotion that did not ask for reward, a love that did not count its cost. And he was moved, not by her beauty and not by her tears, but by her character — by the unbroken line of a will that had refused to bend before fate itself.

And so he granted her what she had not dared to ask for, and what she had never once demanded: the life of her husband, given back. And in the granting of it, heaven itself bowed, for the gods understood that they were not rewarding a woman; they were acknowledging a truth. Her sacrifice had not been a transaction, a coin paid to purchase a miracle. It had been a gift, given freely, and a gift given freely is the one thing even the gods cannot repay.

Behula did not fall to her knees in gratitude, for she had not come to be granted anything. She had come to be heard. And now that she was heard, she stood in the hall of the gods as a woman who had won not a prize but a recognition — the recognition that her love was real, that her grief was not madness, that the road she had walked was not a road of folly but a road of truth.

And the gods looked at one another, and they understood that something had changed in the order of things. A mortal had argued with fate, and fate had listened. A woman had refused the silence, and heaven had answered. And the king of the gods, who had seen empires rise and fall, had been taught something new by a widow on a raft: that the greatest power in the world is not the thunder, but the love that will not let go.

For her sacrifice was for love, not for reward. And yet heaven itself bowed, because even the gods must bow to a love that is true.
