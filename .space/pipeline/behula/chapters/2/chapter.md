# The Widow's Silence

They came to her with their advice, and their advice was a second death. They came with lowered eyes and with soft words, with the pity that is spoken to a woman who is expected to disappear. They did not ask her what she wanted; they told her what she was now. A housewife, they said, is a house whose lamp has gone out when the earner falls, a field that will bear no more, a name that should be spoken softly and then forgotten. And they waited for her to accept the silence they had already prepared for her.

But Behula looked at their pity and saw not comfort but a surrender. She looked at their lowered eyes and saw not kindness but a command. And she understood that the world was not mourning her husband; it was burying her. The same sickness that was taking his breath was being asked to take her life as well, and no one thought this strange, because it had always been so.

She had been taught, as every woman is taught, that her worth was a borrowed thing — borrowed from her father, then from her husband, then from her son. And now that the husband was failing, the world moved to take back the loan. But Behula had learned something in the dark of that first night, and she would not unlearn it. She had learned that a woman's life is her own, and that no custom, however old, can make a gift of what was never theirs to give.

And so she refused the silence. Not with shouting, for she was not yet strong enough to shout. But with a stillness that was louder than any cry — a stillness that said, I am still here. I am not a house whose lamp has gone out. I am not a field that will bear no more. I am a woman, and I will not be buried while he still breathes.

The neighbours did not understand her. They whispered that grief had unhinged her, that she would come to her senses and accept her place. But Behula was already looking past them, toward the hospital, toward the road that no housewife was supposed to walk alone. And in her heart the first stirring of defiance was already moving, like a seed turning in the dark earth, waiting only for the season to break it open.

For she had begun to understand that devotion is not the silence they wanted from her. Devotion is a voice. And she would raise it, not in the house of the dying, but in the streets, where the living and the dying meet, and where a woman's love might yet be heard.
