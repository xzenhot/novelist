# The Court of the Gods

At last the river brought her to the place where the water ends and the sky begins, and she stood before the court of the gods, a mortal woman in a hall of immortals, a widow in a place where death itself was only a servant. And she did not kneel in fear, for she had already knelt to nothing but her own love, and love had made her fearless.

She laid her case before them as a woman lays a burden down, and her case was simple. She had not come to bargain, and she had not come to beg. She had come to say what no one in that hall had ever heard a mortal say: that a woman's devotion is not a small thing, and that it should not be weighed on the same scales as gold or grain or the prayers of the fearful. She had come to ask the gods to look at what she had carried across the water, and to tell her whether love, when it is true, is not itself a kind of divinity.

And the gods listened, and they were troubled, for they had grown used to mortals who came with offerings and with lowered eyes, and here was a woman who came with nothing but a corpse and a vow, and who looked at them as an equal looks at an equal. And in that moment the court of the gods became a place where a human love was finally heard, and the hearing of it shook the hall.

For she had done what no decree had anticipated. She had refused the order of things. She had taken the serpent's verdict and carried it to the very seat of heaven, and she had laid it down and said, This is not justice. This is not the end. And the gods, who had thought themselves beyond surprise, found themselves surprised by a woman who would not accept their silence.

And so the question hung in the air of that hall, heavier than any thunder: what is a mortal's devotion worth, when it is weighed against the decree of the gods? And for the first time, the gods did not know the answer.
