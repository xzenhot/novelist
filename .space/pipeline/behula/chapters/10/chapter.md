# The Question Behula Leaves

And now, at the end of the long streets, Behula does not boast. She does not stand at the gate and demand that the world praise her, for she did not walk the road for praise. She walked it for love, and love asks for no reward. And so she leaves behind her not a triumph but a question, and the question is this: what is devotion worth, when it is not witnessed?

For she knows that her story is told now, and that the telling of it has made her a legend. But she also knows that for every woman who is remembered, there are a thousand who are not — a thousand housewives who refused the silence in the dark, a thousand women who carried their love through a city no one saw, a thousand hearts that argued with the sickness and were never heard. And she asks the world to remember that the worth of a devotion is not measured by whether it is witnessed, but by whether it is true.

She gathers the fever, and the papers, and the streets, and the stares, and the ward, and the weeping flower, and she holds them all in one hand, and she asks the world to look at them and understand. The sickness struck, and she did not fall. The world stared, and she did not turn. The doctors listened, and she did not beg. And the flower wept, because the earth itself could not keep silent before a love that had proven stronger than the sickness.

And so the question she leaves is not a question about herself. It is a question about all of us. What do we owe to the woman who refuses to be buried with her husband? What do we owe to the love that will not let go? And what is a devotion worth, when no one is watching, and no one will ever know?

She does not answer, because the answer is not hers to give. She leaves the question on the streets, and the streets carry it, and it will be asked again, by every woman who ever loved enough to argue with fate. And that, perhaps, is the truest benediction of all: that the question keeps flowing, long after the woman who asked it has gone.
