# The Raft

She built her raft with her own hands, and in the building of it she was already answering the world. The world had told her that a housewife's place is the house, that a woman's road ends at the door. But Behula looked at the man she loved and saw not a thing to be left behind but a passenger to be carried. And so she gathered the papers, the prescriptions, the little money she had saved, and she turned a death into a voyage.

Her raft was not a coffin; it was a shrine that moved. It was not a surrender; it was a question set upon the streets, a question that would not sink. And as she stepped out of the door and into the world, she understood that she was doing what no woman of her house had ever done: she was refusing to let the home be only a place where the dying are kept. She was making it a road.

The people on the street watched her go, and they did not understand. They saw a housewife and a sick man and a few papers, and they called it madness. But Behula saw what they could not see. She saw that the road to the hospital is the one road that runs between the living and the dying, and that a woman who loves enough may walk it. She saw that the city, which had always been the place of endings, could be made the place of beginning again.

And so she steered her raft into the current of the streets, and the current took it, and the door fell away behind her. She did not look back, for there was nothing behind her but the silence she had refused. Before her was the whole long city, and upon it the man she loved, and in her hands the papers that were her only answer to fate.

She was no longer only a housewife, and she was not yet a widow in the way the world meant. She was something the world had no name for: a woman carrying love itself through the streets of death, a living soul steering a dying body toward the chance of life. And the city, which had carried so many to their endings, seemed for the first time to be carrying someone toward a beginning.
