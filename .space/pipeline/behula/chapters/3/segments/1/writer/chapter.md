# The Raft

She built the raft with her own hands, and in the building of it she was already answering the world. The world had told her that a funeral is a thing of earth — a pyre, a grave, a lowering into the ground. But Behula looked at the body of her husband and saw not a thing to be buried but a passenger to be carried. And so she bound the wood, and she laid him upon it, and she turned a death into a voyage.

The raft was not a coffin; it was a shrine that floated. It was not a surrender; it was a question set upon the water, a question that would not sink. And as she pushed it from the bank and stepped aboard, she understood that she was doing what no woman of her house had ever done: she was refusing to let the river be only a place where the dead are given away. She was making it a road.

The people on the bank watched her go, and they did not understand. They saw a widow and a corpse and a few planks of wood, and they called it madness. But Behula saw what they could not see. She saw that the river is the one road that runs between the living and the dead, and that a woman who loves enough may walk it. She saw that the water, which had always been the place of endings, could be made the place of beginning again.

And so she steered the raft into the current, and the current took it, and the shore fell away behind her. She did not look back, for there was nothing behind her but the silence she had refused. Before her was the whole long water, and upon it the body of the man she loved, and in her hands the oar that was her only answer to fate.

She was no longer a bride, and she was not yet a widow in the way the world meant. She was something the world had no name for: a woman carrying love itself across the water of death, a living soul steering a dead body toward resurrection. And the river, which had carried so many to their endings, seemed for the first time to be carrying someone toward a beginning.
