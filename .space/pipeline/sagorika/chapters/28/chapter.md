# The Monsoon

Who promised that relief would arrive gently? Sagorika heard the first rain upon the balcony rail and went out with her face lifted. All afternoon Dwarka had waited beneath a burden of air. Now the dust darkened, the leaves shone, and from the flats around her came the small cries of people greeting what they had desired. She held her palm beyond the roof and watched the water gather along her wrist like a loosened bracelet.

Below, a boy set a folded paper boat in the gutter. It moved uncertainly, paused against a leaf, then escaped. Sagorika smiled. The street seemed to be receiving forgiveness. But rain continued after the first gratitude had exhausted itself. The gutter filled, the boat disappeared, and water began entering the low doorway across the lane. A woman emerged carrying bedding above her head.

Sagorika went downstairs. Already the lane tugged at her ankles. She and a neighbour took the bedding while the woman returned for a metal box. From the balcony the rain had been a single beautiful thing; within it, the rain had become several urgent burdens requiring separate hands. They carried the possessions to a higher landing. The boy who had launched his boat followed with a pair of shoes held against his chest.

The monsoon had come as promise and flood, as the epic within Sagorika had always known. Yet knowing the phrase had not prepared her for the heaviness of wet cloth. She thought of the plain receiving its water, of the city building over the paths by which excess might depart. She would not summon a verdict beyond what she could see. Here was a threshold overwhelmed; here were people making a little height out of their willingness to move.

The woman sat upon the landing and opened her box. Papers inside were damp at the edges. She separated them carefully, less concerned with the garments than with these fragile proofs by which the city acknowledged her life. Sagorika found dry cloths. Around them other doors opened, some only far enough for a face, others wide enough for a family. She felt anger at the narrow doors and then remembered her own first position above the street, admiring the rain.

By evening the water had begun to retreat. Mud outlined the room it had entered. They returned the box first, then what bedding could be saved. The boy searched for his boat without hope and finally swept the threshold beside his mother. Sagorika climbed home with aching arms. At her balcony a few drops still shone upon the rail, as delicate as before. She touched them without lifting her face in celebration. Let water come, she thought, but let our welcome include the places where it cannot stop. Below her, the boy leaned his broom against the newly cleared door. That narrow dry line, won by several hands, was the evening's blessing, and it would need to be defended again.
