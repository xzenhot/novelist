# The Siri Fort's Silence

At dawn near Siri Fort, a runner overtook Sagorika, then returned to retrieve the bottle he had dropped. His irritation broke the morning's hush. She had come expecting silence to reveal what remained of old violence; instead she watched a living man argue with a cap that would not screw straight. He struck it against his palm, spilled water upon his sleeve, and laughed at himself. The small defeat admitted her into the day more readily than the solemn mood she had brought.

He had promised to come because she wanted to walk where the name of a fort and the softness of morning might trouble each other. Now he asked whether they could sit. She began to urge him onward, eager to complete the circuit she had imagined, then recognized the command in her own voice. Even peace could become a campaign when she insisted that another body keep her pace. They found a place to rest.

In the inward scene of her poem, she heard the Khiljis' building and burning as a far disturbance, not a recovered account of this exact patch of ground. The epic's fire passed through her, capable of shaping and consuming. She would not people the morning with invented soldiers merely to make the grass seem innocent. The past had its demands upon truth. The present had its tired companion rubbing one knee, and she could attend to him without diminishing anything that came before.

He told her he had slept badly. All week she had praised the stillness she hoped to find here while overlooking the sleepless room beside her own. Silence was not something a city owed her like a cleared path. It might begin with allowing a person to finish a sentence without turning it towards her subject. She put away the notebook.

Around them people continued their small routines. Someone counted steps under his breath; someone paused to adjust a lace; the runner returned, bottle finally closed, and greeted them with the embarrassed cheer of a man who had recovered his morning. Nothing here abolished the memory of force. Yet these unheroic occupations loosened its monopoly upon the imagination. Ground once spoken of through power could also bear fatigue, distraction, recovery, and a day whose purpose was simply to be lived.

Sagorika's companion leaned back and closed his eyes. She watched the light reach his hands. The ridge within her, that old bone upon which dynasties had placed their claims, seemed capable of a tenderness sterner than triumph. To outlast war was not enough. What followed must be allowed to grow according to needs war had never understood.

They took the shorter route home. She did not call it a concession. At the gate he touched her shoulder and said the walk had helped. Her notebook remained empty until afternoon. Then she wrote about the bottle, the sore knee, the sentence she had finally heard. The fort's silence entered through those particulars, a space made livable by the surrender of one small command.
