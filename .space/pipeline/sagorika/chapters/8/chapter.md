# The Khiljis

The flame beneath the kettle rose higher than Sagorika intended. A draught had entered the room in Dwarka, bringing the restless smell of evening, and I watched the blue edge turn fierce while we spoke of the Khiljis. The epic held them between building and burning, ambition taking the shape of stone and the shape of ash. I asked whether the same fire must always carry both destinies. Sagorika reached towards the stove and lowered the flame before she answered.

There was nothing magnificent in that movement. The water continued towards its boiling, and the room retained its ordinary size. Yet I had wanted a magnificent answer, a judgment large enough to contain a dynasty. She placed two cups upon the table. If the fire is yours to tend, she said, why must your first thought be of what the fire might excuse? The question unsettled my admiration for force. I had been treating ambition as a weather that overtook the soul, rather than a hunger to which a hand might give a boundary.

In her inward account the Khilji age rose behind the steam: walls taking their shape, a city imagining itself stronger with each new enclosure, the heat of desire passing from one command to the next. These were the epic's images, not a catalogue of monuments assigned to names. The earth beneath them held the troubling difference between creation and consumption. To raise a wall was not, by that fact alone, to shelter someone; to call a blaze necessary did not make the thing consumed return. Sagorika let the vision remain unfinished where praise most wanted to hasten it.

The kettle began to sound. I brought a cloth to lift it, then noticed that one cup was cracked along the handle. I had kept it because its painted surface pleased me. For a moment I considered using it carefully, as though caution might redeem my wish to possess what no longer served. Sagorika said nothing. I set it aside and fetched the plain cup from the shelf. Hot water entered the waiting clay, and the steam loosened between us. Our small abundance did not require every beautiful thing I had gathered to remain in use.

We drank slowly. Outside, windows brightened one after another, each a vessel for some private desire. I could not govern their fires, nor settle a dynasty with a lesson learned beside a stove. I could attend to the heat entrusted to me, and refuse the pride which calls every restraint a diminishment. Afterward we washed the plain cups and turned the stove fully off. The room cooled slowly without becoming empty or inhospitable. May the city learn the strength of a flame that is sufficient for its work; may the hand that can enlarge it also discover the dignity of less. In the darkened kitchen the kettle held a little warmth, and nothing had needed to burn away for us to share it.
