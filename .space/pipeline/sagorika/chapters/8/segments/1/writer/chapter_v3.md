# The Khiljis

At Siri Fort, where Alauddin Khilji's military arrogance has been flattened into a peaceful morning park and the joggers step over the bones of a burned ambition, the fire of the Khiljis still smoulders in the memory of the ground. They came to this plain with a fire in them that was both creative and terrible. They built, and they built well — walls and gates and markets and the great minaret that still stands — and they also burned, laying waste to what they had conquered and to what they had made. They were a dynasty of fire, and the fire did not distinguish between the thing it was forging and the thing it was consuming.

And the earth felt their fire on its surface, and it did not flinch, because it is older than fire and it has felt it many times. The earth knows that fire is not the enemy of stone; it is the maker of stone, the force that hardens and shapes and tempers. But the earth also knows that fire, left to itself, will burn everything, and that a people who cannot tell the difference between the fire that builds and the fire that destroys will end by burning their own house. For the uncluttered soul is the soul that has learned to tend its fire, to keep only what the flame can purify and to let go of what it can only consume. The Khiljis never learned this. They gathered and gathered, and they burned and burned, and in the end the fire that had made them great consumed them too.

The Khiljis are gone now, and their fire has gone out, and what remains of them is the stone they built and the ash of what they burned. The minaret still stands, and the earth still holds it, and the lesson of the Khiljis is still written in the ground: that ambition is a fire, and a fire must be tended, or it will consume the hand that lit it. The joggers run their laps, and the earth holds the ash, and the earth waits.
