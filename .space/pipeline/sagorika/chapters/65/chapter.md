# The Chandni Chowk

In Chandni Chowk, Sagorika lost the woman whose basket she had agreed to help carry. A cart passed between them, then three people arguing over space, and when the lane opened again there was only the basket's other handle swinging loose in her hand. Within lay green leaves, a wrapped parcel, and a child's shoe. Nothing told her where the owner lived. She stood in the current of bodies with her red mouth parted, suddenly ridiculous: the woman who was Delhi could not find one woman in her own city.

She moved against the wall so the basket would cease injuring passing knees. Above the human voices rose the metallic complaint of something being dragged, then a vendor's call repeated with weary precision. The market seemed inexhaustible only to those who did not have to replenish it. Every shout came from a throat; every laden cart required shoulders somewhere behind it. Sagorika remembered the plain's grain beneath the city's feasts, the river beneath its cups, the ancient ground beneath this dazzling refusal to stop. She waited, resisting the temptation to make her confusion into a prophecy.

A child asked whether she was selling the leaves. She said no, and explained about the woman. He studied the little shoe, then pointed toward a turn in the lane. Sagorika almost followed, but the basket was heavy and the owner might already be returning. Instead she asked him to look from the corner while she stayed visible. He climbed no wall, performed no miracle; he merely stood where a taller body had blocked her view. After a moment he waved. A woman's voice answered from beyond the cart, angry with relief.

The owner returned carrying a small child whose bare foot explained the shoe. She had stepped aside to lift him and been carried farther by the crowd. Now she inspected her basket before thanking Sagorika, an order of events Sagorika found entirely just. Trust could be generous and still count its belongings. Together they put the shoe on the child. He kicked once in protest, then settled against his mother's shoulder. The boy who had helped was already leaving. Sagorika called her thanks after him; he raised a hand without turning, swallowed again by the labor of his day.

She carried the basket to the next crossing, this time on the same side as its owner. They learned each other's names only when they parted. The market continued, neither perfected by their kindness nor condemned by its confusion. Under evening's first lights its surfaces shone like the fresh red upon Sagorika's lips, while beneath them the earth bore a weight no window displayed. She walked home more slowly than she had arrived. A city might seem never to sleep because someone was always obliged to remain awake. Remembering the basket, she let a laden stranger pass before her, and kept the narrow space beside the wall open until both handles had gone safely by.
