# The Sultan Ghari Crypt

At Sultan Ghari, Sagorika's companion began with a royal name, but his voice faltered before the thought of a son. They had come to look at old stone. Now the word altered the scale of the place, drawing it from the long account of dynasties into a room where somebody had once waited for footsteps. She did not claim to know that room. The crypt's darkness would not furnish a mother's exact cry merely because a poet wished to hear it.

He continued reading, uncertain of the tale. She asked him to stop where his certainty stopped. Around them the present went on quietly: cloth brushing stone, a foot finding its place, light holding at the edge of an opening. Within her, the woman who had watched kingdoms rise felt no triumph in having outlived them. Longevity was a poor crown beside the thought of one life ended before those who loved it were ready.

She remembered a neighbour who had kept her dead son's cup upon a shelf. Once Sagorika had offered to help clear the room, misunderstanding the careful arrangement as a task left undone. The neighbour had thanked her and declined. That refusal returned now with the force of instruction. A thing could remain because its keeper had chosen it, not because grief had failed to advance. The city buried its dead and continued; continuation did not settle what was owed to them.

Her companion saw that she had grown quiet and asked whom she remembered. She told him about the cup. He did not answer with a larger sorrow. Instead he moved aside so another visitor could pass, and the small courtesy gave the silence a shape they could inhabit. The earth beneath the tomb held neither a contest of suffering nor an explanation for it. She imagined its old bone bearing the weight without calling that endurance justice.

She wanted to kneel, but feared borrowing a devotion she had not earned. She stood with her hands still. The vanished river came into her mind, not as a lineage connecting waters and bones, but as the image by which she understood an absence continuing to shape a place. One could walk beside what no longer flowed. One could thirst in sight of a vessel left deliberately empty.

When they stepped back into the light, the day seemed almost immodest in its brightness. A message waited on her phone from the neighbour, asking whether she would be home that evening. Sagorika answered yes before composing any account of what she had seen. The word contained no wisdom. It merely made a chair and an hour available.

Later they drank tea together. The son's cup stayed upon the shelf, while two other cups warmed their hands. Sagorika did not mention the crypt until asked about her day. Then she described the edge of light, the hush, and the point where she had ceased pretending to know. Her neighbour listened. Outside, Delhi continued building, and for once its continuance did not require that every room be cleared.
