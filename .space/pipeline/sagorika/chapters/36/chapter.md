# The Meeting of Rock and River

How shall what stands greet what passes? Sagorika carried a small stone from the Ridge toward the Yamuna's plain, not as a treasure but as a question she could hold. By afternoon its weight had become familiar in her bag. The city crossed between those two presences in roads, walls, hurried conversations. At the bank she took it out and set it upon the young, damp soil.

Beside her a woman was washing mud from a cloth. She wrung it slowly, turning the water back toward the place from which it had come. Sagorika watched the droplets make small pits around the stone. The ancient fragment did not become young because the water touched it; the new soil did not become ancient because it bore the fragment's weight. Their meeting required neither to surrender its difference.

A child approached and asked why she had brought a stone to the river. To see them together, Sagorika answered. He considered this, then pressed the stone into the mud until only its upper surface showed. Now they are together, he said. She laughed, but felt the uneasiness beneath his certainty. Had she imagined meeting as possession, union as one body disappearing into another? Even tenderness could be careless in the names it gave itself.

She loosened the stone, leaving it half exposed. Inwardly the Ridge and the plain became two measures of time within her own body: endurance worn low, renewal carried from elsewhere. Delhi had arisen where their capacities could be drawn together. Yet the city too often celebrated marriage while requiring the weaker voice to vanish. Let this joining be more honest, she thought, allowing hardness its place and softness its power to receive without being erased.

The child began building a tiny wall across a runnel left by the rain. Mud slipped between the stones he chose. Sagorika helped him see how the water found a way around every sealed entrance. He opened a passage deliberately, and the little current continued. His wall remained, changed from a command into a bank. The woman, gathering her cloth, said they had made work out of play. Perhaps, Sagorika thought, the distinction depended upon who bore the consequence.

Before leaving, she placed her carried stone above the wettest ground, where it would not obstruct the runnel. The child waved goodbye and ran after the woman. Sagorika stood between the slow rock and the restless surface, feeling neither required a vow. Their meeting would continue through weather, through sediment, through the city's attempts to divide what sustained it. She returned with a lighter bag and a less possessive thought of love. To remain beside another need not mean to halt their movement; to receive another need not mean to lose one's ground. At home she emptied the last grains of soil from her palm into a pot, leaving the open hand upon the windowsill until evening cooled it. Outside, the streets carried others toward their meetings.
