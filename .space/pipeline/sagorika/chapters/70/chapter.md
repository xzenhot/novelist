# The Daryaganj Books

Rain threatened while Sagorika was still kneeling beside the Daryaganj books. She had opened a volume whose former owner had underlined a sentence so fiercely that the page was nearly torn. What need had driven the pencil? She would never know. The seller was watching the sky rather than her face. When the first drops struck the plastic sheet, he began covering the nearest pile. Sagorika closed the book too slowly; a dark circle appeared upon its margin, and the sentence acquired another reader in water.

They worked without arranging who should do what. He lifted the books from the edge; she drew the sheet over those in the center. A loose page caught against her wrist. It carried a list written by hand, ordinary purchases beside figures, bread and soap sharing space with a phrase copied from somewhere else. She tucked it back between the leaves. The city had always stored its needs together, whatever grand division scholars later made between its hunger and imagination. Under Delhi's pavements the old rock and young soil kept their own unequal closeness.

The rain strengthened briefly, enough to make every uncovered surface seem accused. Sagorika held down a corner while the seller searched for a weight. He returned with another bundle, wrapped securely, and placed it upon the plastic. Water gathered in a shallow depression between the stacks. She lifted the sheet gently so it would run away from the books. For an instant she felt the vanished river as a tenderness in her own wrist: all that people expected water to nourish, all that they feared it might carry beyond retrieval. The little stream reached the gutter without entering another page.

Then the shower passed. They uncovered the books cautiously. Some corners had softened; one cover would need to dry apart from the rest. The seller did not declare the damage beautiful. Sagorika was grateful for this. She selected the volume she had been reading and paid without asking that the wet mark reduce its price. He wiped the cover with a cloth and placed it in a dry bag. The old underlining remained, its pressure entering the paper like a footprint which no subsequent owner could entirely smooth.

At home, she set the book open beneath moving air. The page would never be quite flat again. She read the marked sentence, but found that what held her was the space around it: the anonymous hand, the seller's practiced alarm, her own fingers keeping a sheet from folding inward. Words cost money, sometimes little money, yet their survival required acts no price could fully gather. Sagorika placed the handwritten list beside the page until both were dry. She did not discard the bread and soap in favor of the borrowed phrase. All three had belonged to one life. When she finally closed the book, she left them together, and the rain beyond her window became again something the thirsty earth might welcome without being asked to forget what it had nearly taken.
