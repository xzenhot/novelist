# The Alluvial Plain

Must the youngest earth ask leave of the oldest? Along the Yamuna's plain, after the water had withdrawn from a shallow reach, Sagorika walked where the soil still darkened her soles. She had come from the Ridge with dust in the seams of her shoes. Here each footfall took an impression, as though the ground were learning a new script from whatever passed. A woman ahead of her pressed a narrow furrow closed with the side of her hand.

Sagorika bent to help, too quickly, and her heel sank. The woman laughed without cruelty. Such a shoe, she said, had mistaken the invitation. Sagorika drew it free with a sound like a small mouth opening, then took both shoes off. The mud received her bare weight more gently. Against her ankle the older dust became a dark paste; she could no longer distinguish what she had brought from what had welcomed her.

The woman was setting a few seeds in earth softened by the season. There was no grand field in their immediate care, only this narrow place and the uncertain promise hidden within it. Sagorika asked whether the soil was new. New to this patch, the woman answered, and pressed another seed below the surface. The river had brought what it had gathered elsewhere. Youth, then, was not innocence of travel. Even the freshest deposit arrived bearing the consequence of distant weather.

She thought of the ancient stone beneath the city's layered life, and of her own young face above that antiquity. Men sometimes praised her as though freshness required forgetfulness. Yet here was renewal made from the carrying of old matter, tenderness born of grinding and distance. The plain did not contradict the Ridge. It answered a different need. What stood could shelter; what yielded could nourish. The city required both and too often honoured only what could be raised into a monument.

A trickle wandered into their shallow furrow. One seed floated free, circling like a little closed eye. Sagorika reached to seize it, but the woman cupped a handful of mud to turn the water aside. Then she lifted the seed with scarcely a ripple. The lesson entered through action: to preserve a fragile thing, one might first attend to the force around it. Sagorika rebuilt the low edge while the woman replanted what had escaped.

By noon the surface had begun to pale. They left a small depression where another watering could gather. Sagorika washed her hands, but a line of earth remained beneath each nail, younger than the rock she remembered and older than the morning's labour. On the way home she carried her shoes rather than wearing them at once. Nothing in the furrow assured a harvest. Still, something had been entrusted to a place capable of receiving it. She would not call that certainty. She would call it the present: a thin, worked layer over immense inheritance, asking care before it could offer bread.
