# The Agrasen ki Baoli

At Agrasen ki Baoli, Sagorika descended until the street's noise seemed to hover above her like something she had forgotten to bring. Each step lowered her through another measure of shadow. A young man ahead was photographing the depth, leaning to make the darkness look more complete. She watched him adjust the image and thought of the vanished river, how readily absence became beautiful when framed by someone who could purchase water on the way home. Her bottle knocked against her bag, a small rebuke she could hear.

Farther down, a woman sat with her child, who had grown frightened of the climb back. He had descended eagerly, counting the steps, but now the height behind him seemed larger than the depth that had invited him. His mother tried to persuade him to stand. Sagorika stopped at a distance and asked whether he remembered the number he had reached. He shook his head. She suggested that they need count only the next few, then rest. The young photographer moved aside, his screen still holding the enormous emptiness he had come to preserve.

The child rose. They climbed slowly, his mother behind him and Sagorika a little ahead, turning whenever he called. One, two, three; then another pause. The depth became a series of reachable places. At each rest he looked down with disbelief at the distance already crossed. Sagorika felt the heat increasing as they returned toward daylight. Beneath her heels the steps remained firm, yet the effort had entered her knees. It was easy to speak of descent as knowledge when another body did not require you to help it rise.

Halfway up they shared the shade. The mother took out a bottle and found it nearly empty. Sagorika offered hers, pouring a little into the child's cup rather than asking him to drink from her mouth. He watched the water level with an attention the architecture had not received. No reflection of the whole sky was necessary to make that small surface precious. She thought of Saraswati as the book of grief within her, the dry vein through which imagination traveled. Here thirst asked for water itself, and would remain thirst if answered only with a river's name.

At the top, the child turned and counted the first few steps downward without moving. Then he took his mother's hand and walked toward the street. Sagorika stayed beside the opening until her breath settled. She had brought the earth's depths into her speech so often that she had almost forgotten the labor of leaving them. The young man emerged behind her, looked at his picture, and put the telephone away. She could not know what he had understood. Her own bottle was lighter. That measurable difference went home with her through Delhi's widening noise. Before evening she filled it again, watching the clear column rise. She would remember the well's shadows; she would also remember that a child had needed four small rests, a cup of water, and someone willing to count only as far as he could go.
