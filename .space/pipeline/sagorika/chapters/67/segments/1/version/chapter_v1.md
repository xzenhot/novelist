# The Kinari Bazaar

Sagorika entered Kinari Bazaar intending to buy a narrow border for a plain red garment. The seller drew lengths of glittering trim across the counter until the surface resembled a small, obedient sunrise. Each strip promised to change the cloth it touched. Beside her, a young woman counted coins twice and asked for something less costly. Sagorika watched the seller's scissors open above the shining braid. What portion of joy, she wondered, could be measured without cutting away the hand that hoped to wear it?

The young woman had brought a sleeve to show the color. Its edge was worn where someone had unpicked an earlier decoration. She laid it gently upon the counter, covering the frayed place with her thumb. A companion urged her to choose quickly. The woman asked to see one length again, then shook her head at the price. Sagorika almost offered the difference. The impulse rose in her like an easy blessing, generous because it would cost her little, dangerous because she had not been asked. She held it back and looked instead at the sleeve.

The seller found a shorter remnant, sufficient for one edge but not both. The companion dismissed it. The woman did not. She set it diagonally across the fabric, moved it, and smiled for the first time. What had been insufficient as a border might become a different ornament. Sagorika placed her own undecorated cloth beside it, asking whether the same arrangement would suit hers. Now the young woman leaned across the counter with assurance, explaining where each bright line should stop. The seller waited while the design passed from one pair of hands to another.

Outside, wind dragged a shred of discarded tinsel along the ground. It caught at a crack, flashed, and went dark. Beneath the shop's abundance lay the city Sagorika knew: the old ridge wearing down, the earth supporting every purchased radiance without receiving a receipt. Yet she did not rebuke the glitter. A woman had a right to pleasure that need not justify itself as hunger, marriage, duty, or grief. The fault would be to insist that brightness made her life complete, or that wanting it made her shallow. Sagorika chose her braid and paid the stated price.

The young woman gathered her remnant and sleeve. Before leaving, she drew the arrangement on the back of Sagorika's paper packet so that she would remember how to sew it. The pencil made a plain dark path through the possibility of gold. At home, Sagorika followed that path imperfectly. One line wandered; another had to be unpicked. She kept working until the garment held both the purchased shine and the evidence of her own hands. When she wore it, she remembered no rescue, no lesson delivered to someone poorer. She remembered a stranger finding another shape for beauty, and making room within that discovery for her. The city glimmered beyond her window. For once its light seemed large enough to include the unfinished seam.
