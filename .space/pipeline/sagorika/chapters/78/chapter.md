# The Najafgarh Drain

Beside the Najafgarh Drain, Sagorika recognized a wrapper like one she had thrown away that morning. It was not hers; she could not claim the private accuracy of that accusation. Yet the familiar colors caught against the edge with a persistence she could not dismiss. At home the wrapper had vanished into a bin, and she had called the room clean. Here disappearance ended. The city's hidden passage opened before her, carrying what its bright rooms no longer wished to see. Where, she wondered, had she imagined away to be?

A worker was gathering litter from the nearby ground. Sagorika began to approach the water, thinking she might retrieve the floating scrap, but he told her to stay back. There was loose ground ahead. She stopped. The wish to correct her own discomfort had nearly become another person's emergency. Instead she asked where the dry litter should go. He indicated a collection bag, and she picked up the pieces within safe reach of the path. The floating wrapper remained beyond them. It was difficult to leave the most visible image of her shame untouched.

The worker did not discuss symbolism. He showed her how a torn bag could spill what had already been collected, and asked her to hold its mouth while he transferred the contents. She obeyed. The weight surprised her: all these light things becoming heavy together. Beneath their feet the earth received the pressure without comment. Sagorika thought of the ridge's old bone, the buried water of her dreams, the city she had called her body. A body was not only the face it offered a lover. It was also what moved through its hidden passages, and the labor required when those passages were harmed.

When the bag was secured, the worker wiped his hands and drank water. She stood aside, unwilling to turn his brief rest into an interview he had not invited. He asked whether she lived nearby. She said Dwarka. He nodded and returned to the task that would continue after her departure. Sagorika gathered the last dry scraps beside her shoes. She had done little. The plain measure of it was preferable to the imaginary enormity she could obtain by declaring herself awakened. The channel still carried its burden; one bag stood tied upon the ground.

At home, she looked again at the place where the morning's wrapper had disappeared. She changed no history by looking. She began, instead, to separate what could be kept clean and recovered from what she had carelessly mixed, and to learn where each collection actually went. The questions were tedious beside a poem about a lost river. She wrote them anyway. Through the open window came Delhi's evening noise, the familiar assurance that everything was proceeding. Sagorika no longer heard that assurance as permission to stop following the things she used. Somewhere the bright wrapper would lodge again. Its journey would not become harmless because her own small room had regained its order, and she would not call that order innocence again.
