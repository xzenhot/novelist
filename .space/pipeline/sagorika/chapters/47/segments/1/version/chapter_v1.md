# The Manger Jungle

They entered the Manger jungle seeking a place where nobody would ask who they were to one another. Sagorika wore her bright city mouth and carried her shoes in one hand; her beloved walked ahead, parting the thin branches as though privacy were a door he could hold for her. Under them the air changed, and their whispered laughter seemed to belong to a younger language than the one they used at home.

He found a sheltered place and spread his scarf upon the ground. Here, he said, the world cannot see us. Sagorika almost accepted the gift. Then something moved in the undergrowth, a short urgent disturbance that made him rise. In the tale her mind was making, a wild boar passed near enough to unsettle their borrowed kingdom, though she could not see what had stirred. The green was inhabited. Concealment had never meant vacancy, and the lovers were not its only claimants.

They gathered the scarf and withdrew to the path. He had wished to offer a world with its witnesses removed. She touched his cheek. Was she less beloved where other lives continued? The city had taught them to confuse safety with enclosure, intimacy with a boundary drawn around everything else. Here the leaves admitted light by openings they could neither number nor close. Their affection must learn such a shape.

They sat on a fallen branch beside the path, close enough that their shoulders met. Sagorika told him about a day when she had hidden her tears in a crowded train, and how a stranger had moved a bag without asking why. That small provision had given her more shelter than an empty room. He listened without seeking to become the hero of the recollection. Around them the forest held its own unfinished business: a dry leaf dropping, an insect's persistent sound, something feeding beyond sight.

Then he confessed that he feared being known as someone who could lose her. The words took longer to leave him than the earlier promises. She set her dusty feet upon the earth. Even the mountain, she said, is altered by what it holds. Thou canst not love the woman and forbid the weather passing through her. She did not offer eternity as consolation. Her hand remained in his, warm with the very life that made consolation difficult.

They stayed until the light began to slope. Nothing granted permission; nothing drove them out. Before leaving, he shook the scarf gently so that the small leaves returned where they belonged. She found the shoes heavier than when she had carried them in. They would be needed again among the streets, their hard bright rhythm part of the woman she was, but she no longer mistook that rhythm for the whole of walking.

At the forest's edge they put a little distance between themselves to let others pass. Their hands met again afterwards without haste. Behind them the green closed upon no secret it was obliged to keep. They returned to Delhi carrying an intimacy spacious enough to have neighbours.
