# The Heat Island

Why does the night refuse to cool what the day has surrendered? Sagorika lay awake in Dwarka, listening to the fan turn the same warm air above her. Outside, the walls released the heat they had gathered, as though each building possessed a grievance against sleep. She rose and pressed her palm to the balcony floor. Long after sunset, the city continued repeating the sun.

On the neighbouring balcony a woman rocked a wakeful child. Neither mother nor child spoke. Their stillness looked more exhausted than any cry. Sagorika brought two chairs to the corridor where a slight movement of air could be felt, and called softly across. The woman hesitated, then came. They sat with the sleeping flats behind them and the child's uncertain breathing between them.

The child demanded a story. Sagorika began with the Ridge, the broken green heights that remained beneath and within Delhi. She spoke of shade lying over old stone, of the earth's oldest bone wearing leaves instead of a crown. Yet the child wanted to know whether the mountain was hot too. She stopped. A story that made the Ridge an enchanted escape would merely carry their thirst into another false promise.

Yes, she answered, stone could be hot. But a place with trees and open earth received and released the day differently from a sealed court of walls. She offered this as a thought they could test with their own bodies when morning came, not as a spell for sleep. The mother laughed faintly at the prospect of an expedition before breakfast. Still, when the child asked again, she agreed.

At dawn they walked to a patch of shade near the trees. The difference was modest, precious, insufficient to cure the city. Sagorika placed the child's hand first against a sunward wall and then beneath the shaded leaves. He looked surprised that the answer could be felt rather than believed. His mother sat down, and for the first time since the previous evening let her shoulders fall. A little breeze moved the edge of her sleeve.

Sagorika knew the ancient volcanic fire of her inward mythology was not this fever made by the city's surfaces. To confuse them would be to excuse the work of human hands beneath the grandeur of origin. The walls had been chosen; the vanished patches of soil had once been present. They returned with no great remedy, only a sharpened understanding of what shelter meant. That evening the mother asked the neighbours whether their bare courtyard might hold shade again. Some shrugged; one brought an empty pot; another remembered where a tree had stood. Sagorika listened while the fan began its weary turning upstairs. The night remained hot. Nevertheless, a question had passed from complaint into company, and company might yet become work. She carried water down for the first pot, knowing that one small root could neither absolve nor replace the forest the city had forgotten.
