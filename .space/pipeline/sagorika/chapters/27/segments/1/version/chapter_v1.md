# The Dust Storm

By that late afternoon the desert had entered the colour of the sky, though Delhi still believed itself securely elsewhere. Sagorika stood outside a shop in Dwarka while the keeper argued with a cloth awning that would not stay tied. The wind arrived first as annoyance, turning the edge of her dress, placing dust upon her painted mouth. Then it deepened. Across the road, a line of trees bent as if something invisible had asked them all one question.

The keeper abandoned his knot and pulled at the awning with both hands. Sagorika caught the loose end before it struck a child's face. For an instant they were joined by the straining cloth, strangers bound to the same urgent task. Sand hissed along the shutters. The street she had known a moment earlier withdrew behind a moving brown veil; distance became something heard rather than seen.

Come inside, the keeper called. They brought the child and his mother through the narrow entrance. Others followed, lowering their heads. In the dimness Sagorika tasted grit between her teeth. The desert had crossed the imagined border by which the city separated its polished life from the land around it. No invitation had been sent. No guard could return these innumerable small arrivals to the west.

She remembered the Thar as the source named in the city's account of such storms, but what reached her now was not an account. It was a rasp in the throat, the sudden labour of holding a door, the blindness of a man searching for the hand beside him. Beyond that immediate force, her imagination heard the dynasties calling their walls permanent. Dust settled upon each claim without troubling to refute it.

A shelf near the doorway shuddered. Sagorika steadied it while the mother wiped the child's eyes with a damp corner of cloth. The keeper fretted over his goods until he saw the child's frightened breathing, then fetched water without being asked. Nothing magnificent transformed him. He merely turned from a possible loss to a present need, and that was sufficient to alter the small room. The awning outside snapped once more, then fell silent.

When the storm passed, the shop opened upon a street drawn anew in powder. Footprints began crossing it at once. The keeper found his awning torn, and Sagorika helped fold what remained. There was no reason to call the damage a blessing. Yet among the losses lay a memory of hands working without introduction, of shelter briefly understood as an obligation rather than a possession. She washed her mouth at home; the red came away upon the cloth with the dust. In the mirror her lips looked bare and tired. She left them so. The desert had not taught her to despise adornment. It had shown how quickly adornment could become irrelevant, and how much another person's open door might matter when the familiar road vanished between one breath and the next.
