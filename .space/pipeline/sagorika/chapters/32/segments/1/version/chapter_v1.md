# The River for Water

Before the city raised a banner, whose lips bent toward the water? Sagorika went down to the Yamuna in the pale early light with an empty vessel. In the city of her imagination she had watched empires arrive by this thirst, dressing need in the language of destiny. This morning there was no empire beside her, only a man rinsing mud from his hands and a woman waiting for him to finish.

The vessel was an old one, dented near its base. Sagorika had brought it not to drink from the river but to remember the weight of carrying. Filled, it pulled at her shoulder with an authority no inscription possessed. She moved a few paces up the bank and set it down. The woman smiled at her surprise. Water, so easily pictured as a shining surface, became labour the instant someone needed it elsewhere.

Sagorika lifted it again. Inwardly she saw the successive cities rising along their chosen ground, men praising rulers for what countless unnamed hands had made habitable. Each courtyard with its coolness implied a journey. Each full cup concealed a back that had bent. The river gave possibility; it did not carry every vessel to the mouth. Between abundance and refreshment stood the human arrangements by which some received and others bore.

A child running down the bank struck the vessel accidentally. It tipped, sending a bright sheet of water across the dust. He stared, expecting anger. Sagorika felt anger rise, sharp and disproportionate to the loss, and understood how quickly the burden of possession could turn a gift into an accusation. She caught his arm only to steady him. The water had already entered the soil; no rebuke could gather it again.

They refilled the vessel together. This time the child held one side while she held the other. Their steps were awkward, their shared weight unequal, yet he took such care that almost nothing spilled. They carried it to a little patch of dry earth away from the path and watered what grew there. Its leaves did not leap in gratitude. The act needed no visible miracle to have been worth performing.

When the vessel was empty, Sagorika remained beside the child until he ran back to his family. The dented metal was light once more, but her understanding had gained weight. She would return to Dwarka, to taps and pipes and the apparent simplicity of turning a hand. Let no convenience erase the river from the cup, she thought, or the carrier from the convenience. The dynasties within her had all required water and all passed away. Thirst endured in the living, a recurring command more ancient than their names. She walked homeward with the empty vessel striking softly against her leg, hearing in that hollow note neither triumph nor lament, but an unfinished question about who would carry, who would receive, and whether those places might at last be shared with justice.
