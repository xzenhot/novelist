# Prithviraj

At dusk among the old stones, Sagorika found me rehearsing the name of Prithviraj. I spoke it as though a name could summon a rider from the emptied air and make the ruin whole. What is a kingdom when it becomes a memory? I asked. She waited while a little dust settled upon my shoes. Beyond the wall the living city continued its unroyal business, and the sound of a passing vehicle entered the place where I had imagined only horses.

The epic gave her the king's rise and fall, the moment when possession loosened into remembrance. She received that outline without furnishing it with a battle she could not witness for me, without claiming the last words of a man whose silence had been crowded by other people's wishes. In her inward vision a rider passed across the plain and dwindled. The earth bore his weight, then bore the absence of it. No border upon the horizon could prevent the evening from travelling beyond the reach of his name.

I had brought a small red thread, intending to tie it round a twig beside the stones. It was my private ceremony, a way of binding the present to the courage I wished the past to contain. As I tried to make the knot, the old dry twig snapped. The thread remained in my hand, bright and useless. Sagorika stooped for the broken piece. The sound was slight, she said, yet you have heard it more clearly than all the hoofbeats you summoned. What will your remembrance ask of you now?

I wanted to defend the gesture. I had meant reverence, and surely intention deserved some refuge from its consequence. But the twig lay across her palm, and the king's name could not mend it. I loosened the thread from the break and put it away. Around us the scrub had no need of my vow. It needed the humble permission to continue, unadorned by my grief for a kingdom. I saw how mourning could become another form of command, requiring the living ground to display the story I had brought to it.

Sagorika set the twig beside the root from which it had fallen. The act restored nothing, yet it refused to make a trophy of the damage. We stayed until the stone had lost the day's last warmth. I thought of the king as mortal, no longer obliged to embody every loss that came after him. His name would remain in the poem; the evening would remain beyond it. When we left, I carried the red thread home, where it could bind a torn handle instead of a living branch. May remembrance become such a change of service: the hand withdrawing its demand upon the dead, then finding among the living something it can hold together. The road beneath us darkened, and the city received our returning footsteps without asking which kingdom had taught them how to walk.
