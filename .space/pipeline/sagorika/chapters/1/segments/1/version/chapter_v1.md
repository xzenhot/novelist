# The Woman in Red

Who is she, the woman in red, whom the evening glass of Dwarka gives back to every passing eye? I had asked the question lightly, as one asks the price of something shining. Sagorika stood beside the station entrance, her lips bright, her slender reflection crossing the reflection of a tower. I lifted my hand to summon her into the photograph. Behind her the city rose obediently within my little frame; beneath her shoes lay dust that would not keep its appointed place.

She came towards me, then stopped. A loose stone had turned beneath her heel. The movement was scarcely more than a hesitation, yet the picture I had prepared broke apart: a shoulder bent, a hand against the wall, the bright mouth drawn briefly with pain. I lowered the glass. The little screen went dark, holding only a dim reflection of the hand that had enclosed it. Until that moment I had wanted her beauty to remain undisturbed by the labour of being alive. Now she asked me to hold her bag while she loosened the strap, and I discovered how little of a woman can enter an image which has no room for her need.

I am the woman in red, she said, and I have been young for a very long time. Her voice seemed to come from the pavement as well as from her mouth. The Aravalli lay within it, worn and patient; the vanished Saraswati passed through it as a remembered thirst. She spoke of Indraprastha as a dream the ground had carried, of dynasties whose stone had outlasted their commands. She did not offer these names as jewels for my admiration. Each name made the silence beneath our feet more crowded, until the tower behind her looked less like an achievement than a weight entrusted to something older.

People moved around us towards the trains. A man glanced at her bare foot and looked away; another stepped carefully over the stone she had dislodged. Nothing in the street announced a revelation. I held the bag, warm where her hand had been, and waited while she tested the strap. The small service had no grandeur, and therefore I could not hide inside grandeur. Here was the city asking whether my praise could become attention before its light changed and called me elsewhere.

When she stood again, I asked whether she still wanted the photograph. She considered the evening, then smiled and shook her head. We walked towards the crossing at her pace. The red upon her lips remained red; truth had not required that she surrender colour, nor had care demanded that desire be extinguished. Only my wish to possess the whole of her in one unanswering surface had begun to loosen. Beneath the traffic I heard the thin scrape of her sole finding a steadier place. May I remember that sound when beauty next arrests me: the beloved earth moving beside me, asking to be met, step after mortal step, upon the ground.
