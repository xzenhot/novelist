# The North and South Blocks

Between the North and South Blocks, Sagorika watched a sheet escape from a man's folder. He lunged after it, and the remaining papers slid against his elbow like a flock preparing to leave. She placed her shoe upon the runaway corner before the wind could take it farther. The man thanked her with disproportionate relief. It was one page among many, he said, yet without it he might have to come again. Above them the buildings held their composed faces, while at their feet a life depended upon keeping its pages in order.

She lifted the sheet without reading it. He smoothed the place her heel had marked, then checked a number against another document. His hands shook slightly. Sagorika asked whether he needed somewhere to set the folder down. Together they found a level surface out of the strongest wind. He counted, beginning again whenever the edges clung. She held the empty cover open. The city that called her beautiful knew how to carve dignity into stone; it was less certain how to grant it to a man counting papers under an unforgiving sky.

When he reached the last page, he discovered that the sequence was wrong. He began blaming himself. Sagorika asked him to show her the order, and he explained it patiently, as though teaching her steadiness would restore his own. They arranged the sheets. The missing place turned out to be no loss at all, only two pages folded together. He laughed once, with more exhaustion than amusement. She thought of dynasties naming the ground their possession, and of the ground bearing each declaration until its seals and banners passed away. This folder, too, sought recognition from a power that could delay.

She did not tell him that every empire ended. His need existed today, and eternity would have been a cruel answer. Instead she found a spare clip in her bag and secured the papers. The little piece of metal was not enough to change the institution before him; it could keep his application from becoming weather. He asked her name before he left. When she answered, he repeated it carefully. Then he said his own, which she kept without turning it into an emblem of all who waited. One person was sufficient to remember.

The wind rose again after he had gone. Sagorika stood between the buildings and felt the air pull at her garment, searching every loose edge. Somewhere a desk would receive the folder. Someone might read it attentively, or return it, or require another page. She could not accompany his claim into every room. Yet she no longer saw the monumental faces apart from the trembling fingers below them. Power lived in stone only because living people agreed to meet it there. She turned toward the road, her heel carrying a faint trace from the rescued corner. Before evening she wrote the man's name in her notebook, not as a petition she could promise to answer, but as a safeguard against letting the buildings become the whole of what she remembered.
