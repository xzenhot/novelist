# The Chawri Bazaar

In Chawri Bazaar, a roll of paper escaped its binding and opened across the shop floor. Sagorika stepped back as the white curve advanced toward her shoes. The shopkeeper reached for it, but a gust from the doorway lifted its loose edge and carried it into the dust. He said something angry beneath his breath. She had come to order a few invitations, words announcing a modest gathering, and now watched the unwritten sheet acquire the street before it had received a single name.

She helped lift it from the threshold. Together they rolled it more tightly, pressing out the air. From another shop came the ring of metal being moved, bright strokes without a tune. Sagorika thought of how readily a city declared that a street had changed its song, as though all the singers had departed together and left no one behind to remember a room. The earth did not change in such clean verses. It kept broken rhythms, footsteps under footsteps, the ridge beneath the lane, a woman's voice beneath the uses others made of her name.

The shopkeeper showed her the sample type. It was graceful, but in the address one letter had been omitted. She pointed to the absence. He corrected it, then asked her to check the rest. Reading slowly, she found that the invitation named her companion first and described her merely as the person accompanying him. This had been copied from an old model, the shopkeeper explained. Sagorika understood; models had governed more formidable realms than a sheet of paper. She asked for both names to stand plainly, each complete, neither leaning for permission against the other.

While he changed the arrangement, she waited beside the rescued roll. A smudge remained along its outer edge. There would be work that could use it, the shopkeeper said, turning the blemish inward. She thought of the women whose histories became a picturesque sentence on someone else's walk, their labor and longing folded away so that the finished account might look clean. She would not invent their testimony. She could refuse, here, the small erasure offered to her own living name. The press of that refusal was quiet, no louder than the keys under his fingers.

The proof emerged. She read it aloud, listening to whether the words allowed her to enter the gathering she herself was arranging. They did. She approved the sheet and asked that the corrected version be kept for the remaining copies. Outside, the metallic ringing continued, joined now by a call from the lane and the scrape of wheels. Sagorika carried the proof flat against a piece of board. The street had not recovered any lost song for her, and she had not made herself its historian. She had placed one woman's full name upon an ordinary invitation. Beneath the white paper her hands were dusty from the rescued roll. She let the dust remain until she reached home, a faint record of what keeping a page open had required.
