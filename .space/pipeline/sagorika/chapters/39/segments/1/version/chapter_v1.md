# The Tropical Geography of Delhi

If Delhi were stripped of its names, by what would we know it? Sagorika asked the question over a sheet of paper spread upon her table in Dwarka. A girl visiting with her mother began drawing the city. First came tall buildings, then a train, then a woman with red lips. Sagorika recognized herself and laughed. The child had made the visible world with admirable economy, leaving a white space beneath everything.

What holds it up? Sagorika asked. The girl drew a straight line. They looked at that simple ground together. It seemed enough until Sagorika placed a glass of water beside the paper and asked where its contents had begun. The girl's pencil hesitated, then made a river at one edge. Where did bread begin? A field appeared. Where could a tree stand? The straight line grew uneven.

They took the drawing outside. From the road they could not see the whole geography, only fragments disclosed between structures, a patch of soil, distant roughness, the brightness of leaves. Sagorika resisted turning the walk into a lecture. The child stooped to touch dust and rose with a dirty finger. Here was an answer the paper had omitted, matter entering the maker's hand.

In the heat they sought shade, and the girl added a dark cloud to her drawing. Sagorika asked whether that cloud meant rain. It might, the child said. This uncertainty pleased her more than a confident emblem would have done. Climate was not a decorative border around the city; it entered the body and altered the day's choices. Their shortened walk had already obeyed it.

Back inside, the mother asked whether the city was finished. The girl shook her head and pointed below the line. She drew dark strokes there for stone, then a blue curve for hidden water. Sagorika gently said that a picture could show the idea without pretending to map every buried path. The child accepted this distinction more readily than many adults accepted limits upon their knowledge. In the corner she wrote that the drawing was what they had thought about Delhi.

Sagorika placed it near the window. Ridge, river, plain, weather: the old conditions had entered the child's modern city without driving out the train or the lipstick. The woman in red now stood upon something more substantial than whiteness. Sagorika felt the burden of that recognition, for an image of dependence could remain merely charming unless conduct followed it. She filled the child's bottle for the journey home and asked her to carry the paper carefully. Later, alone, she looked at the patch of wall it had occupied. No map could make the city just by representing its ground. Yet a hand that had drawn beneath the surface might one day hesitate before covering it thoughtlessly. Let that hesitation grow into knowledge, she thought, and knowledge into a way of building that remembered what made the building possible. The glass stood empty upon her table.
