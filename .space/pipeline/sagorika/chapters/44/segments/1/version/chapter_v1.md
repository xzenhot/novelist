# The Schist and the Sky

Before sunrise Sagorika climbed the ridge to watch the sky arrive, carrying a dark stone her beloved had wanted polished. Schist, he called it, pleased by the sound. He hoped it would shine upon their shelf. She had promised to consider it; now she turned its uneven face towards a horizon still withholding colour. Below her, Delhi's lamps maintained their little claims. The stone gave back almost nothing, and she wondered why she had expected recognition from a thing she herself had scarcely seen.

A child came along the path with a drawing board tucked beneath one arm. He chose a place near her and began to paint the morning before it happened, laying down a vigorous orange band. Sagorika watched without speaking. In her own mind the rock remembered a nameless sky, though she knew memory here was the language of her poem, not a message she could extract by breaking it. She wished to keep that difference clear. Reverence grew careless when it believed every beautiful statement true.

Clouds thickened. The orange the child had prepared found no answering fire. A pale grey spread across the city, and he scowled at his paper as though the heavens had broken an agreement. Sagorika recognized herself: the woman dressed in brightness, waiting for the world to become a suitable mirror. Her beloved wanted lustre from the stone; she wanted the first light to honour its age; the child wanted sunrise to resemble what he had already made.

A gust lifted his sheet. He snatched it, smearing the painted sky with his thumb. For a moment he looked ready to throw it away. Sagorika offered the stone as a weight. Beneath its rough edge the paper rested, and a narrow wet mark appeared where the paint touched its side. He stared at the accidental grey upon his thumb, then mixed another colour. The expected sunrise disappeared under a slower weather.

She sat while he worked. The ridge's oldest bone supported two bodies impatient in different ways, and did not rebuke them. A bird crossed the paling air. Traffic rose from the plain, gathering into the day without ceremony. On the child's page, a cloud acquired the shape of the very obstruction he had resented. His hand grew quieter. He was no longer summoning a spectacle; he was attending to what had come.

When he lifted the stone, a small unpainted place remained. He left it so. Sagorika wiped the damp colour from the rock with her scarf, but a trace clung to one hollow. She would explain that mark at home, along with her refusal to polish every broken surface into obedience.

They descended separately. At her door the first clear sunlight found the stone, illuminating neither its whole face nor any imagined beginning of the world. It lit a single edge. She set it beside the window, where weather would alter its appearance daily. Her beloved touched the roughness, listened to her account, and moved the lamp away. The sky had been given room to arrive.
