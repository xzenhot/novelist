# The Jama Masjid's Crowd

In the crowd near Jama Masjid, Sagorika lost sight of the woman who had been walking beside her. A moment earlier they had been discussing the price of fruit; then bodies moved between them, and the familiar scarf vanished. She rose upon her toes, calling a name that the surrounding voices absorbed. The great city within her, the city she claimed to hold like an ancient memory, could not help her find one living person a few paces away.

She tried to push forward, then stopped when her elbow struck an older man's arm. She apologized and stepped aside. Fear had made every other body seem an obstacle. Now she saw the burden in his hands, the care with which someone guided a child, the fatigue on a face turned towards an opening. The crowd was not a single disorder set against prayer. It was people, each carrying an errand or devotion that could not be read from outside.

From the space of worship came a stillness of purpose she felt without claiming as her own. She remained where visitors could stand, attentive to the movement around her. In the epic's long city, rulers had desired the earth, rivers had supplied thirst, stone had held their buildings. Here the question narrowed: could she inhabit a shared place without requiring it to arrange itself around her alarm? She reached for her phone.

The call failed. She moved to the place where she and her companion had agreed to meet if separated, a simple precaution almost forgotten in panic. Waiting there felt harder than searching. Her body wanted action, proof that love was doing something. Instead she watched the passing feet and held her bag close, making room where she could. Each minute asked her to distinguish attention from control.

A child dropped a small packet near her. Sagorika picked it up before it was crushed and handed it back to the waiting adult. The child stared solemnly, then smiled. The exchange steadied her. She had not found her companion, but she had ceased being entirely enclosed within the failure. The living ground did not have to know where everyone stood in order to bear the next small obligation under its feet.

At last the familiar scarf appeared. Her companion was irritated, frightened, relieved, all at once. They began speaking together, stopped, then laughed with tears close beneath the laughter. Neither was certain who had turned first. They let the question remain unanswered. Sagorika took the older woman's bag for a while, and they stood until their breathing settled.

When they moved again, they chose a slower pace and repeated their meeting place aloud. The crowd continued around them, holding worship, commerce, impatience, tenderness, and noises no poem could make wholly harmonious. Sagorika did not ask it to become silence. Near the edge she looked back once, hearing the city's many lives without reducing them to chaos. Her companion touched her wrist to guide her forward, and the touch carried them both into the open street, accountable to the next step together.
