# The Kashmere Gate

At Kashmere Gate, Sagorika raised her hand toward a mark in the stone and stopped before touching it. A young man beside her was trying to frame a photograph in which no passing body would interrupt the wall. He asked whether she could move aside. She did, and another person immediately entered the cleared space. The young man sighed. Sagorika looked at the worn surface and wondered how a city learned to remember injury while resenting the living who stood in front of its memory.

She carried no account detailed enough to make every scar speak. Some marks might have histories she could name only after study; some might resist naming altogether. The temptation was to let her ancient voice supply what she did not know, to say that she had watched each blow and therefore understood it. Yet even the earth, receiving everything, did not render every suffering intelligible. She lowered her hand. Beside the gate, a woman balanced a sleeping child against her shoulder and tried to retrieve a fallen sandal without waking him.

Sagorika picked it up. The child's foot stirred when she slipped the sandal on, and the mother held her breath until he settled again. The young photographer, seeing them, asked to take their picture. The woman shook her head. He lowered the telephone at once, then looked uncertainly back toward the stone. This small refusal entered the afternoon without drama. Sagorika felt its force nevertheless: a living person had kept the terms of her own appearance, while behind her the city offered old wounds for anyone to interpret.

The woman needed to cross, but the child made it difficult to turn and judge the movement around her. Sagorika walked beside her until they reached a quieter place. They spoke only of where she was going and whether she had far to travel. No ancestral voice issued from the gate; no imagined soldier furnished a blessing. The mother's arm trembled from the weight she carried. When they reached a place to sit, she lowered the child onto her lap and thanked Sagorika, who found herself relieved to have been useful in a way that did not require explaining the entire past.

Returning, Sagorika saw the young man taking a different photograph. People remained in it now, moving across the wall's patient surface. She could not know what he would call the image, whether it would preserve complexity or merely find a newer beauty. That uncertainty belonged to him. Hers was the responsibility to leave the stone unforced, its marks neither forgotten nor made to certify a story she had invented. The ridge beneath the city held fractures deeper than any inscription, and her own hands retained the warmth of the little sandal. She looked once more, then walked on. Memory would require knowledge, and knowledge the humility of returning. For this afternoon she carried a woman's refusal, a child allowed to sleep, and the unfilled space between seeing a wound and claiming to speak in its name.
