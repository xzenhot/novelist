# The Lalkot Walls

The gap in the Lalkot walls troubled Sagorika's companion more than the stones that remained. Through it the scrub entered freely, and afternoon light crossed what his imagination had supplied with a gate. He spoke of defence, of a kingdom's last resistance, of Prithviraj as though a name could close the opening. Sagorika listened from the earth's side of the tale. She had held walls and those kept outside them; neither kind of weight had cancelled the other.

In the story he wished to tell, the wall stood whole again, every stone returned to its proper place. She asked what would happen to the small plant rooted in a crevice. But she was not arguing against repair. She was wondering how readily a beautiful restoration could make present life disappear, how often the desire for completeness began by calling something living an intrusion.

A thorn caught her scarf. She turned too quickly and the fabric tightened against her throat. He reached to tear it free, but she stopped him. Together they followed the thread back to the branch, lifting it slowly until the cloth released. One pale line remained across the red. Her breath returned with an anger she could not immediately name. The wall, the scarf, the kingdom in his voice had briefly become one wish: let nothing cross except by my permission.

She sat in the available shade. He waited, chastened by her silence without understanding it. At last she told him about the times protection had been offered to her as a smaller life. Stay here, dress thus, answer promptly, do not walk alone. Each instruction had arrived bearing concern, and each had added a stone. He began to defend intentions, then stopped. The opening between them remained uncomfortable. She was grateful that he did not hurry to fill it.

In her inward Delhi, banners lowered and the ridge remained. She did not need to call any ruler the last of an entire people to feel the grief of a kingdom ending. Such titles made crowded histories too simple. Here, before this actual roughness, the poem could hold a more difficult defence: how to keep someone safe without making her the prisoner of the safety one had built.

Her companion offered the scarf. He had folded it so the pulled thread lay visible, neither concealed nor displayed as a wound requiring apology. She put it around her shoulders. They resumed their walk, leaving the thorn where it grew. At the gap he asked which way she wished to go. It was a small question, and she answered it with unexpected relief.

They passed through the opening into light. Behind them the wall continued to stand in its broken measure, neither useless nor complete. Sagorika felt the afternoon move against her neck where the cloth had tightened. Her companion kept pace without taking her arm. Somewhere within the woman who was Delhi, a guarded place admitted air, and she discovered how much strength was needed to leave it open.
