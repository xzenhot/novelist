# The Witness

The voice in the recording belonged to someone Sagorika would never hear again. She played it once in her room in Dwarka, then returned to its beginning before the final silence had quite arrived. Outside the window, traffic continued with the indifference that mourners often mistake for an insult. She had been called the witness, the woman who outlived the dynasties and remained young. Tonight the title seemed less a greatness than an occupation from which no one had thought to give her leave.

What becomes of the one who remembers when those remembered can no longer answer? The epic gave her stone enough to hold a city, fire enough to imagine its beginnings, a vanished river through which grief could speak. None of these images could reply to the little voice upon the table. She listened again. The words were ordinary, concerned with an errand, a meeting, something that had mattered within an hour now closed. Their ordinariness hurt more than any farewell might have hurt, because they had not known themselves to be approaching an ending.

A knock interrupted the recording. Her neighbour was trying to lift a container through the doorway and needed the outer door held open. Sagorika almost pretended not to hear. She had given the absent person the whole room, and another person's need felt like an intrusion upon that fragile tenancy. Then she stopped the voice and went to the door. The container caught upon the threshold; together they shifted it slightly and found the angle at which it could pass. The labour was brief, awkward, and entirely without solemnity.

Her neighbour thanked her and noticed her face. Neither asked for a story nor offered a ready consolation. They stood a moment in the passage, sharing the quiet left after the effort. Sagorika felt no sudden release from grief. Yet the hand with which she held the door had found a use outside the repeated circle of the recording. The living had not taken anything from the dead by asking her to be present. A witness might serve memory more faithfully by remaining capable of response than by sealing herself within the room where remembrance could never be interrupted.

When she returned, she let the recording play to its end. The silence afterward entered fully this time. This time she did not fill it by beginning again. Instead she opened the window and moved the chair nearer the evening air. Somewhere along the passage a container was set down, a door closed, water began to run. May the earth's long memory leave such sounds audible; may those who remember be permitted an hour in which they are simply among the living. Sagorika sat with the absence beside her rather than inside every corner of the room. Her young face darkened with the window, holding a grief that had not diminished and a world that had, by one small doorway, become possible to enter again.
