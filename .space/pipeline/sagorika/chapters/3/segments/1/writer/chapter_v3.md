# The Vedic Age

At the edge of the plain, where the fire altars once stood and the smoke of the first offerings rose into a sky that had no city in it, the Vedic age still smoulders under the neon of Dwarka. The people gathered around fire then, and they sang of the dawn and the waters and the fire that carries the word upward, and their voices were the first music this land ever heard. They did not build cities; they built a way of seeing, a way of naming the world, and that naming has never left the ground.

But the names they sang have faded, and this is the sorrow of every age: that the words which once held a people together become, in time, only words. The Saraswati ran through that age like a bright thread, and they praised her as the mother of rivers, the giver of speech and wisdom. They did not know that she would one day vanish into the sand, that the river they called eternal would become a memory, that the hymns they sang would one day be sung by no one, or sung by rote, without the fire that first gave them breath.

And yet the debt remains, and a debt is not cancelled by forgetting. The fire has become the neon, and the hymns have become the noise of traffic, and the people who once gathered around the altar now gather around their screens. But the earth still listens for the voice that first called her sacred, and the fire still waits for the offering that first gave it meaning, and the river, though vanished, still remembers the name by which she was first loved. A civilization that forgets its first prayers has not escaped them; it has only buried them, and the buried thing always returns, asking to be remembered. The city does not hear it. But the ground does, and the ground is patient, and the ground is still waiting for the prayer that began it all.
