# The Vedic Age

When the evening light failed in her room in Dwarka, Sagorika brought a lamp to the table. I had been asking about the Vedic hymns, about the first prayers which the epic placed among the beginnings of her memory. I wanted a name to carry away, some honoured syllable that would make my ignorance appear instructed. She struck a flame, sheltered it with her hand, and waited until the wick had caught. The room returned by degrees: cup, window, bread, the small unsteady brightness upon her lip.

What does a civilization owe to its prayers? she asked. I began to answer with remembrance, with fidelity, with the solemn words that grow readily in a mouth relieved of action. Before I had finished, a gust crossed the open window and bent the flame. She reached for the lamp; I reached to close the window. Between our two movements the light recovered. It was a trifling labour, yet it interrupted the ease with which I had been praising the care of people whom I had never known.

Then, in the inward country where her voices meet, she saw a gathering round an altar. The scene was a vision, not a recovered chronicle. Fire took the offered substance and made its brief ascent; those beside it turned their faces towards warmth and utterance. No poet stepped forward to demand that we remember his name. The hymn belonged, for that imagined moment, to breath passing between people, to the listening which allowed one voice to become more than a solitary claim upon the dark. Behind it the vanished river moved as a remembered brightness, carrying the sorrow that no invocation could guarantee its continuance.

I asked whether the city had betrayed that listening. She did not answer by condemning the street outside, where horns and footsteps mingled with the hum returning to the buildings. Noise could carry need as truly as silence could conceal indifference. A prayer might survive in a busy room where someone noticed hunger; it might perish beneath an eloquent account of sacred things. She broke the bread and placed half beside my cup. The gesture neither explained the hymns nor pretended to complete them. It gave our conversation something that could be received.

The light returned before we had eaten. Its sudden steadiness made the little lamp look frail, almost unnecessary. I moved to extinguish it, then paused long enough to see the smoke begin only when the flame had gone. How quickly a service disappears beneath the convenience that succeeds it. We cleared the table together, carrying the cups to the sink, leaving no ceremonial words to cover what our hands could do. Later I could not recall the name I had hoped to learn. I remembered her palm beside the wavering wick, and the interval in which another person's attention had kept the room from darkness. May the debt of a prayer be paid there, where a living breath still needs an answering breath.
