# The Lodhi Gardens

In Lodhi Gardens, a runner stopped so abruptly that Sagorika nearly struck his back. A small branch lay across the path, ordinary enough to step over, yet a bird stood beside it, unwilling to retreat. The runner waited, breath falling heavily from him. Sagorika had been thinking of the old graves and the living bodies that hurried past them, ready to accuse the morning of forgetting. Now she watched the halted man and wondered whether remembrance might sometimes begin without looking backward at all.

The bird moved under a bush. The runner bent to lift the branch, then hesitated when another flutter came from the leaves. He laid it beside the path instead of throwing it farther. People passed, adjusting their stride, some annoyed by the interruption, others scarcely noticing. Sagorika stood where her shadow would not darken the little refuge. The man checked the time on his wrist and winced at whatever measure he had broken. Then he turned the display away. His breathing gradually slowed to the pace of the morning around him.

He said he had been trying to finish before work. Sagorika nodded. She knew the city's appetite for every hour, its insistence that even rest prove useful by making the body more efficient. Behind the leaves, stone held another arrangement of time: names and ambitions laid down beneath the weather. Yet she would not make the dead rebuke his running. A living heart had the right to beat quickly for its own delight, just as a tired woman had the right to sit. What mattered was the moment when another creature's need entered the path.

A child approached and asked why they were waiting. The runner pointed toward the bush without stepping closer. The child crouched at a distance, and after a while the bird emerged, crossed the open patch, and disappeared into deeper growth. Nothing had been saved that they could confidently count. They had merely given the small passage time to happen. The runner resumed his route at a gentler pace. The child went back to her companion. Sagorika continued alone, carrying the absence of certainty without wishing to fill it with a grander ending.

Near an old wall she sat and listened to shoes striking the path. Each impact traveled briefly through the ground, then vanished. The earth beneath the garden was no royal servant; it supported the anonymous ankle as completely as the monumental dead. Sagorika touched the place where her own pulse showed at her wrist. There was no eternity in the beat, only its present obligation to continue. She rose when she was rested, making no ceremony of departure. On the path ahead, someone had moved the branch farther from the walking space while leaving it beside the bush. Care had continued after its witnesses went away. That was the morning's blessing: no crown recovered, no jogger shamed, only a narrow route made passable for bodies whose names the stones would never need to learn, and whose brief lives still altered how the living ground was used.
