# The Winter Fog

What remains of Delhi when Delhi cannot see itself? In the winter morning Sagorika stepped from her building into a whiteness that had taken away the opposite wall. A car sounded close and then seemed far away. Her own heels returned a small, sharp testimony from the pavement. She had intended to walk swiftly to the metro, carrying the confidence of a route repeated until it no longer required attention.

At the corner she found an old man standing beside the crossing. He asked whether the road was clear. Sagorika listened and could not honestly say. The fog withheld the answer from both of them. For a moment they stood in the discomfort of shared ignorance, each hoping the other possessed a certainty. Then she offered her arm and said they would wait for someone who could halt the traffic.

Waiting changed the street. A bicycle bell emerged before its rider, and a voice before the face that owned it. The city, deprived of its broad visible declarations, arrived in portions small enough to demand care. Sagorika thought of the monuments she had carried inwardly through centuries, their domes and towers so confident in daylight. None could guide the old man's next step through this hidden road.

A guard from a nearby entrance saw them and came forward. He raised his hand to the approaching vehicles, his dark sleeve becoming their temporary boundary. Sagorika led the man across. Halfway, he faltered where the paving changed. Her arm tightened; his weight answered. It was a little crisis, over almost before it could be named, but she felt the full claim of his body upon her attention.

On the other side he told her that he usually made this crossing alone. She heard the dignity inside the explanation and answered that she too had needed help that morning. This was true. Without the guard she might have pretended to see enough. The old man smiled, and they continued until a doorway took him in. He became a shape, then a voice thanking her, then nothing visible at all.

Sagorika reached the metro later than intended. In the carriage window her face appeared against a blank exterior, suspended as though memory alone held it upright. She resisted the easy thought that fog made all people equal; some had warm rooms, others long roads and thin clothes. What it had removed was the excuse of believing distance to be understanding. At the next stop she shifted her bag to make a seat for a woman whose hands were red with cold. Outside, the city would gradually return, building by building, until its outlines again persuaded everyone that it could be known at a glance. She wished to retain something of the crossing: the honest admission of not seeing, the raised sleeve, the necessary pressure of another arm. Let sight return without abolishing attention. Let the visible city inherit the tenderness briefly learned in its absence.
