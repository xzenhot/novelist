# The Plain for Grain

What fed the throne before the throne gave thanks for victory? At the gathering dusk Sagorika waited beside a woman making bread in a narrow room in Dwarka. Flour had whitened the woman's wrist and entered the little folds of her skin. Outside, the city lifted its illuminated towers. Inside, a round of dough changed under a moving hand, turning earth's long labour into something the evening could eat.

The woman's son had returned from school with a lesson about rulers. He recited their names while his mother worked, pleased by the order in which greatness seemed to arrive. Sagorika listened until he paused. Then she asked what the rulers had eaten. Bread, he answered, laughing at the simplicity of it. And before bread? He looked toward the flour, suddenly less certain of how far an ordinary question might extend.

She did not conduct him through an invented history. She spoke of the plain, of grain growing where soil and season allowed, of water received or withheld, of the field's dependence upon weather. In the book her body held, Delhi's dynasties had chosen the plain for grain. Yet choice was only the beginning. Seeds did not ripen in obedience to titles; hands had to labour, and the harvest could disappoint the proclamation.

The boy took a little dough and tried to shape it. His circle tore. He concealed the tear with more flour, making a pale, stiff island beneath his fingers. His mother laughed, then showed him how to press without forcing. Sagorika saw the lesson descend from the mind into the wrist. The bread would bear the manner of its making, just as a city bore the manner in which it obtained what fed it.

A neighbour knocked, asking whether there was a little extra. Her cooking fire had failed. The woman glanced at the prepared portions, counted silently, and divided one into two. Sagorika offered to fetch what was needed, but the woman waved her toward the stool. It would take longer than sharing. The boy watched his mother's arithmetic and then pushed his own unfinished round toward her, asking if it could be made usable.

It could, with patience. The bread came from the heat misshapen, sound, and fragrant. They sent it next door wrapped in cloth. Later, eating her portion, Sagorika found no grandeur in its flavour, only the dense presence of things easily forgotten: river soil, uncertain sky, repeated work, a hand willing to divide. She thought of palaces whose splendour had survived in stone while their daily dependence disappeared from praise. Let the plain have its place in every account of power, she thought. Let the meal interrupt the boast. The boy began his list of rulers again, but stopped to bring his mother water. It was a small correction, too small for an empire's chronicle. Sagorika received it as the evening's clearest word, and broke the remaining bread so everyone could finish together.
