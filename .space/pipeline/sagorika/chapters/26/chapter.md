# The Pre-Monsoon Summer

What wisdom is there in a season that makes the wise seek shade? By midday the street in Dwarka seemed to have lost its distance. Everything stood near and merciless: the wall, the glare, the heated railing beneath Sagorika's hand. She had dressed for an appointment in a room where the air would be kept obedient. At the entrance a delivery man lowered his parcel and sat abruptly upon the step.

For a moment the people passing treated his stillness as an obstruction. Sagorika herself was late. The thought crossed her mind cleanly, without disguise: someone else would attend to him. She recognized it as one recognizes a familiar cowardice, then turned. His eyes were open; he answered when she spoke, but his words seemed to have travelled a great distance. She brought water from inside and called for help.

They moved him into the entrance's shade. A guard fetched a chair. The parcel, abandoned in the sun, bore a cheerful promise of swift arrival. Sagorika pushed it under the bench with her foot. She could not command the summer to lessen; she could command this small refusal to let a promise about objects outweigh a body. Around them the heated air continued as though no moral event had taken place.

Within her the city's remembered summers gathered, mouths beneath banners, labour beneath stone, kings whose cups had hidden the thirst of those who filled them. She did not pretend that every burning season improved its sufferers. Heat could teach nothing to the dead. The lesson belonged to those with shelter, whose comfort had made the exposed appear distant. Thou must not praise endurance merely because another bears the cost of thy convenience, she told herself.

The man drank slowly. Colour returned to his voice before it returned to his face; he worried about the delivery. Sagorika went upstairs and told the waiting recipient why it was delayed. Her own appointment was already lost. She found herself strangely ashamed of the relief this brought, as though useful interruption had released her from a ceremony of importance. Yet the man had not faltered for the sake of her enlightenment. His need remained his own.

When someone arrived to accompany him, he insisted upon taking the parcel. The guard carried it instead. Sagorika watched them cross toward the shaded side of the road, a little procession too ordinary to enter any history of Delhi. Above them the towers held the light like sealed furnaces. She removed her heels before climbing the stairs again, carrying them by their narrow straps. Her feet had swollen, and she no longer wished to quarrel with that truth. In the room she opened the curtains enough to see the street. Let the city's cool chambers remember what lies beyond their doors, she thought. The summer would return with its ancient severity; perhaps the door could remain open to a need that no season had invented, and that no season excused.
