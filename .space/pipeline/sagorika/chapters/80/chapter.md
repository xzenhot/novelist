# The Okhla Barrage

At Okhla, Sagorika stood watching water pass through an order imposed upon it. Gates, edges, measured openings: the river entered a language of permission. She had come prepared to call it captivity, to speak as the earth against every hand that presumed to govern her. Then a man beside her lifted an empty bottle and said that his family waited each day for water to arrive. His words broke her grand sentence before it formed. What freedom could she praise that did not also make room for their thirst?

He was there with his daughter, who wanted to know where the water went after it disappeared from view. He gave an answer, stopped, and admitted he did not know the whole route. Sagorika admired the admission more than a confident tale. The child traced a path in the air with one finger, curving it around an invisible house. In her drawing the river could reach everyone because her hand encountered no wall. Below them the water moved according to forces and arrangements none of the three could comprehend by looking alone.

The bottle slipped from the man's hand and struck the ground. His daughter caught it before it rolled toward the edge. She returned it with the solemn pride of having prevented a loss. Sagorika saw, in that rescue, the scale at which responsibility first becomes possible: a thing within reach, a consequence understood, a hand moving in time. Beyond it lay decisions larger than a hand, decisions requiring knowledge, labor, and the willingness to answer those who lived downstream. She could not bless the gates merely because water was needed, or condemn every boundary because a river appeared more beautiful without one.

They watched a little longer. The child asked whether a river could grow tired. Sagorika said that people could exhaust what sustained them without hearing it complain. The father looked at her, then at the bottle. She regretted the weight of the answer and added that people could also learn to do otherwise. The child asked how. This time Sagorika did not reach for the vanished river, the ancient ridge, the volcanic fire beneath her imagined bones. She said they would have to follow where their water came from, where it went, and whose hands and lives were changed along the way.

When the pair left, the daughter carried the bottle. Evening lowered its light across the moving surface, refusing to settle into one reflection. Sagorika remained until her own face disappeared from the angle of the water. She was still the woman in red, still the city in her long dream, but neither beauty nor endurance would answer the question she had brought. Delhi would have to know the ground in ways that altered what it did. She turned toward home carrying no final pardon. At her door she paused before opening the tap, remembered the child's unfinished river drawn upon air, and began with the nearest reachable thing: the water she would use, and the attention she would owe after it had left her hands.
