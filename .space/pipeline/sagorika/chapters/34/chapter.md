# The Climate for Rhythm

Can a city command the season to keep its appointment? Sagorika found an old calendar folded behind a shelf in her Dwarka room. Upon it she had once marked the expected rain, the worst weeks of heat, a visit postponed until cooler weather. The squares looked like little rooms prepared for obedient guests. She spread the paper flat. Outside, a hot wind passed through the street, indifferent to the date.

She remembered the year contained in those marks through the things that had refused their arrangement. In summer she had carried water to a neighbour. When the rain came, she had helped lift bedding above a flooded threshold. In winter she had missed a train because fog slowed the road. The same city had required three different forms of patience, none of which could be practised by merely admiring the calendar.

A friend arrived while she was sorting the papers and asked why she kept such useless things. Sagorika almost answered with a defence of memory, but the word useless troubled her more profitably than an argument would have done. What use had she made of remembering? Had she prepared earlier, shared more readily, noticed who faced each returning hardship without shelter? Or had she merely accumulated evidence of having endured?

Together they listed what the building would need before the next rain. The friend knew a blocked outlet downstairs; Sagorika knew the neighbour whose doorway lay lowest. They went to look. Leaves and old rubbish filled the channel. Clearing it was unceremonious work, requiring a stick, a sack, and repeated bending. Their conversation shortened into directions. By the time they finished, the dust had marked their clothes more clearly than the calendar had marked their intentions.

Sagorika thought of the dynasties that had chosen this climate's alternation of scarcity and abundance. To choose a rhythm was not to own its beat. The summer could exceed anticipation; the rain could delay or overwhelm; the winter could obscure the very road toward shelter. Human wisdom lay partly in preparing, partly in admitting that preparation would remain incomplete. Pride made a promise no season had agreed to honour.

Back upstairs, she placed the calendar on her desk and wrote upon its empty margin what they had done. She left room beneath it for what remained. Her friend said it was strange to record work on an obsolete year. Sagorika answered that the year was finished, but the lesson had not yet earned its conclusion. Evening entered, bringing a slight relief they had neither caused nor deserved. She let it touch her face without making it a reward. May the city keep time with the needs of its people, she thought, and cease mistaking recurrence for assurance. The seasons would return by their own measures. Before they did, there were channels to clear, vessels to fill, and doors whose opening could be arranged by human hands while the weather still withheld its answer.
