# The Land That Outlasts

What shall I ask of a city whose lifetime is shorter than my waiting? At evening Sagorika stood upon the Ridge and watched Delhi prepare its lights. She had come carrying a small cloth bag, intending to gather what others had left along the path. The work had begun without ceremony: a wrapper, a broken cup, a length of string caught among roots. Now the bag pulled at her wrist.

Below her, the city seemed almost innocent at this distance. Its noise softened; its waste disappeared; windows opened like orderly constellations. She understood why poets loved the view from heights. Distance could turn suffering into pattern and call the result beauty. She would not relinquish beauty, but neither would she grant it the right to conceal what her hand had gathered.

Within her the old geography assembled: rock that had outlasted royal names, river soil laid down beyond any single life, hidden water, heat, rain, winter's uncertain veil. The dynasties moved through that inheritance in their brief colours. She had been desired beneath each changing banner and mistaken for the prize of its arrival. Even now, the red upon her mouth could persuade an admirer that her whole meaning waited upon his gaze.

A man climbing the path paused beside her and asked what she carried. Rubbish, she answered. He looked surprised, perhaps because the word did not belong to the woman his first glance had invented. Then he picked up a bottle from beside the stone where he stood and asked whether there was room. Sagorika opened the bag. Their exchange required less eloquence than any declaration she had prepared for the city.

They descended together as darkness collected beneath the trees. He spoke of how long the Ridge had endured. She listened, then said that endurance was not the same as immunity. The earth would outlast them, but a living place could be diminished within their lifetime, and the people deprived of it could not wait upon geological patience. Eternity offered no excuse for postponing care. The bag struck her knee at each step, keeping the argument bodily.

At the road they found a place to leave the collected waste properly. The man continued toward the traffic; Sagorika remained a moment with the emptied cloth in her hands. She had not cleansed Delhi. She had not solved the sorrow of being seen as surface while carrying depth. Yet the evening had yielded one companion in a modest work, and she would neither inflate nor despise that gift. Tomorrow the land would still be older than every promise made upon it. Tomorrow she would still possess only a human day's reach with which to answer. Let the city see me here, she thought, neither enthroned nor vanishing, my hands soiled, my mouth bright, my task unfinished. Then she folded the bag for another use and walked toward Dwarka, where the lights had become individual rooms again, each holding someone who would need the earth at dawn.
