# The India Gate Names

At India Gate, Sagorika watched a girl copy a name into a notebook while her family opened food upon a cloth nearby. The girl looked up, wrote, then looked again, troubled by a letter she could not distinguish. Sagorika had been preparing an accusation against the afternoon: how easily the living ate beneath the memory of the dead. But the girl's pencil interrupted her. Perhaps remembrance did not always announce itself by refusing pleasure. Perhaps it began with the stubborn wish to get one unfamiliar name right.

She stood beside the girl and followed the letters with her eyes. Neither of them spoke the name confidently. They tried its shape once, then left the uncertain sound between them. Sagorika would not give the girl a fabricated certainty. The stone carried more names than she could gather into one act of attention, and each belonged to a life beyond the carved letters. The girl asked what the person had liked to eat. Sagorika said she did not know. From the cloth, someone called them to come before the food grew cold.

The girl closed her notebook but kept one finger between the pages. She asked Sagorika to sit with them. A place was made at the edge, small enough that their knees almost touched. Bread passed from hand to hand. The girl described the name to her family, and an older woman repeated it slowly, offering a pronunciation without insisting upon it. Their conversation moved to the journey home, then returned briefly to the inscription. Memory traveled among ordinary concerns, losing nothing essential by sharing the afternoon with hunger. Sagorika ate, chastened by the meal she had nearly condemned from a distance.

A gust lifted an empty wrapper. The girl caught it and tucked it into a bag. Later, when the family began packing, she opened her notebook once more. Beneath the copied name she wrote a question about the life behind it. Her elder promised that they would try to learn more. Sagorika listened to the modest terms of that promise. No complete recovery had been offered, no claim that the dead could be repaid by one child's curiosity. Yet an unread mark had entered a household and acquired a future hour in which someone might attend to it.

When they left, the grass slowly released the shape of their cloth. Sagorika remained standing, her red mouth tasting the bread they had shared. The earth held soldiers and children, grief and appetite, without obliging one to abolish the other. That old generosity did not excuse neglect; it made neglect harder to defend. She returned to the name the girl had copied and studied its letters again. Then she wrote it for herself, including the uncertainty that remained. Delhi's evening gathered around the monument, full of footsteps and calls. Among them she carried one unfinished inquiry, lighter than stone and less secure, requiring a living hand to keep it from slipping unnoticed out of the day. Tomorrow she would open the notebook before allowing the city's haste to close it.
