# The Fatehpuri Steps

At the Fatehpuri steps, an old man spilled the grain he had brought for the pigeons. The paper cone loosened between his fingers, and the birds advanced before he could bend. Sagorika, crossing the afternoon with the impatience of someone expected elsewhere, stopped to help. He waved her away from the grain and asked her to retrieve the empty paper. It was drifting toward the road. The birds could manage their portion, he said; the street had no appetite for the wrapper.

She caught it beneath her heel and lifted it. A small crowd of wings rose at the movement, then settled again, each bird correcting its distance from the others. The man sat where the shadow had not yet reached his knees. Sagorika offered to move his bag into shade, but he preferred to wait for the shade to arrive. She could not tell whether this was wisdom or merely a habit made necessary by tired joints. She sat one step below him, refusing to turn his age into a lesson before he had spoken.

He asked the time. She told him, and he calculated aloud how long remained before someone would come to fetch him. He had not chosen a boundless afternoon; he was waiting through a particular interval, with an aching knee and the wish for familiar company. Across the street people hurried under burdens light and heavy. Sagorika remembered the ridge's immense patience, and how often she had bestowed it upon the poor, the old, the overlooked, as though endurance cost them nothing. The man's fingers were still dusted with grain. He rubbed them against each other slowly.

A child ran up the steps, scattering the pigeons into sudden brightness. The old man flinched, then laughed when one bird landed close enough to resume eating at once. The child apologized. He asked her to look inside his bag for the second cone. Together they distributed it more carefully, her hand steadying his wrist while his fingers decided how much to release. Sagorika watched the grain fall in a narrow arc. It seemed neither a miracle nor a symbol until she noticed how long she had ceased looking at her telephone.

When the person he awaited arrived, the old man rose with help and gathered his bag. He thanked Sagorika for the paper, the least conspicuous thing she had done. She remained a moment after they left. The step retained no visible shape of his waiting; another body would occupy it, and another afternoon would move its shadow across the same worn place. She folded the wrapper small enough to put away for disposal. Delhi pressed onward around her, but its movement no longer seemed to require her haste. She sent word that she would be late, then walked without trying to recover the lost minutes. They were not lost. They belonged to an old man's measured handful, a child's brief assistance, and the precise distance a shadow had traveled before someone came to take him home.
