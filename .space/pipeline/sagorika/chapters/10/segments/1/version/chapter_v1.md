# The Mughals

In the garden Sagorika paused beside a fallen flower while I was looking at the stone. I had come to admire the beauty the Mughal name gathered into my mind: gardens, water, the patient ordering of a view. I spoke of eternity as visitors do when a building has survived longer than their own small reckonings. She lifted the flower by its stem. Its petals were already loosening, though the colour had not yet learned to look diminished.

What would you ask beauty to survive? she said. I answered that I wished this place to remain. It seemed an innocent desire, and perhaps it was, until I heard the bargain hidden within it. I wanted preservation to protect me from the thought of ending. I wanted the garden to demonstrate that grace could negotiate with time on different terms from a body. Sagorika let one petal fall upon her palm. She did not answer with contempt for monuments; the labour that had made them deserved a more careful love than either worship or dismissal.

The Mughals passed through her remembering as the epic gave them: grandeur, gardens, the confidence of power, and the long surrender of that confidence. She imagined hands guiding water and hands fitting stone, the attention by which beauty exceeds the command that commissions it. The empire's passing did not make those hands foolish. Nor did the survival of a graceful wall vindicate every power sheltered behind it. Between gratitude and judgment there was room for an honest gaze, though I had tried to purchase the easier gaze which admires everything at once.

A child came running along the path and nearly struck my arm. I drew back sharply; the rebuke had already gathered in my mouth when I saw what she carried. Her own fallen flower had scattered, and she was trying to recover its pieces before the wind took them. Sagorika opened her palm. The child looked from one flower to the other, uncertain whether accepting another petal would mend the first. They knelt beside the path together. Not all the pieces could be found. The child gathered those remaining and rose with an incomplete brightness held carefully between both hands.

My anger had passed without finding a throne. Nothing required me to defend the garden by wounding someone who had entered its beauty differently. Sagorika returned to my side, and we continued beneath the changing light. The stone remained beautiful; it had become less useful as a promise that loss could be defeated, and more demanding as an invitation to care. At the gate the child showed her handful to the person waiting for her. May the city keep its gardens with that imperfect tenderness, neither denying what falls nor crushing what remains in the wish to possess it whole. We stepped into the street, where the evening had no enclosure, and carried away a beauty which asked to govern our hands before it governed our praise.
