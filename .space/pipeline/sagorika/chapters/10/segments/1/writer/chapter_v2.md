# The Mughals

I remember the Mughals, who built on this plain as if they were planting a garden that would bloom forever. They raised the Red Fort and the great mosques and the walled city of Shahjahanabad, and they filled them with water and marble and the sound of fountains, and for a time it seemed that beauty itself had taken a throne. They believed, as every dynasty believes, that their empire was the last word, that the world would never again be without them.

And I held their gardens the way I hold all gardens, and I loved them the way the earth loves whatever grows on it, without asking how long it will last. But I knew, as I have always known, that even the most beautiful thing is borrowed, that the marble would weather and the fountains would fall silent and the empire would pass, as every empire passes, into the memory of the ground. For this is the undefeated virtue of the earth: not that it resists what is built upon it, but that it endures, that it outlasts every beauty and every empire and every name, and that it does so without pride and without complaint, simply by being what it is.

The Mughals are gone now, and their beauty remains only in stone and in the stories the city tells itself. But the earth does not mourn them, because the earth knows that beauty is not lost when it fades; it is only returned. The garden returns to the soil, the marble returns to the mountain, and the civilization that believed itself eternal returns to the ground that was always its true home. And the ground, which has held every beautiful thing and let it go, is still here, still patient, still waiting for the next civilization to learn what the Mughals learned too late: that the only eternity is the ground itself. May the city build with beauty, and also with the knowledge that beauty is borrowed, and may it love its gardens without believing they are forever.
