# The Humayun's Tomb

At Humayun's Tomb, Sagorika's companion tried to place her exactly in the centre of his photograph. A step left, he said; now half a step back. Behind her the ordered beauty rose with such assurance that her own breathing seemed an irregularity. She had quarrelled with him on the way, and the picture was meant to rescue the day without requiring either to speak of it. She stood still, wishing it would work.

Something was wrong with her expression. She could have told him that symmetry had not reached her face, but the cleverness would only wound. Instead she stepped out of the arrangement and walked beside him. The tomb remained beautiful. Its beauty did not need to be called a lie because sorrow inhabited it. The difficulty was theirs: they had asked a monument to give two living people the composure they had refused to make through conversation.

They paused. Sagorika remembered the book's empires passing through the woman who was Delhi, each wearing splendour over the certainty of its ending. She resisted the easy contempt by which later ages reduced a ruler to a fall or a flaw. Death had already simplified enough. Here her imagination might consider the human wish to give loss a durable form, without pretending to recover a dead man's private soul from the proportions of stone.

Her companion asked what she wanted him to say. The question sounded weary, but it was at least addressed to her. She told him she wanted him to stop describing her anger as something that spoiled occasions. It had reasons. He looked away, then back. The silence between them lasted longer than the photograph would have taken. Around it the afternoon continued, and the ordered place did not collapse because their account of themselves remained untidy.

He repeated what he had heard, imperfectly. She corrected one word. He tried again. That small revision moved her more than a polished apology might have done. It had the grain of work in it. The ridge beneath her inward city had borne so many finished declarations; perhaps what she needed now was a sentence willing to remain unfinished until another voice had entered it. Her shoulders loosened, though she did not yet reach for his hand.

They walked on without seeking the centre of anything. Light altered the surfaces as they passed. From one angle the composition seemed complete; from another, people crossed it, a shadow interrupted it, distance concealed what had looked obvious. Sagorika felt no wish to choose between beauty and these disturbances. A living eye needed both, and the earth could bear the ordered garden without becoming orderly in every depth.

Near the exit he asked for another picture. She agreed, standing beside him this time, their arms visible at the edge of the frame. It was an awkward photograph. At home she kept it because she remembered what preceded it: not the erasure of a quarrel, but the first honest alteration in its shape. The stone had witnessed enough masks; they had removed one.
