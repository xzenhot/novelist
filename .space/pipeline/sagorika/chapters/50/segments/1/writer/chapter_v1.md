At Anangpur, where the scrub holds its breath against the Aravalli's oldest stone, Sagorika came with an empty bottle and a sentence about water being mastered. By noon the sentence had dried on her tongue, and only the bottle's emptiness remained, a small dry river she carried against her ribs. The dam stood in the heat the way a threshold stands: not a wall, not a door, but the long pause between wanting and having. Her companion turned his notes for an age, a founder, a name that would pin the work to a past. She felt her tongue against her teeth and said nothing, because thirst has no grammar.

A little water stayed in his bottle. He offered it, insisting he was not thirsty, and she knew the lie by its tenderness — the way a man gives away the last of himself and calls it nothing. They divided what remained. The old stone turned suddenly legible without yielding any date: here were bodies whose hours hung on water, here the wish to hold enough against a later need. The ridge could defend a settlement; it could not command thirst to stand outside its walls. Even victory, she understood, required someone to fill a vessel, and the filling was never written down.

In the poem opening inside her, rain gathered and a patient hand guided it — not a reconstruction but the shape of an enduring labour. Her imagined builder did not speak. He tested the course of a small stream with his fingers and shifted a stone when the water found another way. She had expected dominion. She found negotiation, a listening so practical it left no room to boast over what stayed beyond control. This was the eighth century, and also this morning: the Sahibi dead nine months, alive three, waiting under the concrete for a monsoon of feeling to make a witness of it again.

Her companion closed the notebook. He had written a phrase about the city before its name, and now crossed it out. A name was no measure of whether people had needed one another here. Nor did an old structure grow older through his admiration. They could leave the unanswered questions intact and still attend to the work before them. Sagorika touched the warm surface lightly, keeping her weight on the path. The stone gave heat, texture, resistance; she accepted those first, the way her father had taught that the body is the temple and the ache is the prayer.

On the return they met a woman carrying a bottle wrapped in cloth. She gave them directions toward water, exact where their meditations had been vague: follow the turn, keep to the road, ask at the next place. They thanked her and obeyed. Sagorika found relief in receiving knowledge that tired feet could test. The city's survival, she thought, had always included such unrecorded sentences, spoken at the scale of another person's need — the buried layer beneath every monument, the water under the stone.

They filled their bottles and drank slowly. Her companion washed dust from his face, using less than he would have used that morning. The restraint was small and would need remembering tomorrow. She refused to enlarge it into redemption. Water ran between his fingers and vanished into the ground, leaving a darker patch the afternoon began at once to claim — the dry river returning to its hiding.

Back in Dwarka, she filled a jug before sitting to write. The dam entered her page as a question about keeping and sharing. Outside, a tap somewhere kept dripping, and she rose, followed the sound, and found the loose fitting. The unfinished poem waited while she asked who could repair it — and the tap answered only with its patient, unclosed sound, one drop at a time, like a city learning to be thirsty without being cruel.
