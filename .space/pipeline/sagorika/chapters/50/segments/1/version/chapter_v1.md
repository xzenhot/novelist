# The Anangpur Dam

Sagorika came to Anangpur with an empty bottle and a story about water being mastered. By noon the bottle's emptiness mattered more than the story. She and her companion stood among scrub and stone, looking at the dam through heat that made every distance tremble. He searched his notes for an age, a founder, a sentence that would place the work securely in a past. She felt her tongue against her teeth.

A little water remained in his bottle. He offered it, insisting he was not thirsty. She knew the lie by its tenderness. They divided what remained. The act made the old stone suddenly intelligible without supplying any missing history: here were bodies whose hours depended upon water, here the wish to hold enough against a later need. The ridge could defend a settlement; it could not command thirst to stand outside its walls. Even victory required someone to fill a vessel.

In the poem unfolding within her, she saw rain gather and a patient hand guide it, not as a dated reconstruction but as the shape of an enduring labour. Her imagined builder did not speak. He tested the course of a small stream with his fingers and altered a stone when the water found another way. Sagorika had expected dominion. She found negotiation, a listening so practical that it left no room for boasting over what remained beyond control.

Her companion closed the notebook. He had been writing a phrase about the city before its name, and now crossed it out. A name was no measure of whether people had needed one another here. Nor did an old structure become older through his admiration. They could leave the unanswered questions intact and still attend to the work before them. Sagorika touched the warm surface lightly, keeping her weight upon the path. The stone offered heat, texture, resistance; she accepted those things first.

On their return they met a woman carrying a bottle wrapped in cloth. She gave them directions towards water. Her instructions were exact where their meditations had been vague: follow the turn, keep to the road, ask at the next place. They thanked her and obeyed. Sagorika found relief in receiving knowledge that could be tested by tired feet. The city's survival, she thought, had always included such unrecorded sentences, spoken at the scale of another person's need.

They filled their bottles and drank slowly. Her companion washed dust from his face, using less than he would have used that morning. The restraint was small and would require remembering tomorrow. She refused to enlarge it into redemption. Water ran between his fingers and disappeared into the ground, leaving a darker patch that the afternoon began at once to claim.

Back in Dwarka, she filled a jug before sitting down to write. The dam entered her page as a question about keeping and sharing. Outside, a tap somewhere continued to drip. She rose, followed the sound, and found the loose fitting. The unfinished poem waited while she asked who could repair it.
