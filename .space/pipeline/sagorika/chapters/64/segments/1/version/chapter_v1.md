# The Ghalib's Haveli

At Ghalib's haveli, Sagorika found a hungry boy reading the paper wrapped around his bread. He held the bread in one hand and turned the wrapper with the other, pursuing a line that disappeared into a stain. She had arrived prepared to honor the endurance of words. Now the boy's divided attention unsettled her ceremony. Which survived here, she wondered: the poem that crossed generations, or the appetite that crossed each day without assurance of the next? From the lane came the sharp exchange of a price disputed, and the boy folded his paper away.

She asked what he had been reading. He showed her a fragment, too damaged to establish its beginning or its end. She did not supply a famous name. The city had enough voices taken from their owners and dressed in borrowed grandeur. They tried instead to make sense of the surviving sentence. A word near the margin might have meant returning; it might have belonged to something more ordinary. The boy shrugged and bit into his bread. Sagorika felt the old embarrassment of interpretation before hunger, which does not wait politely for a difficult passage to become clear.

A gust entered the lane and lifted the wrapper from his knee. He reached too late. It slid beneath a shutter, where the dirt received it without reverence. For a moment he looked genuinely bereaved, and she understood that he had wanted both things: the food, and the words no one had sold him. She crouched, her heels awkward beneath her, and drew the paper out with the edge of an empty envelope. The rescued sheet carried a dark smear across its sentence. The boy took it carefully, avoiding the dirt with the hand that held the bread.

They sat near the wall while he finished eating. Sagorika offered the clean envelope, and he copied the words he could still read. Where the stain had obliterated the rest, he left a space. The blank pleased her more than any clever restoration. It admitted that something had been lost, while preserving the possibility that someone else might know it. Beneath them the ground held its older scripts, quartzite and dust, the city's successive occupations of a hunger it could never abolish by building higher. Yet the boy's pencil moved, and the small sounds were sufficient for that afternoon.

When he had finished, he asked whether the incomplete sentence was worth keeping. Sagorika answered by holding the envelope while he put it safely in his pocket. She had no blessing that could purchase his next meal, no right to turn his want into proof that poetry required suffering. Before leaving she bought another portion of bread from the lane and asked whether he would share it. He nodded. They ate without discussing immortality. The haveli remained behind them, gathering visitors and shadows. What she remembered afterward was the clean space he had left between the known words, and how steadily his hand had written once it no longer had to choose between reading and eating.
