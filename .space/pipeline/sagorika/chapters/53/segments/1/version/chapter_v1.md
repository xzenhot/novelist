# The Tomar Forts

Her companion wanted a founder's name; Sagorika wanted to know where to put her foot. Among the stones associated in their itinerary with the Tomar forts, a loose edge shifted beneath her shoe. She caught his arm. The notebook fell, opening upon a page crowded with beginnings: first wall, first ruler, first claim. For a moment the only beginning that mattered was the next secure step, and he found it before retrieving his account of the past.

He brushed the page and complained that the names were difficult to keep in order. Sagorika watched a grain of soil lodge in the notebook's fold. The earth had entered his chronology without permission. It did not refute the names, but it changed their setting. Before a wall acquired a ruler, stone had possessed weight; before a founder received a sentence, someone had carried that weight to the place another person wanted it.

She imagined such a carrier without claiming to restore a lost biography. In her poem he paused, shifted his burden, and called to the person behind him to wait. That was all she allowed herself to see. No invented battle furnished his silence, no borrowed date dressed him in certainty. The unrecorded labour remained partly unrecorded, resisting the poet who might otherwise turn absence into an invitation to possess. Sagorika held the little imagined pause with more care than a throne.

Her companion asked whether she distrusted every story. She answered that she wished to know which stories they were telling and whose missing voices made them seem complete. He looked again at the page. After the names he added a question about hands. It was not a replacement for history; it was a place kept open inside his attention. The wind lifted the paper, and he held it down with his palm.

They rose to continue. This time he walked beside her instead of ahead, testing the uncertain ground together. From somewhere beyond the stones came the ordinary noise of a present day carrying on. Sagorika felt Delhi's many masks turn within her: the founded city, the conquered city, the city photographed by visitors who wished to take an origin home. Beneath them the ridge's old bone kept its roughness. It had never promised an entrance free of difficulty.

At a narrow place they met another pair coming towards them. There was not room for all four. Sagorika stepped back to the wider ground and waited. The others passed with a word of thanks, and the moment vanished so quickly that her companion did not record it. Yet she felt its relation to the wall more sharply than any claim of first possession: a space becomes habitable through decisions made by people whose names it will not keep.

On the way home he asked to photograph her dusty shoes. She agreed, amused. Beside the picture he wrote no royal caption. He wrote that they had nearly fallen, had helped each other, and had found a way through. The account began modestly enough to be true.
