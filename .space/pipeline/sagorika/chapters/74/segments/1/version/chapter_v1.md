# The Connaught Place

In Connaught Place, Sagorika passed the same bright window twice before admitting she had been walking in a circle. Within it a garment hung without a body, arranged to imply a life whose every hour had already been forgiven. She saw her own reflection laid across it: red lips, tired eyes, the elegant heel that had begun to hurt. A young woman stood nearby comparing the displayed price with the balance on her telephone. Neither wished to be the first to move away. What did the city sell when even departure felt like defeat?

The young woman laughed suddenly and darkened the screen. She asked Sagorika whether she knew the way to a particular exit. Sagorika pointed, then confessed that she had already mistaken her own route. They decided to walk together until one of them recognized it. The curved succession of shopfronts returned their images repeatedly, women multiplied by glass while remaining inconveniently singular in their feet. Above the polished surfaces, afternoon light softened the white architecture. Beneath it, the earth bore the shops without sharing their confidence that everything necessary could be displayed.

At the next turn the young woman's sandal strap broke. She stopped with one foot half lifted, caught between laughter and anger. Sagorika offered an arm. They reached a place where she could sit, and the woman examined the damage as though it were a personal betrayal. The desired garment disappeared from their conversation. Now they needed something that could bind two small pieces together. Sagorika searched her bag and found thread wrapped around a card, left from a repair at home. The young woman accepted it with the gravity appropriate to a gift precisely matched to need.

The knot would not hold at first. They tried again, pulling the thread through a narrow opening, then winding it several times around the strap. It made an inelegant repair. When the woman stood, however, the sandal stayed with her foot. She tested three steps, then three more. Sagorika felt a pleasure no window had anticipated. The ancient ridge had entered her imagination often as endurance; here endurance looked like a clumsy knot, dependent upon attention and likely to need doing again. The woman thanked her and offered to buy tea. They found a place within what both could afford.

They parted after asking for directions from someone who knew. Sagorika passed the bright window once more on her way out. The garment remained desirable. She did not need to pretend otherwise in order to leave it there. In the glass she noticed the dust on her shoes, then her face, which had changed during the hour without becoming younger. The city could reflect her indefinitely; it could not decide what the reflection owed. She followed the indicated road, carrying the thread's empty card. Somewhere behind her a repaired sandal made its provisional agreement with the ground. The circle opened through that ordinary movement, and Sagorika went home with enough money left for tomorrow, neither triumphant nor ashamed, simply able at last to recognize her own departure.
