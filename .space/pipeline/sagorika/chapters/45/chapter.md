# The Badarpur Quarries

The new wall in Sagorika's apartment had the colour of a mountain after dust. A worker was smoothing it when she returned, and in the hollow room each scrape seemed larger than the hand that made it. Badarpur, someone had written upon a sack. She touched the letters and thought of the quarries, of a ridge diminished so that a room might stand upright. The thought was not a ledger of this particular wall; it was the question the powder brought into her house.

She had asked for a smooth surface. Upon it she meant to hang a picture in which the city appeared luminous, free of scaffolds, waste, and men waiting to be paid. Now the worker coughed, turned aside, and continued. She offered water. He drank quickly, as though rest were another material he must not use too freely. Sagorika looked at the patch already finished and saw how easily labour disappeared beneath the appearance of completion.

In her inward country the earth's oldest bone entered the room by handfuls. No trumpet accompanied it. It arrived in sacks, on shoulders, under fingernails; the mountain that kings had used for defence now bore the smaller ambition of a private life. She wanted shelter, a place to lay her head beside a beloved head. That desire was innocent only until she mistook its walls for things without an outside. Beyond every smoothness somebody stood in dust.

The worker asked where she wanted the last uneven place corrected. She began to point, then noticed that his light was failing. The day had passed while she considered the meaning of stone. He had to travel home. She told him tomorrow would do, settled what was owed, and helped clear the passage. One sack split as they lifted it. Pale powder ran across her shoes, hiding their red straps and the shine she had taken care to preserve.

He apologized. She heard herself say that it was nothing, then changed the words: it can be cleaned. Nothing was what the city called whatever it did not wish to count. Together they gathered what could be gathered, carefully, without pretending this small attention could repair every wound beyond the room. When he left, his footprints and hers crossed the threshold in the same colour.

She unwrapped the picture that evening. The luminous city looked suddenly unfinished in its perfection. Instead of hanging it immediately, she set it on the floor and opened the window. Air moved through the room; the unpainted wall darkened where a little water had touched it. Its beauty would come, but she no longer wished beauty to be an alibi.

The next morning she asked the worker his preferred time before asking when the surface would be ready. He answered with surprise, then with an ordinary hour. Sagorika wrote it down. Beneath the future picture, she left the wall bare until the work was done. The mountain in her waited there without grandeur, requiring that a home be built also in the terms of its making.
