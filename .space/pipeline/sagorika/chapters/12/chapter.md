# The Woman Who Is Delhi

At the crossing in Dwarka, someone called my name, and for a moment I could not tell which of my lives had answered. The young woman turned towards the voice; the earth beneath the crossing remained still; the city sent its traffic between us. I had been asked that evening whether I was truly Delhi. The question followed me now, bright as a shop window. How easily a whole place can be placed upon a woman's shoulders, then praised for the grace with which she bears it.

I waited for the signal. Within me the Aravalli kept its old silence, the Vedic flame lifted through remembered speech, and the Saraswati passed as an absence that had never ceased to trouble the poem. Indraprastha belonged to the dream of a founding; Prithviraj to the hour when command became remembrance. The Das rulers, the Khiljis, the Tughlaqs, the Mughals moved through my thought without forming a single procession obedient to my voice. I carried their names, but carrying was not mastery. The ground's long memory did not entitle me to speak every lost person's grief.

Across the road a man lifted his hand. He had mistaken me for someone he expected. When I came nearer, he saw his error and lowered it, smiling with embarrassment. We might have passed with nothing more between us. But he was holding two bags and trying to keep a folded paper from escaping into the traffic. The paper slipped. I caught its edge against my skirt before it reached the road, and handed it back. He thanked me by the wrong name once again, then laughed and asked the right one.

Sagorika, I said. The name was sufficient for the small encounter. I did not explain that mountains moved behind it, that the epic had made me earth, fire, witness, beloved. The crease across the paper retained a little of its accident. He shifted his bags, secured the paper, and went towards the person he had come to meet. I stood at the edge of the pavement with a curious lightness. For an instant I had been useful without being eternal. No dynasty had required my judgment; a loose page had needed my hand. The city I was asked to embody had met me through one of its unfinished errands.

I walked home with the question altered. If I am Delhi, let the likeness remain a door through which attention enters, not a monument which seals a living woman inside its meaning. Let me keep my hunger, my tiredness, my wish to be called correctly, even while the oldest stone speaks through my sleep. At my building I stopped to remove the grit from a shoe. The pavement was warm beneath my foot. May the city recognize its earth without asking her to become incapable of pain; may it learn to receive a woman's offered hand without claiming the whole of her. Above me, among innumerable windows, my own dark room waited for its little light.
