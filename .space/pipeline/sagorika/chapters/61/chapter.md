# The Khirki Masjid

At Khirki, the afternoon entered in pale squares while Sagorika stood beyond the reach of any moving air. She had come seeking coolness, carrying the day's heat beneath her red mouth as a coal is carried beneath ash. Beside her a boy folded a sheet of paper into a fan. Its little blows disturbed his hair and nothing else. From the street came a horn, then another; within, each sound seemed obliged to kneel before it could proceed. What sanctuary, she wondered, asks the living chest to grow as patient as its walls?

The boy offered her the fan. She refused at first, ashamed of needing what his small wrist had made, but he continued holding it toward her until refusal became another burden laid upon him. She took it and moved it twice. A crease opened across a word written in blue. It was part of his schoolwork, a sentence about rain. Outside, no cloud troubled the blazing roof of Delhi. Sagorika remembered the ridge's thorny shade, where even silence had room for a bird to pass through it, and the vanished river whose absence could enter any enclosure.

She asked whether he wanted the paper back. He said he would have to copy the sentence again before morning. Then his mother called from the doorway, and he ran toward her without retrieving it. Sagorika followed, the borrowed wind growing feebler in her hand. At the threshold the mother was searching her bag for a bottle. Its last water struck the bottom with a sound so slight that Sagorika heard, beneath it, the weight of every stone which had ever risen in the name of shelter. The boy drank. His mother watched his throat before allowing herself one mouthful.

There was room beside the doorway for all three to stand without obstructing anyone. Here a current of air found them, hardly stronger than the fan, yet unpurchased and uncommanded. Sagorika returned the paper and asked the boy to show her the sentence before he folded it again. Rain gives water to the earth. The words were ordinary; she had outlived too many magnificent utterances to despise their plainness. He smoothed the creases against his mother's palm. For a moment the hand, the paper, and the stone behind them made one small chamber open to the world.

When they left, Sagorika remained where their bodies had interrupted the light. She did not accuse the building of the city's heat, nor pretend that a doorway could undo a summer. She merely stepped aside whenever someone approached, leaving the narrow current free. Prayer had changed its place within her. It no longer rose toward the highest dark, asking to become imperishable. It stayed at the level of a child's mouth, where breath entered and departed, where water must arrive again tomorrow. Her heels sounded softly when she finally crossed into the street. The paper fan went ahead of her, opening, closing, carrying its unfinished lesson home through the evening.
