# The Groundwater

What becomes of a city when its hidden answer ceases? Before sunrise the pump in the courtyard of Dwarka gave a dry, uneven cough. Sagorika stood beside a row of vessels whose open mouths had been arranged in hope. Yesterday the water had come readily enough for impatience to seem reasonable. This morning each metallic shudder brought the waiting neighbours closer, as though their bodies could persuade the depth by gathering above it.

A man struck the pipe with his palm. Another told him to strike harder. The oldest woman in the queue asked that they stop and listen. In the interval between their voices there was a thin sound, water arriving unwillingly through the throat of the machine. It fell into the first vessel in broken threads. Sagorika watched the woman move her bucket aside before it was full, making room for the vessel behind it.

Beneath these walls, she thought, the earth possessed recesses the street did not acknowledge. The city pictured supply as a straight line from desire to possession. Yet the water had waited in darkness, passed through places no nameplate recorded, and reached this courtyard by labours the bright taps concealed. Within her imagination the vanished Saraswati stirred, not as a surveyed channel under their feet, but as the grief by which an absent river entered a living thirst.

A little girl asked whether the lost river could be called back by its name. Sagorika had no miracle to place in the child's vessel. A name might keep absence from becoming indifference, she said, but the mouth that spoke it would still have to learn restraint. The girl looked at the buckets, considering this difficult division between remembrance and water. Then she put her small cup beneath a leak that had been dripping upon the paving.

Its few spoonfuls seemed a mockery beside the need. Sagorika nearly smiled, and checked herself. The child's act was neither a solution nor a performance; it was the available answer of a hand. She found a rag and bound the loose joint while a neighbour fetched something stronger. The pump continued its troubled speech. Enough arrived for drinking and cooking, less than anyone had intended to carry away. The queue, altered by necessity, learned to measure without shouting.

When the vessels were taken upstairs, the courtyard looked ordinary again. Damp circles marked where each had waited. Sagorika remained beside the pipe until the machine was still. Her own bucket stood half filled, reflecting a small square of sky. She did not see an ancient river in that reflection. She saw a quantity that could be exhausted, a morning that had taught the cost of pretending otherwise. Carrying it carefully, she climbed to her room. At the threshold she turned back to close the tap fully, and the last hanging drop remained suspended, bright with a world too large for it to contain. Behind her, the child carried her cup with both hands.
