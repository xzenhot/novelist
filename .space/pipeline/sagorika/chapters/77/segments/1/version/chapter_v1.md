# The Yamuna at Wazirabad

At Wazirabad, Sagorika saw a child point toward pale foam and call it flowers. The mother drew him back from the edge, correcting him sharply, then softened her voice when his face changed. Sagorika stood beside them with an offering wrapped in paper. She had intended a small act of reverence, something beautiful released into the river, and now the packet weighed strangely in her hand. What was she asking the water to receive: gratitude, or another burden made acceptable by the tenderness of its name?

The child watched the pale shapes gather and separate. They seemed purposeful until the current undid them. Sagorika did not claim to know what substances composed the foam or which unseen place had sent them there. It was enough, for this moment, that the mother would not let the child touch it. The river was present, moving, unlike the vanished Saraswati that passed through Sagorika's inward speech; yet presence alone could not assure welcome. A living vein could still be injured. She felt the packet's damp stem press against her palm.

A gust tore its outer wrapping. One corner fluttered loose, and Sagorika caught it against her dress. The child laughed at her sudden awkwardness. She laughed too, grateful that he had restored her from an emblem to a woman struggling with paper. Then she folded the offering closed. The mother looked at her but did not ask. They stood together while a dark fragment traveled through the pale surface and emerged farther away, continuing beyond the reach of any of their hands. The river accepted no distinction between what had been dropped carelessly and what had been placed with solemn words.

Sagorika put the packet into her bag. This was no cure, only the stopping of one gesture. She had lived long enough in imagination to distrust the relief of believing a small renunciation had balanced a city's account. Still, the act changed the direction of her attention. Instead of watching the offering depart, she watched what was already there. She made a note of the place and of what she had seen, separating the visible foam from every explanation she could not establish. Reverence began to look less like release and more like the obligation to return with better knowledge.

On the journey home, the flowers darkened within their paper. She would place them in earth instead of asking the water to carry them. Beyond the window Delhi passed in surfaces of dust, glass, and sudden green, each drawing upon a hidden thirst. Sagorika touched her red lips and found their brightness insufficient to describe the day. The child's mistaken flowers remained before her, beautiful for the instant before his mother's warning. She could not restore that innocent seeing, and would not mourn its loss more deeply than the threatened river itself. At home she opened her notebook before unwrapping the flowers. The page held an observation, a place, and room for the questions her first act of devotion had almost allowed her to avoid.
