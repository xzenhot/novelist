# The Hauz Khas Tank

At Hauz Khas, Sagorika sat beside a window from which the tank appeared to belong to the table. A glass caught the light, doubling the water in miniature. Her companion turned his chair until the view stood behind her, then raised his telephone. She smiled before she had decided whether she wished to. In the image her red mouth, the glass, and the distant surface joined into one purchase. What part of a place became ours merely because we had paid to look at it while being served?

A server reached between them to remove an empty plate. Her companion's elbow struck the glass, sending water across the table and into Sagorika's lap. He laughed in embarrassment, then called for help. The server returned with a cloth. Sagorika rose and lifted the papers from the spill, while the man continued apologizing to her alone. She asked him to pass the dry napkins to the person doing the cleaning. The request was quiet; it made him look, at last, beyond the inconvenience to their afternoon. He gathered the napkins and held the plate himself.

Afterward they sat without attempting another photograph. Sagorika watched a small bead of water travel along the table's edge and fall. The tank outside could not be reduced to an ornament for their leisure, yet leisure itself was not a trespass she needed to condemn. The question was what vanished from sight when pleasure arranged the world: the carrying, the cleaning, the thirsty ground beyond the pleasing frame. The old earth beneath Delhi had furnished generations with water and received their reflections in return. Reflection was the lighter offering, endlessly renewed at almost no cost to the giver.

Her companion asked whether she was angry. She said she disliked how quickly the server had become invisible after the accident was contained. He listened, then looked toward the doorway through which the person had gone. When the bill came, he thanked them directly, without making an exhibition of his correction. Sagorika let the moment remain small. No change of empire had occurred at their table; two people had interrupted a habit and might still resume it tomorrow. She finished the water left in her glass instead of asking for another merely to replace what had spilled.

They walked outside before leaving. From this angle the tank was no longer a backdrop arranged behind her face. It occupied its own space, indifferent to whether she could make an attractive image beside it. Sagorika put away her telephone and stood with both hands free. She did not declare the water full or empty on behalf of every season; she looked at what this particular afternoon allowed her to see. Her garment was still damp. That discomfort kept the scene from becoming too easily beautiful. When they turned homeward, she carried the view without owning it, and remembered the cloth passing over the table: a brief circular movement through which another person's labor had restored their pleasure, asking to be seen as clearly as the light.
