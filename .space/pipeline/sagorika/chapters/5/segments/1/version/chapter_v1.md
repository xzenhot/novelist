# Indraprastha

Upon a sheet of paper in Dwarka, I drew a city with no inconvenient trees. Its roads were straight, its towers luminous, its river a blue line kept obediently within the margin. Sagorika watched while I gave the drawing a name. Indraprastha, I said, borrowing the splendour of a story for my little arrangement of desires. She placed a fallen leaf across the place where I had drawn the palace. Where shall this belong, she asked, when your vision has become a wall?

The leaf spoiled the symmetry. One curled edge caught the light and trembled while I considered. I moved it towards the edge, and she followed my hand with her eyes. There was no accusation in the gesture, only the patience which makes an easy answer difficult. I began to explain that every building requires a clearing, that every future must displace something. She agreed that we must live somewhere. Then she asked why I had found room for the image of a fountain before finding room for a living root. The paper, which had seemed so generous, revealed the smallness of the welcome I had prepared.

In her telling, Indraprastha rose within the mythic country of the epic. The Pandavas entered that country carrying the difficult word dharma; the city took shape, and beside its brightness stood the question of what the ground had surrendered. Sagorika did not turn the tale into a dated account of stones beneath our street. She let it remain a story capable of judging the hand that borrowed it. A noble name could be spoken over a careless act, just as a palace could cast its beautiful shadow across an absence no visitor was asked to see.

I looked again at the leaf. Its stem had made a faint damp trace upon the paper. The mark crossed one of my roads and ended inside a room. No clever rearrangement could make the imagined city harmless, yet the impossibility of innocence did not absolve me from choosing. I could let the drawing confess its needs. I could begin with water, shelter, passage, and space for what had already rooted itself. Necessity would still accompany the work, but it need not be used as a seal placed over every avoidable wound.

We turned the sheet over. Sagorika held one corner against the air from the window while I drew again. This time the road bent. The palace became a cluster of rooms, and the leaf remained where she had laid it, its narrowing veins dark against the page. Our drawing housed no one that night; I knew how easily a changed picture could be mistaken for a changed world. Yet before the work of building came the willingness to see what the picture had excluded. I left the new city unfinished upon the table. May the hand that names a future endure its necessary hesitation, and may the earth enter the plan before she is asked to bear the weight of our completed dream.
