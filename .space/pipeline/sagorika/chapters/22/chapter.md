# The Quartzite and the Schist

What name shall be given to the bones a city borrows? Sagorika found two fragments upon the Ridge after a morning wind. One lay dull and stubborn beneath her thumb; the other caught the light along its broken surface. Quartzite and schist, she murmured, letting the names remain names rather than turning them at once into a sermon. In her bag a little mirror lay beside her lipstick. She placed the stones against the glass, and her face appeared between their edges.

A boy watching her asked whether they were jewels. He had seen the second fragment glitter. She handed it to him, and the brightness departed when he turned his wrist. His disappointment was swift and honest. A moment earlier the stone had promised wealth; now it offered merely itself. He rubbed it upon his sleeve as if dullness were dirt. Sagorika stopped his hand, not sternly, and showed him how the light returned when his palm inclined.

The stone had not altered its character to reward him. His relation to it had changed. She considered how often Delhi demanded that the ground prove its worth by becoming something else: a wall, a floor, a height upon which a richer height might stand. The bones were praised only after the body had been broken into useful pieces. Even her own body was summoned into bright arrangements by eyes that would not trouble themselves with what sustained it.

They sat beside the outcrop. She did not tell the boy that every glitter held a secret, nor that every ancient thing was wise. The Ridge needed no false mystery to deserve its place. Its hardness, its fractures, its patient weathering were sufficient. What could be handled could also resist the hand; what could be named need not therefore belong to the namer. The boy set his fragment upright and balanced the duller one against it, making a tiny gate through which an ant disappeared.

His gate fell. He began again. This time he chose a hollow in the earth, and the stones held. Sagorika watched the difference between command and attention enter his fingers. The imagined kings of her inward city had demanded arches of the world. Here was a builder learning that the ground must be considered before the entrance, that stability was an agreement rather than a boast. No trumpet marked his success. The ant returned by another path.

When his mother called from the track, he asked to take the bright stone home. Sagorika said the choice was his, and waited. He lifted it, saw his gate collapse, and replaced it carefully. The little structure would not survive the season; it had nevertheless changed the manner of his leaving. She put away her mirror. On the journey back to Dwarka, the city flashed its windows at her, each demanding a brief allegiance. She remembered a hand turning until it found the light, then opening to leave the stone where it could continue being ground.
