# The Adham Khan's Dome

Under Adham Khan's dome, Sagorika heard a man offer his companion the old bargain: here was a wicked life, here its punishment, and here the stone that made the account complete. His words rose confidently and returned altered by the hollow above them. She could not tell whether the echo approved. On the ground a small pebble pressed through the sole of her shoe. She shifted her weight, and the man mistook her movement for attention. He repeated his conclusion more loudly, as though justice were a reluctant guest that volume might persuade to enter.

She had known the confidence of rulers, the clean division they made between a hand that struck unlawfully and a hand entitled to strike. Beneath their names the ridge kept its broken surfaces. Beneath their sentences the earth received what neither victor nor condemned could carry away. Yet she would not turn that long receiving into innocence. Stone endures without deciding; a woman must decide, and sometimes must live after discovering that the decision was wrong. She bent to loosen the buckle of her shoe. The man stopped speaking, inconvenienced by an action which furnished him no audience.

Near the entrance, his companion was rubbing her own wrist. A red line showed where the heavy handle of her bag had rested. Sagorika pointed toward a patch of shade. The woman sat there, set down the bag, and exhaled with such relief that the dome seemed suddenly less spacious than this one released breath. The man called for her to hurry. She answered that she would come when she had rested. Nothing spectacular followed. No throne cracked; no ancestral verdict fell in dust. Only her hand remained open on her knee instead of closing again around the handle.

Sagorika sat beside her. They spoke of the heat, of the distance still to walk, of how a strap could cut without breaking the skin. These matters would never be inscribed beneath a monumental name. Nevertheless, the woman moved her bag between her feet and divided its contents into two bundles. When the man returned, she handed him one. His expression tightened; then he accepted it. Sagorika watched them leave beneath the great shadow, their loads imperfectly balanced, their conversation too low to reach her. The echo had nothing useful to add.

She put on her shoe and stood. The pebble was gone, leaving a small tenderness in the foot that would remember it after the stone itself had been forgotten. Perhaps this was why she distrusted an account declared complete merely because someone lay beneath a dome. Hurt continued in the living, seeking neither architecture nor eloquence but an answer measured to its shape. She would carry no invented verdict from this place. She carried the sight of a hand refusing its accustomed burden, and another hand learning its weight. Beyond the shade, Delhi burned with the day's unfinished demands. Sagorika entered that brightness slowly, unwilling to let the grandeur behind her shorten the work ahead.
