# The Quartzite Vein

The mason held the stone to the light and asked Sagorika whether the pale seam should face inward. They were repairing a broken doorstep in Dwarka, where yesterday an old woman had caught her foot and fallen. Beauty had seemed a simple matter until he asked. Sagorika bent to see the quartzite, the patient brightness running through its weathered face, and felt the ridge answer somewhere beneath the clatter of buckets. Turn it outward, she said; then stopped him before his hammer rose.

She had almost chosen it as one chooses a bracelet. In the stone's hard shine she had seen a vein for her own wrist, an ornament through which her ancient body might declare itself. But the mason was waiting with his shoulders tired, and the doorstep had a purpose more urgent than her revelation. Would that face hold a foot safely in rain? He turned the piece, tested it against its neighbours, and showed her where the edge would stand too high.

Thus the earth received correction from a man whose name she had not yet asked. She laughed, softly and without triumph. Her red mouth, reflected in a puddle beside the mortar, broke into fragments when he rinsed his hand. The old bone of the ridge did not need to be displayed at the expense of another bruised knee. What outlasted an empire might still require care in the placing of one stone.

He worked until the pale seam lay at a slant, visible without ruling the surface. Each blow sounded small beside the traffic, yet she followed it as one follows a pulse through fever. In imagination the city's monuments bent down to this threshold. Their banners, verses, and desires for permanence stood waiting while a tired hand made an entrance fit for those who would never be named upon its wall. She had held their dynasties; today she held the basin so it would not overturn.

The old woman returned from the shops before the mortar had settled. Sagorika stepped forward, offered her arm, and guided her through another doorway. The woman complained about the inconvenience, then looked back and recognized the repair. Her annoyance eased into a practical blessing: may it last through the rains. No eternity was demanded. Sagorika felt the scale of that wish enter her, modest and therefore heavy.

At dusk the mason washed his tools. She learned his name, paid him, and brought a chair while he waited for his ride. Neither spoke of the stone's hidden blood. They discussed the falling light and the delay of buses. Under the dust his hands were ordinary, and their ordinariness troubled every grand word she had prepared.

Next morning she crossed the threshold in her high heels. The seam caught the sun for an instant, then vanished beneath her step. She went back and checked that the edge was level. The mountain had become part of a welcome. Within that use its long endurance found no final explanation, but it had acquired a living obligation.
