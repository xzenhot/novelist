# The Das Dynasty

At the foot of a weathered wall, a man showed Sagorika a name he could no longer read. The letters had withdrawn unevenly into stone; one stroke remained sharp, another had softened until his finger followed only expectation. Was this, he asked, a remnant of the Das dynasty? She did not pretend that the wall had supplied an answer. The early sultanate belonged to the epic's procession of banners and builders, but this particular mark kept its own silence beneath the afternoon.

The man wanted the name to give the stone its dignity. Sagorika asked him to look lower, where a loose fragment had slipped into the passage. People were turning sideways to avoid it. A woman carrying a bundle paused, measured the narrow space, and waited for those approaching from the other direction. No inscription praised that interval of patience. Yet here was the wall affecting a life, more plainly than any title it might once have displayed. The man stepped aside to let her through and found that his question had acquired another weight.

In Sagorika's imagining, the Das rulers came upon the plain with the wish to build, and the earth received their stones without learning obedience to their names. She would not make the ground's patience an excuse for cruelty. Stone might bear a ruler and a labourer without distinction, but the bodies beneath a command did not therefore suffer equally. The banners passed; the burden had been carried by particular shoulders. To speak of vanished power without remembering that burden was to erase the people once again in the act of remembering their kings.

The woman shifted her bundle. Its cloth caught against the broken edge, and a small packet fell from an opening. Before the man had decided whether helping would interrupt his inquiry, Sagorika had knelt to gather it. He bent beside her. Together they held the cloth while the woman secured the knot. Her thanks was brief, for she had somewhere to go; soon she was among the moving figures at the end of the passage. The wall had offered them no recovered name, yet their hands had found a task requiring neither certainty nor a title.

We stood where the bundle had rested. The man brushed dust from his knees and looked once more at the inscription. He still wished to understand it, and Sagorika did not scorn that wish. Knowledge could be a form of care, provided it did not make the unreadable more worthy of attention than the person struggling beneath it. We left the loose fragment undisturbed where moving it might disturb the old fabric, and guided another passerby around its edge. A name can wait for the patience of those able to read it; a burden sometimes cannot wait another breath. May the city grant its learning that modest order, and may the nobility it seeks among vanished rulers become visible, for an instant, in the unnamed hands that make a narrow passage easier to cross.
