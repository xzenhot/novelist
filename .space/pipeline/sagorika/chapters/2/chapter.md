# The Aravalli Remembers

## Question

What does a mountain remember when it has been worn down to a ridge? When the Aravalli, older than the Himalayas, older than the Alps, older than almost every mountain the human eye has named, lies low and broken through the heart of Delhi — does it still remember being young? And does the city, which walks over it every day, know that it is walking on the oldest bone of the earth?

## Oration

I am the Aravalli, and I was old before the first word was spoken. I rose when the earth was still learning to be earth, a spine of quartzite and schist that lifted toward a sky that had no name. The Himalayas came later, young and proud and covered in snow, and I watched them rise the way an old woman watches a child grow tall. I did not envy them. I had already seen everything there was to see.

Then the people came. They came to my slopes for shelter and for stone, and they built their first fires in my shadow. They called me the ridge, and they forgot that I was once a mountain. They cut roads through me and laid their cities over me, and still I held them, the way the ground holds everything, without complaint and without end.

I remember the Saraswati when she ran beside me, bright and full, before she vanished into the sand. I remember the first kings who climbed my slopes to look out over the plain and choose the place where their city would rise. I remember every dynasty that thought it would last forever, and I remember how each one of them became dust that settled back into me. I am the oldest witness in Delhi, and I have never once looked away.

## Benediction

May the city remember that it walks on the oldest stone. May it treat the ridge not as a thing to be cut and buried, but as the bone that holds it up. And may the Aravalli, patient and unbroken, still be standing when the last tower has fallen and the last name has been forgotten.
