# The Aravalli Remembers

At the edge of the Ridge, Sagorika asked me to put down the stone I had lifted. It fitted my palm so well that I had already imagined it upon my desk, a little portion of antiquity made obedient to my room. What does a mountain lose, I asked, by one small fragment? She knelt beside the hollow where it had rested. The afternoon lay upon her shoulders, and in that shallow vacancy a root curved nakedly through dust.

I had come to hear the mountain remember. I expected an account of vanished heights, the grand speech of an elder before whom the young Himalayas must fall silent. Instead she pointed to the root. A trail of ants, interrupted by my curiosity, divided and searched along its edge. Their bewilderment was so minute that I nearly dismissed it. Yet the stone in my hand had ceased to be a souvenir; it had acquired the weight of an interrupted passage, a roof lifted from a dwelling whose inhabitants had never consented to become my lesson.

I am the Aravalli, she said, and your cities are among the brief things I have borne. Her voice did not thunder. It moved through the scrub with the dry sound of leaves touching one another. In the book of her body, the old rock preceded the forts, the towers, the names by which rulers had tried to gather the plain into their keeping. She remembered shelter sought upon her slopes and stone carried away for walls. But remembering was no promise that every wound would close. The endurance of the earth could not excuse the haste of those who cut it.

I turned the fragment over. On one side it held the sun; on the other it retained a cooler darkness. So near together were these two conditions that my fingers joined them without understanding. I thought of the city beyond the trees, its separate rooms, its quarrels over belonging, its hunger for a secure foundation. We asked the ground to hold us while forgetting the lives for which we ourselves might become either shelter or disturbance. The mountain needed no resemblance to our bodies to make this kinship binding; we were already standing inside its consequences.

Sagorika drew her hand away, leaving the choice with me. I lowered the stone carefully, turning it until its old underside met the hollow. The ants found their shaded edge again. Nothing applauded the restoration. The afternoon continued, and somewhere beyond the scrub a motor sounded, impatient with a delay I could not see. I rose with an empty hand that felt less empty than before. We took the path back without claiming that the Ridge was healed. A small roof had been returned; the greater work remained beyond our sight. May the city learn this measure of remembrance: to know what bears it, and to leave room beneath its own restless hands for another life to pass.
