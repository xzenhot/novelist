# The Geology of Memory

What does the ground remember when a house forgets its inhabitants? Sagorika returned to a street she had known before its walls were raised anew. The turn was familiar; the doorway was gone. A bright entrance stood where she remembered a low step, and for a moment she doubted the fidelity of her own mind. Delhi changed its face so confidently that memory began to seem an impolite visitor.

She searched for the small unevenness in the path where rain had once gathered. It remained, though the paving around it was new. After the night's shower a little water still occupied the hollow. Sagorika stood beside it with the absurd relief of recognizing a friend who could not speak. The reflected window belonged to the new building. The place where the reflection rested belonged to a longer continuance.

A resident asked whether she was looking for someone. She said she was looking for a step. He smiled uneasily, suspecting grief or confusion, and she understood how strange her answer sounded. There had been people here, she added. He told her there were people here still. The correction entered her without cruelty. Remembrance could become possessive if it denied the present its right to inhabit the earth.

They spoke beside the puddle. He knew nothing of her vanished doorway, but he knew which room grew hottest and how the water entered during heavy rain. His knowledge had begun where hers had ended. Beneath both lay older matters: the ground's bearing, its hollows, the water's persistence. Sagorika thought of quartzite and schist, alluvium and hidden moisture, the book's archive written without the intention of comforting anyone.

She nearly called the hollow proof that the earth remembered. Instead she allowed the metaphor its proper tenderness. Ground did not recall a mother's voice as a daughter did. It retained pressures, paths, the consequences of shape and substance. Human memory could find a companion there without demanding that stone possess a human heart. The vanished Saraswati within her imagination was such a companion: absence keeping open a place for attention.

The resident brought a small broom to clear leaves from the hollow's outlet. Sagorika helped, and the water began to move. Her familiar puddle thinned until the old unevenness stood bare. She felt a brief wish to stop it, then let the wish pass. To preserve the sign at the expense of the living rooms would be a poor devotion to what had been loved. When she left, the path was drier. The doorway in her mind remained, no longer requiring the street to restore it. Let memory deepen care rather than arrest change, she thought. Beneath her steps the earth carried both the departed house and the inhabited one, neither explaining nor reconciling them. She walked home with the address unwritten, holding instead the altered weight of belonging to a place that could continue without keeping her face. Behind her, someone opened the new door to evening.
