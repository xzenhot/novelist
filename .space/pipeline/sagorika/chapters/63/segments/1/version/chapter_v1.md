# The Jamali Kamali

Sagorika came to Jamali Kamali with a question she had promised herself not to ask aloud. A friend had stopped answering her letters. In Dwarka she had told herself that silence must conceal some meaning large enough to excuse its wound; among old stones, she hoped, the meaning might consent to appear. She stood where the afternoon reddened the surfaces before her, a woman wearing red upon her mouth, and felt how easily resemblance could be mistaken for reply. The earth was full of closed chambers. Must every chamber belong to someone who would never answer?

A young visitor nearby held a telephone toward his companion, asking her to whisper so that he might record how the place transformed her voice. She laughed, tried once, and was told to do it again without laughing. The second whisper was scarcely audible. He listened to the recording with disappointment. Sagorika watched the woman's face close, not from mystery but from the small fatigue of being instructed how to be remembered. Then the telephone slipped. Its fall made a sharp sound against stone, and all three bent toward it with the same ordinary alarm.

The screen had cracked across the captured image. The woman picked it up and handed it back without speaking. Her companion began to apologize, first to the broken thing, then awkwardly to her. Sagorika moved away before their words became hers to keep. She had come hunting a secret, but the afternoon offered instead the sight of two people deciding whether to hear each other. Behind her the woman said that she had not wished to whisper. Her natural voice crossed the intervening space clearly. It possessed no ancient authority, and needed none.

In her bag Sagorika carried the last letter she had written. She unfolded it. The page contained questions arranged like doors which could only open inward toward her own longing. Where are you; why have you forgotten; what have I become to you? She read them beneath the patient color of the stone and felt no revelation. The ridge did not lend her its age. The vanished river did not explain the living friend's absence. All the earth's memory could not make another person answer before that person was willing, or able, to speak.

She turned the paper over and wrote one further sentence: I shall leave room for what I do not know. It did not cancel the pain. It altered the place she gave it, moving it from the other woman's locked door into her own keeping. When she folded the page again, the young visitors were leaving together, the damaged telephone dark between them. Their voices were unrecorded now, unequal, occasionally interrupted by the street. Sagorika followed at a distance. She would send her letter, and then cease filling the silence with invented testimony. Some secrets belonged to stone; some were merely lives beyond her reach. Evening opened over Delhi, and she entered it carrying a question that had finally learned to make space for another human voice.
