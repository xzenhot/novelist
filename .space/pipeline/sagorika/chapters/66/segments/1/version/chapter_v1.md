# The Khari Baoli

The first breath Sagorika took in Khari Baoli made her cough. She had imagined fragrance as an invitation, a soft procession entering through the body's open doors; the air arrived instead with the force of a hand. Beside a sack, a worker tied cloth over his mouth and resumed lifting. His eyes watered without interrupting his labor. Sagorika stepped back, ashamed of the ornamental language she had brought with her. What did it mean to call a scent ancient while the lungs that bore it were required to endure only until evening?

A seam gave way as the worker raised the sack. Spice poured across his shoe and spread into a bright, bitter fan. He froze for an instant, calculating something she could not see: the loss, the blame, the delay. Then he lowered what remained and began gathering the spill with a piece of cardboard. People edged around him. Sagorika crouched on the other side and used an empty folded packet to keep the spreading grains from the tread of the crowd. The dust entered her throat again. This time she could not retreat without abandoning the small boundary her hands were holding.

The worker told her which portion to leave. Dirt had entered it; appearances could not restore it. She obeyed, surprised by the exactness of his refusal to save everything. The city praised abundance, yet here abundance had to be sorted with care, and some of its brilliant color belonged already to waste. In the ridge's dust she had imagined the patient remains of mountains. This dust was another matter, paid for, carried, breathed. She would not confuse them merely because both could redden the crease of a palm.

They shifted the sound sack into shade. Someone brought another container, and the worker transferred what could be kept. His movements grew less hurried once the torn fabric no longer threatened a second loss. Sagorika straightened painfully, her elegant shoes powdered and her eyes streaming. He offered water from a bottle without ceremony. She drank a little and returned it. The wetness traveling down her throat seemed to loosen the entire city: market and plain, kitchen and river, the unglamorous passages by which the living were permitted to continue. No vanished river could supply this mouthful. Someone had filled the bottle.

Before she left, she bought the small quantity she had originally come for. The worker weighed it, and she waited while he checked the measure twice. At home in Dwarka, the packet would release its fragrance above a cooking pot, and someone might praise the richness of the smell. She would remember the torn seam, the portion left upon the ground, the cloth darkened by breath. The pleasure would remain; she would no longer ask it to erase its journey. Carrying the packet through the evening, Sagorika kept it upright against her breast, as though within its light folded walls lay the balance of a heavy sack, and the hands that had prevented its loss.
