# The Oldest Mountain

Who shall console a mountain for becoming small? At dawn Sagorika climbed the Ridge with a man who had promised to show her Delhi. He carried his shining telephone as though the city were a thing that could be gathered whole into a palm. Below them, windows kindled; above them, the sky possessed neither balcony nor name. He sought a summit worthy of the morning. She stopped where the worn stone scarcely rose above the thorn.

Here, she said. He laughed, believing tenderness had made her easy to please. This broken height could not rival the towers beyond it. He turned his glass toward those towers, and she watched their little upright images enter his hand. Then a loose stone shifted beneath his shoe. His arm struck hers; his breath, suddenly mortal, broke the stillness. She steadied him against the very rock he had dismissed.

For a while neither spoke. The stone received his weight without becoming greater, without demanding that he remember its assistance. Sagorika laid her palm beside his. Beneath the warmth left by yesterday she imagined an older warmth, the buried fire of the book her body carried. The Aravalli had endured before these streets, before the city's dynasties had learned to call endurance conquest. Its ancient age could not be restored to visible height by any proud recital.

Thou hast mistaken remaining for rising, she thought, and knew the rebuke belonged also to herself. Had she not worn her heels like arguments against the ground? Had she not sometimes accepted admiration as evidence of being understood? The mountain had relinquished height grain by grain. What seemed defeat had entered other places as dust, had laid a fine inheritance upon the passing air. Yet she would not praise every wound as wisdom; a mountain cut for vanity was wounded, though it bore the cutting silently.

The man examined the scrape upon his wrist. A slender red line appeared, brighter than the towers in his darkened glass. He asked how old this stone was, and she answered that its antiquity exceeded the kingdoms they were accustomed to mourning. She offered no procession of numbers. Let his hand first learn what it had leaned upon. He crouched and touched the roughness with a care that had been absent from his photograph.

By the time they descended, the city was loudly awake. He put his telephone away to help her across a broken step. She needed little help, but accepted the offered hand; recognition should be allowed its first awkward practice. Behind them the Ridge remained low, neither crowned nor vindicated. Before them the towers rose in their borrowed morning. Sagorika carried the difference down into the street: to stand above another is a brief arrangement, but to bear another without consuming him is a labour through which even the diminished may remain immense. At the road she brushed the dust from his sleeve, and left her own sleeve untouched.
