# The Hand-Axe Maker

## Question

Who was he, the man at the core? Among the many hunters who worked the ridge, the poem imagines one — the maker whose edge was so true that we hold it now. What did he see that the others did not, and what did his excellence cost him, and give him?

## Oration

He worked slightly apart from the band — that much the poem is free to imagine, and imagines carefully. The others knapped quickly, serviceably, at the pace of need. He turned the core of quartzite in his hands until he found the angle inside it, the hidden symmetry the stone had been keeping. Then he struck, examined the scar, struck again. Flakes fell like small decisions. Where the others stopped at useful, he worked until the edge ran true the whole length of the tool — and then, only then, held it up to the low sun and looked along it.

What did this cost him? The band's rhythm, for one: he ate later, talked less, was thought a little strange. What did it give him? The first signature in the archive — not a name, which he had no use for and no medium to write it in, but a standard: the true edge, the patient strike, the thing finished only when nothing false remains in it. Anyone who picks up one of his tools knows, without being told, that a person stood behind this edge deciding what *good* meant. That decision, more than any single axe, is what he left us.

Because craft is where culture begins. Before writing, before worship, before the altar and the well and the wall, someone cared about the quality of an edge beyond its use — and that caring is the seed of everything this sequence has visited. The altar's fitted stones, the stepwell's geometry, the rampart's coursing, even the poem's own line: all descend from the stubbornness of one anonymous maker who would not stop at useful.

He never learned that his tool would lie in a museum case under glass. He only knew the stone had a right shape in it, and that finding it was his work — the work of the lowest stone in the arch, invisible, unsigned, bearing the whole of what came after. I, who choose words the way he chose flakes, recognize a colleague across the whole depth of human time, and bow to him the only way he would respect: by finishing the thing properly.

## Benediction

May every hand that shapes anything care as the maker cared. May the standard he set — the true edge, the patient strike, the refusal to stop at useful — remain the measure of all work done on this ridge. And may the poem, like the axe, be finished only when nothing false remains in it.
