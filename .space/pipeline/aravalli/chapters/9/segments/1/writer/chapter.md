# The Lost Layer

## Question

What is gone, and who will say it out loud? I walk the quarried outcrops, the concrete mouths of buried streams, the graded lots where old surveys show ridge — and I ask the question no plaque ever asks: when a layer of the record is erased, does the mountain grieve? And if it does not grieve, who must?

## Oration

This poem is an inventory, and inventories must be exact, because grief for geology earns nothing if it is vague. Item: an outcrop that a survey marked a century ago now ends flush with a cleaned lot; the quartzite that held the fold went to road metal, and the cars run on it daily without noticing they are riding on a page. Item: a stream the stepwells once caught now runs in a storm drain under a road, nameless, culverted at both ends, carrying monsoon water that no longer recharges anything it was meant to reach. Item: a site the old lines show as intact was graded flat before anyone thought to look — whatever it held, hand-tools or hearthstones, is now uniform subgrade.

I keep the ledger spare. No decoration, because the emptiness is the evidence, and an empty vessel filled with pretty language is a second erasure. The mountain does not mourn; I want to be honest about that too. Stone does not miss what is taken from it the way a body misses a limb — it simply continues, slightly reduced, exactly as the quarries left it. The mourning is ours by assignment. The record loses pages, and a reader who arrives late cannot recover what no one transcribed.

What remains is the naming. I say each loss into the poem the way a clerk reads into the record — flatly, completely, without music — because the plainest sentence is the only honest monument. The outcrop. The stream. The site. Gone, gone, gone. This is not despair; it is accounting, and accounting is what makes the surviving ledger credible. The ridge is diminished but unbroken, and the difference between those two words — the distance between them — is the whole discipline this sequence asks of its reader.

## Benediction

May what was lost be named, so that absence keeps a place in the archive. May the quarried faces, the culverted streams, and the graded sites be remembered as exactly as the surviving shrines — for they are also monuments, of a kind. And may the city feel the missing pages when it reads its own history, so that no further layer goes unrecorded into the dark.
