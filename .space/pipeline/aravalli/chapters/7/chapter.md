# The Contractor's Case

## Question

What if the man with the plans is not a villain? A contractor stands before a room of officials and residents and makes his case honestly: the city must grow, housing must rise, the economy must breathe — and a ridge of old stone cannot outweigh the needs of millions. The poet asks: how do we answer an argument that is sincere, partly right, and would still erase the mountain?

## Oration

The poem gives the contractor his full voice. He speaks of families waiting for flats, of jobs, of the pressing arithmetic of a swelling city. He does not hate the ridge; he simply does not see it — to him it is acreage, an obstruction of the obvious. His honesty is the poem's real opponent, harder to answer than greed.

The poet answers not with slogans but with scale. The contractor's deadline is a quarter's end; the ridge's deadline is geological. He points to the hand-axe in the display case, the altar stone, the stepwell — and asks what the city will be able to show of itself when every witness is leveled. The argument ends unresolved, as honest arguments do: the case for growth is real, and so is the record that growth would bury. The reader is left holding both, feeling their weight, understanding that the fate of the ridge will be decided in rooms like this one, by people like this man, with arguments no poem can settle but no poem should surrender.

## Benediction

May those who build be granted longer sight, and those who preserve be granted honest opponents. May the case for the living never be answered by erasing the dead, nor the case for the mountain by ignoring the living. And may every hearing keep a chair empty for the ridge, which cannot speak except through those who learned to read it.
