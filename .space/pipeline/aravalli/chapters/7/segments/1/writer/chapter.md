# The Contractor's Case

## Question

What if the man with the plans is not a villain? A contractor stands before the room — officials, residents, a stenographer recording everything — and makes his case honestly: the city must grow, housing must rise, the economy must breathe, and a ridge of old stone cannot outweigh the needs of millions. I sit among the citizens and ask: how do we answer an argument that is sincere, partly right, and would still erase the mountain?

## Oration

Let him speak, and let him be heard fully, because the poem loses everything if it cheats. He speaks of families waiting for flats — young households doubled up in one rooms, the city swelling at its seams. He speaks of jobs: quarrying, hauling, building, the ladder a young man from the districts can actually climb. He gestures at the master plan and its blank spaces, the land doing nothing, as he puts it, while people do without. He does not hate the ridge. He simply does not see it — to him it is acreage with an attitude problem, an obstruction standing politely in the way of the obvious.

His honesty is the true opponent. Greed I could name and dismiss; sincerity I must answer. So I answer with scale, as quietly as he spoke. His deadline is a quarter's end. The ridge's deadline is geological — a schedule in which this quarter, this year, this century is a single thin layer among billions. I point to the display case at the back of the room: a hand-axe found on the very land in the plans. I mention the altar stones, the stepwell, the fort wall. I ask what the city will be able to show of itself in five hundred years if every witness is leveled — what its grandchildren will hold.

And I concede what must be conceded: the need is real. He is not wrong that people suffer, and the ridge cannot be allowed to become the excuse of the comfortable. This is the knot the poem refuses to cut: the case for growth is real, and so is the record that growth would bury, and both sit in the same room, and only one of them can speak after today.

The case ends unresolved, as honest arguments do. What stays with me is the sight of the lowest stone in the room's arch — the ridge itself, bearing every argument built across it, silent under the weight of everyone's best intentions. Its only advocates are the record and the reader.

## Benediction

May those who build be granted longer sight, and those who preserve be granted honest opponents. May the case for the living never be answered by erasing the dead, nor the case for the mountain by ignoring the living. And may every hearing keep one chair empty for the ridge — which cannot speak except through those who learned to read it.
