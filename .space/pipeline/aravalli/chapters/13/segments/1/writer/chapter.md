# The Fold Of Time

## Question

What shape does time take? The geologists read the ridge's rock and find it bent — strata laid down level as a sea floor, now folded and upended by pressures older than animals. I walk a cut slope where the bedding stands nearly vertical and I ask: if the stone teaches that even the level ground has been folded, what does that teach us about the past we assume is straight?

## Oration

Begin with the rock's biography, briefly and honestly. These were sediments once — sand and silt settling flat in ancient water, layer under layer, the most orderly arrangement in nature. Then the earth's slow machinery closed on them. Pressure and heat, working over spans the mind refuses, bent the whole stack as if it were cloth. The sea floor became hilltop. The horizontal became vertical. And the eras, which we experience as one thing after another, are here held pressed together, deepest and nearest touching, the folded strata an archive closed by its own weight.

This is why the ridge is the right teacher for our sense of time. The city plans in decades and mistakes its plans for permanence; the fold corrects the error without cruelty. At the ridge's scale, the present is a thin and recent layer — a skin of years over a body of ages. But the fold teaches the other half of the lesson too: the thin layer is the only part that can still choose what gets written next. The older layers are fixed; the present is the living margin.

And what becomes of names, in a world shaped like this? The empires that straightened roads across the plain and named their grids after themselves — their straight lines have already passed into other hands, their names into textbooks, their geometries redrawn. The fold keeps no names. It keeps curves: the bedding plane's long arc, the ridge's line against the sky, the curve no surveyor imposed and no surveyor can erase. The straight-line ambitions fade; the curved stone remains, holding all the eras it touches in one long embrace.

So I learn to distrust the line and trust the curve. Time is not a corridor with the past behind us; it is a fold, and the distant is bent into contact with the near. Put your hand on the tilted strata and you are touching the sea floor that yesterday — by the ridge's calendar — lay under water a hundred million years deep.

## Benediction

May we read the fold and distrust the line. May the deep layers press their patience into the thin present. And may whoever walks the ridge feel the eras touch where the strata bend — and walk more humbly for it.
