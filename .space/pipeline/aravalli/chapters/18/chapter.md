# The First Fire

## Question

Who kept the first flame, and what did they promise it? On a sheltered ledge of the ridge, a fire was kept alive through a season — fed, shielded, carried. The poet asks: what changed in the human heart the night the fire was kept instead of merely found?

## Oration

The poem returns to the ledge. Lightning or friction made fire before people; keeping fire is what people added. The poem imagines the keepers: the one who gathers fuel, the one who banks the coals, the one who wakes at night when the rain leans in. Around the kept flame, the first society forms — the first sharing of food, the first stories told to a listener that never sleeps.

The meditation reads the kept fire as the first covenant. To keep a flame is to make a promise to a future: that tomorrow it will still burn, that the cold will not win, that what was entrusted will be handed on. Every altar that followed — the Vedic fire on ridge stone, the temple lamps, the funeral pyres — repeats that original promise. And the poet sees the ridge itself as the first keeper: the mountain that sheltered the ledge, gave the fuel, held the warmth in its stone long after the embers died. The fire is out. The promise continues.

## Benediction

May every flame we keep be kept as the first flame was kept — with vigilance and shared warmth. May the covenant of the kept fire extend to the ridge itself, which sheltered it. And may what we are entrusted with — stone, water, memory — still be burning when the next keepers wake.
