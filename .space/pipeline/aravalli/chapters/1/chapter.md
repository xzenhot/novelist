# The Oldest Stone

## Question

What is the oldest thing a hand can hold? On a surviving outcrop of the Delhi Ridge, the poet lifts a quartzite hand-axe from the leaf litter and asks it plainly: can a mountain be the oldest witness of a civilization — older than every city it has sheltered, older than the names we give it? The question is not rhetorical. The stone sits in the palm, warm from the sun, and waits.

## Oration

The encounter is recognition, not spectacle — a stone that seems already known before it is named. The knapped edge fits the curve of a hand that vanished a hundred thousand years ago, and the fit is exact. Around it the city hurries: traffic on the flyover, sirens thinning into distance, a generation-fast metropolis that rebuilds itself every twenty years. Beside that haste the ridge keeps its own calendar, a billion years of fold and outcrop, patient as sediment.

The sacred, the poet realizes, is not imported into this place. It is discovered inside the ordinary — inside a common stone of the Delhi Ridge, inside dust and sky and the small heat of noon. And with that discovery comes the first stirring of a thought he does not yet trust: that the mountain is an archive, that each layer of quartzite is a page. He holds the thought lightly. The stone is not a metaphor. It is stone — old, real, and vulnerable — and any reading of it must begin by honoring that.

## Benediction

May the hand that finds a hand-axe find also the patience of the ridge that made the maker. May the city learn to walk slowly where the oldest stone lies open to the sky. And may the question remain open, carried forward like water under quartzite — so that we arrive at the next poem with a quieter, cleaner gaze.
