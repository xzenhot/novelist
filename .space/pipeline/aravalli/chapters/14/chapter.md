# Quartzite And Granite

## Question

Why two stones, and not one? The ridge carries both quartzite — metamorphosed, glassy, resistant — and granite — crystalline, deep-risen, slow-cooled. The poet walks the boundary between them and asks: what are the two temperaments of this mountain, and which one does the city resemble?

## Oration

The poem reads the two stones as two characters of one body. Quartzite is the ridge's endurance: sand changed by heat and pressure into something that rings under the hammer and weathers into ridgelines. Granite is its interior: risen slowly from depth, coarse-grained, holding its crystals like a held breath. One stone remembers pressure; the other remembers fire — and the ridge is the place where their two memories meet.

The meditation turns on the city. Delhi too is quartzite and granite: a surface that takes whatever pressure history applies and keeps its form, and a depth of older populations, older waters, older griefs, cooled slowly into what it is. The poet refuses to prefer one stone. The ridge needs both — the resistant skin and the patient core — and the record it keeps is written in their joint hand. To quarry either without reading both is to tear a page written in two inks at once.

## Benediction

May the two stones keep their conversation beneath our feet. May the city learn endurance from the one and depth from the other. And may whoever reads the ridge honor both temperaments — pressure's memory and fire's memory — as the two hands that wrote the mountain's book.
