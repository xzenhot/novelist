# The Continuous Record

## Question

What if all of it is one document? The poet lays out the evidence side by side — the hand-axe, the fire-altar, the stepwell, the fort wall, the quarry face — and asks: are these separate relics, or pages of a single continuous record, written on one ridge by one unbroken people?

## Oration

The poem reads the ridge as a shelf. On it, in order: a flake struck by a hunter whose language we cannot guess; an altar where the first hymns steamed into the cold morning; a well where medieval hands drew water in the dark; a rampart of squared quartzite raised by sultans; a scar where modern machines took the mountain in tons instead of blocks. Five eras, one material. Every era wrote on the same quartzite, in its own hand, and the ridge never once refused the pen.

This is the poem's quiet revelation: history here is not a sequence of separate cities but a single handwriting that changes slant — hunter, hermit, well-keeper, mason, contractor, and now the walker with a notebook. The record is continuous because the mountain is continuous; it is uninterrupted only because the stone outlasts every hand that writes on it. The poet understands, then, what his obligation is: a record this continuous deserves a reader this faithful, and erasure anywhere breaks the whole document.

## Benediction

May the pages of the ridge be kept in their order — tool, altar, well, wall, wound — so that no era reads alone. May the city recognize its handwriting in the older hands. And may the continuity that stone preserved be preserved in turn by memory, which is the only pen the mountain has left.
