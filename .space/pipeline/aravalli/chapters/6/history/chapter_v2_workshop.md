# The Sultan's Quarry

## Question

When did the first blow fall that was meant to remove the mountain, not to understand it? In the sultanate centuries, the ridge was cut on a new scale — its quartzite squared into walls and gateways for the forts of Delhi. The poet asks: is a quarry a wound, a harvest, or the moment a civilization begins to spend a inheritance it cannot replace?

## Oration

The poem stands at the quarry face and hears the ring of chisels where birdsong used to be. The stone that had held hunters' edges and altar fires is now cut in blocks, dressed, hauled, and raised into ramparts. The same quartzite that memorized a billion years of folding becomes a fort wall that will memorize seven centuries of courts and sieges.

The poem refuses easy grief. The forts are beautiful; the masons were masters; the city needed defending. But the scale has changed. The hunters took flakes; the sultans took faces — whole strata lifted out of the ridge's page. The mountain endures even this, the way an old body endures labor, but the poem marks the moment exactly: the first time the archive itself became raw material. Every later quarry, every contractor's map, begins here, at this ring of chisel on ridge stone. The mountain remembers being read, and it remembers being cut, and it keeps both memories in the same fold.

## Benediction

May the beauty of the walls never be separated from the cost of the stone. May the quarry faces left raw be read as margins where the mountain showed its interior. And may the city that rose from the ridge learn at last to build without removing the witness that makes its history possible.
