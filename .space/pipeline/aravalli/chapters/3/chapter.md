# The Prehistoric Hunters

## Question

Who left these tools, and what did they hope the future would find? Long before Delhi had a name, a band of hunters knapped quartzite on the ridge — striking flakes from cores beside a stream that has since gone underground. The poet stands where they knelt and asks: when the maker struck that last deliberate flake, was he thinking of the animal, the fire, the evening — or, faintly, of us?

## Oration

The poem kneels into the scene. Dawn on the outcrop; smoke from a temporary camp; the rhythm of hammer-stone against core, patient as the mountain itself. The hunters read the ridge the way their descendants would read scripture: where the quartzite breaks clean, where the game crosses, where water gathers after rain. The ridge teaches them, and they answer with skill.

Each finished hand-axe is both tool and testament — an edge shaped so carefully that it outlives its purpose by a hundred millennia. They could not have intended us, and yet they left the future a library of edges. The poet turns one flake in his hand and feels the conversation continue: stone speaking to stone, hand answering hand across the whole depth of human time. First makers and latest builders are one continuous people, and every outcrop is a possible page of what they wrote.

## Benediction

May the patience of the first makers steady whoever walks the ridge today. May every tool recovered be read as a letter, not a relic. And may we prove worthy of what the hunters left: a record that begins, and must not end, in stone.
