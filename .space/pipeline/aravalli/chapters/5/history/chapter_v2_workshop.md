# The Stepwell

## Question

Why would a city build its thirst downward into stone? Where the ridge meets the plain, a medieval stepwell descends in receding galleries to catch the mountain's runoff — architecture turned toward the water table like a question turned toward an answer. The poet asks: what did the builders know about patience that we have forgotten?

## Oration

The poem descends the steps. Each tier is cooler than the last; the air changes; the noise of the city gives way to dripping and echo. The stepwell is human ingenuity joined to the ridge's generosity — the mountain gathers the monsoon in its quartzite and releases it slowly, and the well receives the gift in geometry: symmetrical stages, shaded landings, a pool of green light at the bottom.

The poem meditates on the marriage of stone and water. The ridge did not hoard its rain; the builders did not demand it. Together they made a covenant: catch the runoff, hold it through the dry months, return it to the hands that draw it. Women at the well-edge, caravans watering, children on the stairs — the structure held a civilization's daily life in its cool throat. Against this, the modern city draws its water blind, through pipes, from depths it never visits. The stepwell remains as an argument: that a city can take from a mountain and still see what it takes from.

## Benediction

May the galleries that held the rain hold our memory as well. May the city learn again to descend, to see the water it drinks, and to thank the stone that filters it. And may every stepwell surviving on the ridge's flank be kept as a school of patience, open to whoever is thirsty in more ways than one.
