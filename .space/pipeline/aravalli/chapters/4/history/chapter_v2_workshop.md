# The Vedic Fire

## Question

What did the first worshippers hear in this stone? Deep in the ridge's forest, where the canopy thins onto a terrace of quartzite, a hermitage once kept its fire burning on an altar of ridge stone. The poet asks: when the first hymns rose here — hymns to the rivers the ridge feeds — did the singers know they were inscribing something into the mountain's memory that would outlast every empire?

## Oration

The poem enters the hermitage at dawn. The fire is fed at first light; the altar stones are laid from the ridge itself, so that worship and mountain are one material. Smoke rises through the trees. Around the terrace, the forest hermits chant the first poetry of this place — verses that name the rivers as goddesses, the rain as law, the fire as witness.

The altar of ridge stone is the heart of the poem: a human arrangement of the mountain's own body, offered back to it, so that the ridge becomes a participant in its own veneration. The hymns travel outward along the water — down the seasonal streams, into the plains the ridge quietly feeds — so that the first songs of this land were, in truth, songs about the ridge, sung without knowing it. Fire, water, stone: the three witnesses meet on this terrace, and the poet hears their conversation still continuing beneath the noise of the modern city.

## Benediction

May the fire that once burned on ridge stone be rekindled as attention. May every river we name recall the hymns that first honored it. And may the mountain that carried the first prayers continue to receive them — from chant, from poem, from the grateful silence of those who climb at dawn.
