# The Vedic Fire

## Question

What did the first worshippers hear in this stone? Deep in the ridge's forest, where the trees thin onto a terrace of quartzite, a hermitage once kept its fire burning on an altar of ridge stone. I climb at first light to stand where the chanters stood, and I ask: when the first hymns rose here — hymns to the rivers this ridge feeds — did the singers know they were writing something into the mountain's memory that would outlast every empire built below?

## Oration

Dawn, and the fire is fed. That is the whole of the ritual, and the whole of the teaching: the flame exists only in this tending, this morning, this armful of dry wood. Nothing of it is stored; nothing of it carries over. What carries over is the promise, renewed each dawn, that the tending will continue. The hermits understood that a kept fire is the perfect image of the only present — the one life actually lived, the single breath between two silences.

The altar is laid from the ridge's own body: quartzite blocks pried from the terrace's edge, set in the arrangement that holds the fire's shape. This is what moves me most in the scene I have reconstructed across many dawns of visiting. The worshipers did not haul holiness in from somewhere else. They took the mountain as it was and offered it back — stone serving as the vessel of stone's own veneration, the ridge participating in its first prayers on this ground.

Smoke rises straight in the still air, through trees whose ancestors shaded the first chanters. Around the terrace the hymns go up with the smoke — verses that call the rivers by name, that praise the rain as law and the fire as witness between gods and the gathering dark. The oldest poetry of this land is addressed to water. Follow the logic of the landscape and you see why: these forests catch the monsoon; the ridge's flanks feed seasonal streams that run east toward the great river; the water the hermits praised was water the ridge had gathered and given. Their hymns were, without their knowing it, hymns to the mountain itself.

Fire, water, stone: three witnesses meeting on one terrace. The fire tending the present, the water carrying the blessing outward to the plains, the stone holding the whole arrangement in memory — long, as it turns out, after the chanters, the fire, and the language of the hymns have gone.

I have climbed here in the city's early roar and heard, under the traffic, the continuity still running: someone feeding something at dawn, somewhere, because a promise made long ago is still being renewed. The fire is out; the altar stones remain; the tending has changed objects. It is ours now.

## Benediction

May the fire that once burned on ridge stone be rekindled as attention — fed each morning, only in the morning. May every river we name recall the hymns that first honored it, and the mountain that first gathered it. And may the ridge that carried the first prayers continue to receive them: from chant, from poem, from the grateful silence of those who climb at dawn to keep the only present alive.
