# The Hymn To The Rivers

## Question

Who sings to water now? The Vedic seers named the rivers the ridge feeds — praised them, worshipped them, married their waters into verse. Today the same water moves in pipes and drains, nameless. The poet asks: what is lost when water is no longer named — and can a poem begin the naming again?

## Oration

The poem recalls the hymns. The seers stood where the ridge's streams gathered and called the water by name — river as goddess, rain as law, flood as grace and warning. To name a river was to honor it; to sing it was to bind a people to its source. The first poetry of this land was hydrology set to reverence: the ridge's gift, recognized in language.

Against that music the poem sets the modern silence. The streams run in storm drains; the rivers are measured in quality indices; the water arrives without biography. The poet does not sentimentalize the past — the hymns served their own politics — but he insists on the loss: a city that forgets the names of its water forgets its obligations to it. So the poem does the old work in the current tongue: it names the ridge's streams, the aquifer they feed, the Yamuna they reach. The hymn fades where the chapter ends, and the poem admits plainly that it is only a beginning — one voice re-learning an old song.

## Benediction

May the water be named, and may the naming oblige us. May every stream the ridge feeds recover, in word and in law, the honor the hymns once gave it. And may the song that grows quiet here continue in the reader — so that the rivers of the mountain are never again sung to only by the rain.
