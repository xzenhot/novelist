# The Hymn To The Rivers

## Question

Who sings to water now? The old seers named the rivers the ridge feeds — praised them, worshipped them, married their waters into verse. Today the same water moves in pipes and drains, nameless, unannounced. I stand where a ridge stream disappears into a culvert, and I ask: what is lost when water is no longer named — and can a poem begin the naming again?

## Oration

Recall the hymns. The seers stood where the ridge's streams gathered and called the water by name — river as goddess, rain as law, flood as grace and warning in the same word. To name a river was to honor it; to sing it was to bind a people to its source. The first poetry of this land was reverence with hydrology inside it: the ridge's gift, recognized in language, tracked from the mountain's flank down to the great river the hymns praise among their many bright-named waters. The singers knew where their water came from, and they sang to it the way one sings to a generous relative — with gratitude, with obligation, with fear of the flood years and praise for the gentle ones.

Against that music, set the modern silence. The streams run in storm drains now; the rivers are measured in quality indices and quoted in litigation; the water arrives at the tap without biography, without a name, without a face. We have not stopped depending on water — we have stopped knowing it. And what is lost is precisely what the hymns carried: the obligation. A nameless stream cannot be wronged; a goddess can. Naming was never decoration. Naming was the technology by which a civilization remembered what it owed.

So the poem does the old work in the current tongue, plainly. I name the ridge's streams — the seasonal ones, waking in the monsoon, running the old road to the plains. I name the aquifer they feed, the stepwells that once received them, the Yamuna they reach. I name the rain as the singer's teacher. The hymn will not be mistaken for the original; the original belonged to its age and its language. But the act is the same act: to honor water in language, so that honoring can return in policy, in protection, in the open channel left unpaved.

The hymn fades where this sequence ends — and the poem admits, without shame, that it is only a beginning: one voice, re-learning an old song, half-remembering the melody. The flame of the naming was never extinguished. It only waited for keepers.

## Benediction

May the water be named, and may the naming oblige us. May every stream the ridge feeds recover, in word and in law, the honor the hymns once gave it. And may the song that grows quiet here continue in the reader — so that the rivers of the mountain are never again sung to only by the rain.
