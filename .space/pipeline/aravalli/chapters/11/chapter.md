# The Ridge Elder's Story

## Question

Who keeps the names? An elder of the villages on the ridge's flank — keeper of seasons, paths, wells, and weather — sits in the shade of a neem and tells what the mountain has meant to the people who lived off it. The poet records, and asks: when the elder is gone, where does this memory go, and what does it ask of the one who receives it?

## Oration

The story comes in the elder's own order, which is not the historian's: first the monsoon paths, then the spring that failed, then the wedding that climbed the ridge to be married under the open sky, then the quarry that took the southern face, then the birds that stopped coming. Memory here is practical and sacred at once — the ridge known as a provider, a boundary, a burial ground of stories.

The elder entrusts the record: *write it down, because the young ones move to the city, and the names go with them.* The poet accepts the trust and understands its weight — this is the oral layer of the archive, the part the stone cannot hold. The chapter's handoff is a covenant: the written record must now carry what was carried by voice, and the poem that does the carrying must be exact, humble, and permanent enough to be worth the entrusting.

## Benediction

May the names of springs, paths, and seasons outlive the mouths that held them. May the elder's trust be honored by accuracy, not ornament. And may the poem that receives the oral record prove a worthy shelf — strong enough to hold what the voice could no longer carry alone.
