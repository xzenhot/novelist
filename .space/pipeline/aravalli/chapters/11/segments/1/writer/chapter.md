# The Ridge Elder's Story

## Question

Who keeps the names? An elder of the villages on the ridge's flank sits in the neem's shade — keeper of seasons, paths, wells, and weather — and tells what the mountain has meant to the people who lived off it. I record, and I ask: when the elder is gone, where does this memory go, and what does it ask of the one who receives it?

## Oration

The story comes in his order, which is not the historian's, and the poem must respect that order. First the paths: which saddle the cattle took in the dry months, which gully the monsoon turned dangerous, the shortcut the shepherds kept open by walking it. Then the spring — a seep the elders of his childhood drew from, now silted, its position known to him and to no one else living. Then the weddings that climbed the ridge in procession to be married under the open sky, because the mountain was the village's true hall. Then the quarry that took the southern face, and the years the blasting could be felt in the water pots. Then, quietly, the birds that stopped coming.

He does not moralize. He does not once say the word *heritage*. He says *we*, and he means people and mountain together — the ridge as provider, boundary, shelter, and graveyard of stories, all one word in the grammar he uses.

Then he does the thing the poem is here for. He leans forward and says: *write it down. The young ones have moved to the city; the names go with them; the spring is already hard to find.* And he entrusts the record — not to an institution, not to a file, but to the person present with a notebook, which happens to be me.

I accept, and I accept the weight that comes with it. This is the oral layer of the archive, the part the stone cannot hold: no quartzite preserves the path of the cattle or the position of a seep. Voice carried it for centuries, voice to ear, and now the carrying must pass to the page. The written record must do what the voice did — and it can only do it by being exact, humble, and permanent enough to be worth the entrusting. The inner room where these names survive is no longer the elder's memory. It is whatever we build to hold them.

## Benediction

May the names of springs, paths, and seasons outlive the mouths that held them. May the elder's trust be honored by accuracy and not by ornament. And may the poem that receives the oral record prove a worthy shelf — strong enough to hold what the voice could no longer carry alone.
