# The Monsoon Stream

## Question

Where does the rain go? In the monsoon months the ridge's channels wake — streams that run for a few weeks and vanish, feeding the aquifer and the plains on their way. I follow one such stream from its first pool to its disappearance, and I ask: what can a waterway that barely lasts a season teach a city built to last forever?

## Oration

First rain on long dust: the smell arrives before the water, petrichor rising off the ridge like a memory exhaling. Then the channel wakes. All year it was a rumor — a line of smoother stones, a greener fringe of grass, the ghost of a path between the outcrops. Now it is a voice. Water threads down the quartzite steps, gathers in the hollows, links pool to pool, and goes over the edge of the ridge laughing.

Walk with it. Under the fig roots, where the roots hold the bank like knuckles. Past the stones the stepwell-builders set to steady the flow, still doing their work six centuries after the masons left. The stream does the ridge's most urgent errand: it carries the monsoon down to where the cracked quartzite can drink it in, refilling the aquifer shelf by shelf, and beyond that, feeding the drainage that reaches the great river. Seasonal, unspectacular, faithful. It keeps its appointment every year without a contract, and then it is gone — leaving wet stone, a smell of green, and the quiet.

The city, which wants its water invisible and its land buildable, has paved many such channels into storm drains. The rain still comes; the mountain still sends it; but the old road to the river now runs under concrete, unlit and unnamed, doing a diminished version of its work. A channel paved is a stream silenced — and a ritual the mountain performed for longer than there have been cities, allowed to lapse.

Because that is what a seasonal stream is: ritual. Appearance, offering, disappearance — the ridge's oldest calendar, kept in water instead of stone. The stream's vanishing is not death but a change of garment: surface water becoming aquifer, current becoming quiet, quiet becoming the green that returns next summer to say the promise held. All the poem asks is that a few channels be left open to the sky — that the city let at least one stream run the old road, visibly, the way it always has.

## Benediction

May the seasonal waters be allowed their brief loud lives. May the open channels stay open, and the buried ones be remembered and one day reopened. And may the monsoon find, year after year, at least one stream on the ridge still running the old road to the river.
