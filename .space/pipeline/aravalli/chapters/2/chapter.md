# The Mentor's Map

## Question

How do you map a memory? The poet walks the surviving fragments of the ridge — an outcrop behind a school, a scrap of scrub between apartment blocks, the long spine near the university — marking each one on a hand-drawn survey. Every mark recalls his mentor, who spent a lifetime warning that the ridge was being quarried to death. The map fills. The mentor is gone. What, the poem asks, is a map of the mountain for — and what must it protect?

## Oration

The map is made with care, and each act of naming is immediately qualified. Here is the quartzite shoulder above the old quarry road; here the granite seam that glitters after rain; here the fold exposed where the cut slope bleeds red. But the poet insists: a map is not the mountain. It is only a promise to remember it — paper kneeling before stone, an apology written in ink.

The stone vocabulary gathers as the poem's recurring current: quartzite and granite, fold and outcrop — the ridge words that will carry the whole sequence. The mentor's voice accompanies the survey: *measure what remains, so you will know what was taken.* The map becomes a ledger of survival, drawn not to own the ridge but to stand guard over it. At the end the two worlds hold their balance — the map in the hand, the mountain on the horizon — and neither is allowed to replace the other.

## Benediction

May every line drawn on paper bow to the line of the fold it records. May the map keep faith with the mountain it cannot replace. And may whoever follows this survey inherit the discipline the mentor taught: to document without possessing, to remember without replacing.
