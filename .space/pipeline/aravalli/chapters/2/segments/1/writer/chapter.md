# The Mentor's Map

## Question

How do you map a memory? I walked the surviving fragments of the ridge with a folded survey in my pocket and a pencil: the outcrop behind the school wall, the scrap of scrub pressed between apartment towers, the long spine near the university. Each mark I made recalled my mentor, who spent his life warning that the ridge was being quarried to death. The map filled. He is gone. What is a map of the mountain for — and what must it protect?

## Oration

He taught me the discipline first, before the surveying: *measure what remains, so you will know what was taken.* So I measured. Here, the quartzite shoulder above the old quarry road, its bedding planes fanned like the spine of an opened book. Here, the granite seam that glitters after rain, coarse crystals catching low sun so that the slope briefly wears jewelry. Here, the fold exposed where a cut slope bleeds red — strata once laid flat as a floor, now standing nearly on end, pressed into that curve by weight of years I cannot say aloud without sounding naive.

I mark each fragment on the sheet, and immediately I qualify the marking, because he qualified it too: a map is not the mountain. Paper is paper. The ink line runs straight where the ridge runs its own long curve, and the pencil has never once held the weight of quartzite. A map is only a promise to remember — paper kneeling before stone, an apology written in ink and hoping to be forgiven by accuracy.

Walking the fragments, I began to feel them as limbs of one body. The northern spine, the central reserve, the southern reaches at Sanjay Van and beyond — they do not touch anymore, the city having grown between them like scar tissue, but they answer one another. The same quartzite in each, the same grain, the same folded memory. A wound to any one of them, the mentor used to say, is felt by the whole — and the city, not knowing it lives against a single creature, treats each fragment as a separate inconvenience.

That was his gift and his grief: he saw the ridge as one continuous being, and he spent his years explaining to committees that you cannot amputate a mountain in pieces and expect the remainder to stay healthy. They listened politely. Some patches were notified, some quarries closed; the losses continued elsewhere, quieter.

Now the survey is mine to keep. I write his findings into it in the margins — not his name, he would hate that — only the discipline: document without possessing, remember without replacing. At the end of the walk I stand where the last fragment looks out over the plain, map in one hand, mountain on the horizon, and I hold the two apart, carefully, the way you hold two things that must never be confused for each other.

## Benediction

May every line drawn on paper bow to the line of the fold it records. May the map keep faith with the mountain it cannot replace. And may whoever follows this survey inherit what the mentor left: the pencil, the discipline, and the love that measures because it refuses to forget.
