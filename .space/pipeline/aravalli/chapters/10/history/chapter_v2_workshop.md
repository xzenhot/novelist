# The Public Hearing

## Question

Can a mountain have a hearing? In a room of laminated tables and numbered agendas, a planning body takes up the fate of the last unprotected ridge land. Surveys are read, objections filed, objections answered. The poet, seated among the citizens, asks: what would it mean for the ridge to be represented here — not as acreage, but as the oldest continuous witness the city has?

## Oration

The poem follows the procedure: the developer's projection, the hydrologist's map of the aquifer, the historian's one page on the quarry, a retired teacher reading the mentor's old survey into the record. Argument meets argument. Then, in the poem's turning, the evidence assembled across the sequence — the hand-axe, the altar, the well, the wall, the losses — is entered as a single exhibit, one continuous record too layered to be replaced.

And the ridge is reprieved: boundaries drawn, land notified, the quarry case closed. The poem refuses triumph. A reprieve is not a resurrection; what was lost stays lost, and the protection is only as durable as the memory that sustains it. The mountain was not present in the room. It was spoken for — by the teacher, the maps, the poet — and the poem understands that this is the arrangement now: the ridge's voice is a trust, held by whoever has learned to read it.

## Benediction

May every hearing keep the record open and the evidence whole. May those who speak for the mountain speak accurately, without decoration, in the ridge's own sober register. And may the reprieve become a habit of the city — so that the mountain is never again decided about in its absence.
