## Chapter 1 — The Oldest Stone

- The Delhi Ridge is the northern tail of the Aravalli range, among the oldest fold mountain systems on Earth, with rocks dated to roughly two billion years (Proterozoic quartzite of the Delhi Supergroup).
- Quartzite hand-axes andflake tools attributed to the Paleolithic (Acheulian and later industries) have been found on and around the ridge; Anangpur and surrounding areas of the Faridabad/Delhi belt are known Paleolithic localities.
- The ridge's surviving fragments: the Northern/Central Ridge reserve forest and scattered southern outcrops (Sanjay Van, Jahanpanah, Satbari–Chattarpur belt), much reduced from its historic extent.
- Images available: hand-axe edge, fold lines in exposed quartzite, dust, kikar and dhau trees, the flyover traffic audible from outcrops.

## Chapter 2 — The Mentor's Map

- Ridge fragments survive as discontinuous patches: Northern Ridge (Kamla Nehru Ridge), Central Ridge, South-Central Ridge (Sanjay Van, Sanjay Lake to Chattarpur), separated by built-up city.
- Citizen and scientific surveys of the ridge (geological, ecological, and heritage surveys) have repeatedly documented encroachment and quarrying losses; the notion of a "map of what remains" reflects real survey practice.
- The mentor figure is poetic invention, but the warning literature it channels is real: decades of conservationist advocacy for the ridge (including litigation in the 1990s that led to notified forest status for large sections).
- Images available: hand-drawn survey, outcrop behind a school wall, granite seam glinting after rain, cut slopes bleeding red soil.

## Chapter 3 — The Prehistoric Hunters

- Paleolithic communities in the Delhi region used local quartzite for tools; knapping floors near raw material sources (outcrops and stream gravels) are archaeologically attested.
- The prehistoric climate of the region alternated through glacial-interglacial cycles; streams from the ridge fed into the Yamuna's shifting channels.
- Hand-axe making: hammer-stone percussion, platform preparation, edge finishing; a finished Acheulian-style hand-axe represents many minutes of skilled work and a mental template — craft, not just utility.
- Images available: dawn camp, flakes falling like decisions, stream gone underground, tool cache in leaf litter.

## Chapter 4 — The Vedic Fire

- The Vedic corpus (c. 1500–500 BCE, dating debated) hymns rivers — notably the Nadistuti sukta (Rigveda 10.75) praising rivers including the Yamuna — and centers fire (Agni) as messenger and witness.
- Fire-altars (vedi) built of brick or stone; forest hermitages (ashramas) as settings for chant and teaching are a continuous Indian ideal, and the Delhi region lies within the wider Vedic cultural sphere.
- The Aravalli's forests and streams would have fed seasonal rivers eastward toward the Yamuna; the "hymns to the rivers the ridge feeds" is grounded in this hydrology.
- Images available: altar of local stone, dawn feeding of the fire, chant through sal/dhau trees, smoke rising straight in still air.

## Chapter 5 — The Stepwell

- Stepwells (baoli/baoli, vav) are characteristic of the Delhi region and western India; surviving examples associated with the ridge include Ugrasen ki Baoli, Rajon ki Baoli, and Gandhak ki Baoli, built in the Delhi Sultanate period (14th–16th centuries) though the type is older.
- Stepwells caught runoff and tapped shallow aquifers; galleries and steps gave access as the water level fell seasonally — architecture tuned to the monsoon's rhythm.
- Ridge quartzite was the common building material of Sultanate Delhi (walls, gates, baolis), making the stepwell literally built of the mountain it drew water from.
- Images available: descending tiers, green light on the water pool, women at the well-edge, dripping echo, pigeons in the galleries.
