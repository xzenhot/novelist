# The Bhat Flower Weeps

And it is said that the bhat flower, the humble flower of the riverbank, wept upon her foot. It is said that the earth itself, which had watched her long voyage in silence, could no longer keep its silence, and that the small white flower that grows where the water meets the land bent its head and let fall its tears upon the foot of the woman who had walked the road between the living and the dead.

Why should a flower weep? The world asks this, and the world does not understand. But the flower understood what the world could not. It understood that it had witnessed something that nature itself must honour — a devotion so complete that it had moved heaven, a love so true that even the gods had bowed. And the flower, which asks for nothing and is given nothing, which blooms and fades in a single season and is forgotten, knew that it was standing in the presence of the one thing that does not fade.

For the bhat flower is the earth's own witness. It is the small, unregarded thing that sees what the great and the powerful do not see. It had watched the serpent strike, and the widow refuse, and the raft set out, and the river fight, and the world mock, and the gods listen. And now, at the end of all of it, it wept — not from sorrow, but from the overwhelming recognition of a love that had proven itself stronger than death.

And so the tears of a flower became the earth's own answer to a woman's devotion. The sky had thundered, and the gods had spoken, and the king of heaven had bowed. But it was the small white flower on the riverbank that said, at last, what all of them had been trying to say: that this woman's love was not a small thing, and that the earth itself was moved by it.

For nature does not weep for the ordinary. It weeps only for the sacred. And Behula, standing on the bank with her husband restored, was sacred in a way that no temple could contain.
