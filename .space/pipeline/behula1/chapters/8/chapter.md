# The Bhat Flower Weeps

And it is said that the small flower in the pot by the hospital gate wept upon her foot. It is said that the earth itself, which had watched her long journey in silence, could no longer keep its silence, and that the little white flower that grows where the concrete meets the soil bent its head and let fall its tears upon the foot of the woman who had walked the road between the living and the dying.

Why should a flower weep? The world asks this, and the world does not understand. But the flower understood what the world could not. It understood that it had witnessed something that nature itself must honour — a devotion so complete that it had moved the ward, a love so true that even the powerful had bowed. And the flower, which asks for nothing and is given nothing, which blooms and fades in a single season and is forgotten, knew that it was standing in the presence of the one thing that does not fade.

For the little flower is the earth's own witness. It is the small, unregarded thing that sees what the great and the powerful do not see. It had watched the fever strike, and the housewife refuse, and the journey begin, and the city fight, and the world stare, and the doctors listen. And now, at the end of all of it, it wept — not from sorrow, but from the overwhelming recognition of a love that had proven itself stronger than the sickness.

And so the tears of a flower became the earth's own answer to a woman's devotion. The monitors had beeped, and the doctors had spoken, and the head of the ward had bowed. But it was the small white flower by the gate that said, at last, what all of them had been trying to say: that this woman's love was not a small thing, and that the earth itself was moved by it.

For nature does not weep for the ordinary. It weeps only for the sacred. And Behula, standing at the gate with her husband still fighting, was sacred in a way that no ward could contain.
