# Indra's Honour

And then the head doctor rose from his chair, and the ward fell silent, for when the one who holds the chart rises, even the monitors hold their breath. He looked upon the woman who had carried a dying man through the streets, and he saw in her something he had not expected to find in a housewife: a devotion that did not ask for reward, a love that did not count its cost. And he was moved, not by her tears and not by her pleading, but by her character — by the unbroken line of a will that had refused to bend before the sickness itself.

And so he granted her what she had not dared to ask for, and what she had never once demanded: a bed, a ventilator, a chance. And in the granting of it, the ward itself bowed, for the doctors understood that they were not rewarding a woman; they were acknowledging a truth. Her sacrifice had not been a transaction, a coin paid to purchase a miracle. It had been a gift, given freely, and a gift given freely is the one thing even the powerful cannot repay.

Behula did not fall to her knees in gratitude, for she had not come to be granted anything. She had come to be heard. And now that she was heard, she stood in the ward as a woman who had won not a prize but a recognition — the recognition that her love was real, that her fear was not madness, that the road she had walked was not a road of folly but a road of truth.

And the doctors looked at one another, and they understood that something had changed in the order of things. A housewife had argued with the sickness, and the sickness had listened. A woman had refused the silence, and the ward had answered. And the head doctor, who had seen so many come and go, had been taught something new by a woman with a few papers: that the greatest power in the world is not the machine, but the love that will not let go.

For her sacrifice was for love, not for reward. And yet the ward itself bowed, because even the powerful must bow to a love that is true.
