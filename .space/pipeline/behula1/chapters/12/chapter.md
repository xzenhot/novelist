# The Price of Medicine

The medicine had a price, and the price was not written on the bottle. It was written in the things Behula sold, one by one, in the quiet of the afternoons when no one was watching. First the gold bangles her mother had given her, the ones that had never left her wrists since her wedding. Then the small savings, the money that had been meant for a future that was now slipping away. Then the things that had no price at all, and so cost the most.

She learned to bargain with the world, and the world bargained back with a cruelty that wore a smile. The chemist who had the medicine she needed looked at her and named a number that was not a number but a door, and behind the door was everything she had. She paid it, and she did not weep, because weeping was a luxury she could no longer afford. She paid it, and she walked out with the medicine, and she held it against her chest the way she had once held her husband.

And there were those who offered to help, and their help was a trap. A man who said he could get the medicine cheaper, if only she would meet him, if only she would be kind to him, if only she would understand what a woman alone must give. She understood, and she refused, and she walked away with her dignity intact and her medicine still unpaid for, and she learned that the world's kindness is often only a loan with a terrible interest.

But she did not break. She found the money where she could, honestly, painfully, coin by coin, and she learned that a woman who has nothing can still be rich in the one thing the world cannot take: the refusal to be bought. The medicine had a price, and she paid it, but she did not pay it with her soul. And that, she understood, was the only price that mattered.

For the world will always ask a woman to pay for love with her body, her silence, her shame. But Behula had learned, in the long dark of the ward, that love is not a debt to be collected. It is a gift to be given, and a gift given freely cannot be bought, and cannot be sold, and cannot be taken. And so she paid for the medicine, and she kept her soul, and she walked on.
