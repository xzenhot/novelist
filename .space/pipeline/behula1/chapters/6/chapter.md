# The Court of the Gods

At last the streets brought her to the place where the queues end and the glass begins, and she stood before the doctors, a housewife in a hall of white coats, a woman in a place where death itself was only a number on a chart. And she did not kneel in fear, for she had already knelt to nothing but her own love, and love had made her fearless.

She laid her case before them as a woman lays a burden down, and her case was simple. She had not come to bargain, and she had not come to beg. She had come to say what no one in that hall had ever heard a housewife say: that a woman's devotion is not a small thing, and that it should not be weighed on the same scales as money or rank or the patience of the powerful. She had come to ask the doctors to look at what she had carried through the streets, and to tell her whether love, when it is true, is not itself a kind of medicine.

And the doctors listened, and they were troubled, for they had grown used to families who came with money and with lowered eyes, and here was a woman who came with nothing but a sick man and a vow, and who looked at them as an equal looks at an equal. And in that moment the hospital became a place where a woman's love was finally heard, and the hearing of it shook the ward.

For she had done what no protocol had anticipated. She had refused the order of things. She had taken the verdict of the sickness and carried it to the very seat of the ward, and she had laid it down and said, This is not the end. This is not justice. And the doctors, who had thought themselves beyond surprise, found themselves surprised by a woman who would not accept their silence.

And so the question hung in the air of that ward, heavier than any monitor's beep: what is a housewife's devotion worth, when it is weighed against the decree of the sickness? And for the first time, the doctors did not know the answer.
