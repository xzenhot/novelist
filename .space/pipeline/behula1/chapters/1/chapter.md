# The Wedding Night

On the night that should have been ordinary, the fever came. It did not announce itself with thunder or with the breaking of doors; it came as a cough that would not stop, a breath that grew shallow in the dark, a number on a thermometer that climbed while the city slept. Behula had been a wife for many years, and already the world was teaching her the first and hardest of its lessons: that love is not a promise the body can keep.

She had been told that marriage was a fortress, a house with walls of stone and a roof against the rain. She had been told that a woman's life was a room she entered once and never left. But the walls she had been given were falling now, one breath at a time, and the roof she had trusted was letting the dark pour through. And in that falling she learned what no one had taught her: that the only citadel that cannot be breached is the one a woman builds inside herself, where fear may knock but cannot enter, where the memory of a beloved face is kept like a lamp that no wind can reach.

The house filled with the sound of his breathing, ragged and wrong. The lamps burned low. The man who had been the only earner, the only pillar, lay now in the bed they had shared, and the machine of his lungs was failing, eaten by the sickness that had swept the world. But Behula did not weep as the others wept. She sat very still, and in the stillness she was building something — a room within the room, a wall within the wall, a silence that was not the silence of the dying but the silence of a woman gathering her strength.

For she understood, even then, that the world would ask her to accept the end, to let her own life be lowered into the same grave as his. And she understood, with a clarity that frightened her, that she would refuse. The sickness had taken his breath; it would not be given her life as well. The night had taken her husband toward the dark; it would not be given her as well.

And so, in the darkest hour, when the house slept and the neighbours had gone, Behula rose and looked at the man she loved, and she made a vow that no one heard but the city, which was already listening. She would not let death have the last word. She would carry love itself through the streets, and if the doctors would not hear her, she would make them hear.

This was the first night of her long defiance, though she did not yet know how long the road would be. She knew only that a woman's devotion is not a thing that dies with the body, and that the heart, once it has loved, becomes a fortress that even fate cannot enter.
