# The Wedding Night

On the night that should have been the beginning, the serpent came. It did not announce itself with thunder or with the breaking of doors; it came as a shadow moves across a wall when a lamp is lowered, silent, certain, and already gone before the eye could name it. Behula had been a bride for a single evening, and already the world was teaching her the first and hardest of its lessons: that love is not a promise the body can keep.

She had been told that marriage was a fortress, a house with walls of stone and a roof against the rain. She had been told that a woman's life was a room she entered once and never left. But the walls she had been given fell in a single night, and the roof she had trusted let the dark pour through. And in that falling she learned what no one had taught her: that the only citadel that cannot be breached is the one a woman builds inside herself, where grief may knock but cannot enter, where the memory of a beloved face is kept like a lamp that no wind can reach.

The house filled with weeping. The lamps burned low. The garland that had been laid around her neck in the morning lay now like a noose upon the floor, and the flowers that had been woven for joy were already learning the shape of mourning. But Behula did not weep as the others wept. She sat very still, and in the stillness she was building something — a room within the room, a wall within the wall, a silence that was not the silence of the dead but the silence of a woman gathering her strength.

For she understood, even then, that the world would ask her to be buried beside her husband, to let her own life be lowered into the same earth. And she understood, with a clarity that frightened her, that she would refuse. The serpent had taken one life; it would not be given two. The night had taken her husband; it would not be given her as well.

And so, in the darkest hour, when the house slept and the mourners had gone, Behula rose and looked at the body of the man she loved, and she made a vow that no one heard but the river, which was already listening. She would not let death have the last word. She would carry love itself across the water, and if the gods would not hear her, she would make them hear.

This was the first night of her long defiance, though she did not yet know how long the road would be. She knew only that a woman's devotion is not a thing that dies with the body, and that the heart, once it has loved, becomes a fortress that even fate cannot enter.
