# The Spark in Dhow Lane

## Workshop

The Archivist passed the Young Correspondent a photograph: a charred tanker hull listing against the haze of the Strait of Hormuz, taken from a helicopter gunner's phone.

"This is not the beginning," the Archivist said. "It is the spark after a long drought."

"It looks like the beginning," the correspondent replied. "Smoke, warships, headlines."

"Yes, but the fire had already been laid. What you see here is the moment a hundred silent decisions caught light at once."

She tapped the image.

"Look at the waterline. That is not a torpedo. That is precision. Someone wanted the ship to block the channel, not sink prettily."

## Story

Commander Sarah Lin reached the CIC of the USS Truxtun at 0634, before the first rumours had crossed the Atlantic. Her uniform was still damp from a shower that had lasted ninety seconds. Around her, sailors moved in the controlled hurry of a crew that had practised this drill a hundred times and now feared it was no longer a drill.

"Situation," she said.

The TAO—a lieutenant commander with a beard he had been ordered to shave—pointed at the large-screen display. "Panama Crown, eighty thousand tonnes crude, rudder blown, aground here." He touched the strait's narrow throat. "Two dozen vessels drifting in the traffic separation scheme. Iranian fast-attack craft outbound from Bandar Abbas. Coalition helo warned one off the bow of the Truxtun. No shots, yet."

"Yet," Lin repeated.

She climbed to the bridge wing. The morning air was warm and smelled of fuel. To port, the horizon had turned the colour of rust: the oil slick was larger than she had imagined. A dhow named *Al-Noor*, its hold stacked with air conditioners, had stopped its engine and begun to drift broadside. The boy who had first pointed at the drone sat on the bow, hugging his knees.

Lin thought of her father, who had been a fisherman in Kaohsiung. He used to say the sea did not take sides; it only punished the careless. Today the sea had been made into a weapon.

"Get me a line to Fifth Fleet," she said. "And launch the Seahawk. I want eyes on that dhow and on every boat leaving Qeshm."

Across the strait, Captain Reza Sharifi stood in the command centre on Qeshm Island and listened to the static of too many radios speaking at once. He was a man who had survived the Iran-Iraq war by understanding that not every order deserved obedience. When a major from the Revolutionary Guard insisted on sending twelve fast-attack boats to "investigate" the tanker, Sharifi turned to him slowly.

"Major, if those boats approach a destroyer at speed, the Americans will fire. And then we will have a war before Tehran has finished its breakfast."

"The Americans are the ones who will start it."

"No. The first shot is a political decision. The second shot is an accident. Let us not give them the accident."

But the major was already on the phone with Tehran, and the boats were leaving the jetty.

Sharifi walked outside. The sun had burned away the haze, and he could see the *Panama Crown* clearly now, a black scar on the copper sea. He thought of his son, of his wife's grave in Shiraz, of the verses of Hafez he had memorised as a boy and had stopped reciting because they no longer seemed true.

At 0900 the first real skirmish began. A speedboat—perhaps responding to an order, perhaps to its commander's ambition—broke from the loose Iranian formation and raced toward the Truxtun. The Seahawk hovered ahead, loudspeakers broadcasting warnings in Farsi and English. The boat did not turn.

Lin stood on the bridge and watched through binoculars. She could see the driver, a young man in an orange life vest, hunched over the wheel.

"Warning shots," she ordered. "Across the bow."

The Seahawk's door gun fired three short bursts. The water in front of the speedboat fountained white. The boat swerved, its engine screaming. One of the crew stood up and raised a camera, photographing the Truxtun before they turned back.

No blood. Not this time.

But in Tehran, the photograph was already being labelled *American aggression in the Persian Gulf*. In Washington, the speedboat's approach was being called *Iranian provocation*. In London, Maya Thorne watched both versions on split screens and understood that the truth had stopped mattering hours ago.

By noon, the *Panama Crown* had begun to list further. The captain, a Filipino named Del Rosario, refused evacuation. "I am responsible for the oil," he said over VHF. "If she breaks, I break with her."

Lin admired that. She also knew it was a kind of suicide.

She wrote in her log: *The spark has landed in dry grass. We are watching which way the wind will blow.*

## Discussion

"No one fired a killing shot," the Young Correspondent said. "And still the world tilted."

"Because a photograph is faster than a bullet. Because a lie fits the screen better than a truth."

"What about the tanker captain? He stayed."

"Yes. Some people stay with the fire they did not start. That is one kind of courage. The other kind—the harder kind—is to keep asking who lit it."

The Archivist opened a folder.

"Tomorrow we follow the money. Oil is the next drum."
