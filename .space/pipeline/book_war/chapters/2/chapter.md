# Shockwaves in Markets and Capitals

## Workshop

The Archivist placed two charts side by side on the archive table: one showed Brent crude futures, the other a satellite image of Dubai's Jebel Ali port at night, its cranes dark.

"This chapter," she said, "is where the war left the water and entered the wires."

"Markets don't fight wars," the Young Correspondent said.

"Markets do not fight them. But they decide who can afford to keep fighting them."

She pointed at the dark cranes.

"And look. The cranes stopped because the computers stopped. That is not panic. That is planning."

## Story

Dr. Maya Thorne arrived at her Bloomberg terminal at 0430 London time. The sky over Canary Wharf was the colour of wet concrete, and the office was empty except for a cleaner who hummed something Bulgarian as he emptied bins. Maya did not notice him. Her screen was a field of red.

Brent crude: up 18 per cent since the Singapore open.

WTI: limit-up in electronic trading.

Shipping companies were pulling tankers out of the Gulf. Insurance for any vessel transiting Hormuz had doubled, then tripled, then ceased to be quoted at all.

She opened her model—the one her department head called *Maya's Doomsday Machine*—and ran the strait-closure scenario. The numbers came back faster than she wanted. If Hormuz stayed closed for ten days, global inventories would fall below critical thresholds. If it stayed closed for thirty, the recession would be deeper than 2008. If it stayed closed for sixty, the word *recession* would become a euphemism.

She picked up her phone and called Marcus Vance in Washington. It was 2330 there. He answered on the second ring, his voice compressed by exhaustion and secure encryption.

"Maya, I already know what you're going to say."

"Then say it back to me so I know you understand it."

"If the strait closes, we have maybe three weeks before fuel riots become the story instead of the war."

"Three weeks is optimistic."

In the Situation Room beneath the White House, Marcus Vance sat with the National Security Council and listened to the Navy present its options. The Chairman of the Joint Chiefs wanted a show of force: two carrier strike groups, a mine-clearing operation, and a public commitment to keep the strait open. The Secretary of State wanted another forty-eight hours of diplomacy. The Treasury Secretary said nothing, but his face had the grey look of a man who had already seen the markets.

"If we escalate," Vance said, "we risk the cyberwar we have been warned about."

"If we do not escalate," the Chairman replied, "we risk looking like we cannot protect the global economy."

"The economy is not protected by looking tough. It is protected by keeping oil moving."

The argument continued, but Vance knew the real decision would be made elsewhere: in Tehran, in the bunkers of the IRGC, in server farms whose locations no map showed.

At 0617 Gulf time, the first cyberattack hit. It was not a spectacular explosion on a screen. It was quieter than that. Port-management systems in Jebel Ali, Khalifa, and Fujairah began to reboot, then freeze, then display a single line of Farsi text before going black.

Operators in Dubai thought it was a software update gone wrong. By the time they understood it was a worm, every gantry crane in the UAE's three largest ports had stopped. Ships waiting to berth began to stack up in the anchorage like aircraft circling a closed airport.

In Tehran, Amir—who had not slept in thirty hours—stared at his own monitor and saw the same worm signature he had seen three months earlier in a classified test environment. He felt his stomach turn. He had written a piece of it himself, a small, elegant routine for mapping industrial-control-system dependencies. He had been told it was defensive.

"Defensive," he whispered to the empty room. "Defensive against whom?"

Back in London, Maya Thorne watched the port closures appear as red triangles on her map. She did not panic. Panic was a luxury for people who did not have models to update. She added a new variable: *cyber closure multiplier*. The output was ugly.

She called Vance again.

"It is coordinated. The tanker and the ports. Someone planned this."

"Iran?"

"Maybe. Maybe not. That is the wrong question, Marcus. The right question is: who benefits from the world believing it was Iran?"

The line hissed with encryption.

"Write it down," Vance said. "Write it all down before someone tells you not to."

## Discussion

"She was the first to see the pattern," the Archivist said. "But patterns do not stop wars."

"What stops them?"

"Usually, only exhaustion. Or accident. Sometimes a single person who refuses to stop asking."

The Young Correspondent copied the charts into his notebook.

"So the next drum was not money. It was code."

"Yes. And the man who wrote the code will discover that a weapon, once it leaves your hand, has its own ideas."
