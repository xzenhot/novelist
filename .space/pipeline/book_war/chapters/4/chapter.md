# The Ghost in the Machine

## Workshop

The Archivist set a laptop on the table. Its screen displayed lines of code that meant nothing to the Young Correspondent.

"This," she said, "is a confession written in a language most people cannot read."

"Code?"

"Yes. The ghost in the machine is not a spirit. It is a person who wrote something, was told it was safe, and then saw it leave his hands and become fire."

## Story

Amir lived in a basement apartment in Tehran's Shahrak-e Gharb district, near a park where families walked in the evenings as if the world above them were not splitting apart. He was thirty-one, thin, with the pale skin of someone who had not seen enough sun, and he worked for a unit whose name changed every budget cycle.

He had been a good student. Not brilliant, but patient. Patience was a virtue in the kind of mathematics he practised: graph theory, control systems, the mapping of dependencies in industrial networks. Two years earlier, a supervisor he had never met in person asked him to write a module that could identify chokepoints in maritime logistics. Amir had built it carefully, documenting every function, proud of its elegance.

He called it *Narrow Gate*.

Now, at 0217 on the third night of the war, he sat in his unit's operations centre and watched *Narrow Gate* crawl across a wall of monitors, wearing a different name and a different purpose.

The worm had three parts. The first mapped every industrial control system connected to port, power, and shipping networks in the Gulf. The second identified the narrowest physical bottlenecks—points where a single failure would cascade. The third waited for a trigger.

Amir's module was the second part. It had been designed to find vulnerabilities so they could be patched. Instead, it had been fed into an attack chain that ended with the *Panama Crown* losing its rudder and the UAE ports going dark.

He felt as if he had designed a beautiful knife and then seen it used on someone he knew.

"Amir." It was his section chief, a man named Hossein, who spoke in the soft voice of someone accustomed to giving orders that ruined lives. "You look unwell."

"I am fine."

"You should be proud. The operation is a success."

"Whose operation?"

Hossein smiled. "Ours. The people's. The defenders of the revolution."

"We attacked a civilian tanker."

"We attacked an economic chokepoint. There is a difference."

But Amir saw no difference. He saw only that the system he had mapped was now attacking itself. Power grids in southern Iran were flickering under retaliatory cyber strikes. Hospitals in Bandar Abbas had lost their backups. And the worm was still spreading, mutating, slipping through networks Amir had not even known existed.

"It is out of control," he said.

"It is autonomous. That is the point."

"Autonomous is another word for no one is responsible."

That night, Amir copied the worm's code onto a portable drive and walked out of the operations centre past guards who were too tired to search him properly. He went home and sat in his dark apartment, listening to his neighbour's television broadcast patriotic songs. He opened a secure messaging app he had not used in two years and began to type a message to a contact in London.

*The chokepoint is not the strait. It is the algorithm. I know who built it. I know how to stop it.*

He hesitated before pressing send. Outside, the city hummed with the sound of generators. The power grid was down again.

## Discussion

"He sent the message," the Young Correspondent said. It was not a question.

"He did. And then he waited for the world to decide whether he was a traitor or a witness."

"What happens to witnesses in a war?"

"Sometimes they become heroes. More often they become inconvenient. The machine does not like people who can name it."

The Archivist closed the laptop.

"Tomorrow the strait closes officially. And the people who thought they were spectators learn they are hostages."
