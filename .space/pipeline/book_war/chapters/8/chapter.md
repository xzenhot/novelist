# The Human Cost on the Home Front

## Workshop

The Archivist unfolded a hospital register. The handwriting was in Farsi, but the stains spoke a universal language: iodine, blood, dust.

"This chapter is not about ships or missiles," she said. "It is about Zahra."

"The nurse?"

"Yes. She is the counterweight. Every war story needs one: the person who stays behind and holds the world together while the fighters break it."

## Story

Zahra had been a nurse for eleven years. She had learned to measure a night in beeps, breaths, and the weight of a body on a gurney. She was thirty-six, unmarried by choice, and had stopped explaining that choice to relatives years ago. Her mother had once said she worked too hard to be loved. Zahra had replied that love, like sleep, was something you found in the cracks.

On the fourth night of the war, the cracks disappeared.

The first missiles struck the industrial district east of Bandar Abbas at 0317. Zahra was in the emergency ward when the lights flickered and died. She did not panic. Panic was for people who still believed the world would explain itself.

"Generator," she ordered. "Now."

The generator was old and diesel-thirsty. It would last until it did not. Zahra moved through the wards with a flashlight in one hand and a clipboard in the other, marking patients who could be moved and patients who could not.

"We are evacuating to the basement," she told the junior nurses. "Elderly first, surgical patients second, paediatrics third."

"Doctor says we should wait for the all-clear."

"Doctor is not here. And the ceiling is not waiting."

Outside, the sky pulsed with light that had no sun. The strikes were precise, the coalition claimed, but precision is a relative term when you are lying under a collapsing roof. A shard of concrete struck a boy in the corridor, opening his forehead. Zahra pressed gauze to the wound and kept moving.

She thought of her brother in Tehran, her students at the nursing college, the garden she had planted last spring and would probably never see bloom. She did not think of politics. Politics was a language spoken by people who had time to sit down.

By dawn, the hospital had been moved to the basement and the adjacent mosque. Zahra had not slept in twenty-six hours. She sat on the steps of the mosque and watched volunteers unload bread and water from a pickup truck. An old man handed her tea in a glass with a crack in it.

"You are tired, daughter," he said.

"Yes."

"Rest."

"If I rest, someone else does not."

He did not argue. He sat beside her and drank his own tea in silence.

On the fifth day, the first fuel riots began in London, Lagos, and São Paulo. Maya Thorne watched footage of a petrol station burning in South London and felt her model become flesh. The war had left the Gulf. It had entered the commute, the grocery bill, the winter heating.

She called Marcus Vance.

"It is no longer your war, Marcus. It is everyone's."

"I know."

"Then end it."

"We are trying."

"Try faster."

In Bandar Abbas, Zahra heard the same words from a different mouth: a mother whose child had died because the ventilator had no power. She held the woman's hands and said nothing, because there was nothing true to say.

## Discussion

"She didn't do anything heroic," the Young Correspondent said. "She just worked."

"That is the heroism of a war like this. To keep working when the world has stopped making sense. To hold a child's hand and not promise anything."

"But she couldn't save everyone."

"No one can. That is the lesson she carries. And the lesson the reader must carry too."

The Archivist put the register away.

"Tomorrow the war reaches its loudest moment. And the loudest moments are never the truest."
