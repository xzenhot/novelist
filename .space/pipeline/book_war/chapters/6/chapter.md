# Escalation Dominance

## Workshop

The Archivist placed a flight helmet on the table. It was scuffed, with a patch that read *VFA-143* and a name tape: *MORALES*.

"This chapter," she said, "belongs to the pilots."

"Fighter pilots?" the Young Correspondent asked. "I thought this was a naval war."

"It was never one kind of war. It was always every kind at once. The pilot is simply the next lens."

## Story

Lieutenant Commander Juan "Viper" Morales had not slept more than four hours in three days. He was thirty-four, the son of a bus driver from San Antonio, and he had wanted to fly since he was seven. Now he sat in the ready room of the USS Nimitz, watching a PowerPoint briefing that reduced a country to targets: radar sites, missile batteries, command bunkers, power stations.

Operation Open Gate had begun.

The mission was suppression of enemy air defences: SEAD. Morales would lead a four-ship of F/A-18E Super Hornets into the Iranian coast, mapping radar emissions, destroying missile sites that threatened the mine-clearing force, and avoiding anything that looked like a civilian structure. It was the kind of mission that sounded surgical until you were inside it.

At 0445, the first wave launched. The deck of the Nimitz was a cathedral of violence: steam, noise, and light. Morales felt the catapult slam him back into his seat, and then the night swallowed him and his wingman, Lieutenant Rachel Okafor, call sign *Storm*.

They flew low, below the Iranian radar horizon, the Gulf black beneath them. Morales's helmet display glowed with waypoints, threat rings, fuel numbers. He thought of his daughter, who had sent him a drawing of an airplane before he deployed. She had given it six wings and a smile.

"Viper, Storm. Coming up on waypoint Bravo."

"Copy. Go gate."

They climbed. The Iranian radar, an old S-200 site with a modern upgrade, lit them up instantly. The cockpit screamed with warning tones. Morales fired an AGM-88 HARM and broke left. The missile traced the radar beam home.

The first explosion was clean, a fireball on a hilltop south of Bandar Abbas. Morales allowed himself one breath of satisfaction. Then the second site opened fire.

"Viper, SAM inbound!"

He saw the trail of smoke rising from the coast, impossibly fast, impossibly patient. He called out evasive manoeuvres, but the missile was a newer model, with a logic of its own. It ignored the first decoy, the second, and locked on Okafor's aircraft.

"Mayday! Mayday! Storm hit! Ejecting!"

The radio filled with static and the sound of wind. Then a parachute beacon appeared on Morales's display, drifting toward the Iranian coast.

He circled once, twice, fighting every instinct to fly lower. The rescue chain was already moving—he could hear the CSAR commander on another frequency—but the sun was rising, and the coast below was waking up. Iranian security forces would reach Okafor before any helicopter could.

Unless someone already on the ground got there first.

Morales climbed back to altitude, his jaw aching from clenching it. He had completed the mission. The anti-ship missile site was destroyed. The mine-clearing force was safer. And his wingman was alone in enemy territory because he had told her to climb.

He would carry that fact with him for the rest of his life.

## Discussion

"He blamed himself," the Young Correspondent said.

"Pilots always do. The logic of command is that you send people into danger, and sometimes the danger answers."

"Was the mission worth it?"

"That is the wrong question. The right question is whether anyone asked if there was another mission that did not require the question to be asked at all."

The Archivist put the helmet away.

"Tomorrow we go after the pilot. The SEALs do not leave people behind, even when the math says they should."
