# Asymmetric Shadows

## Workshop

The Archivist unrolled a nautical chart across the table. The Strait of Hormuz was a thin blue corridor between masses of brown land, crowded with red pins: warships, tankers, dhows, oil rigs.

"Do you know what asymmetry means in war?" she asked.

"The weaker side finds a cheaper way to hurt the stronger side," the Young Correspondent said.

"Yes. But it also means the stronger side must decide whether to risk its expensive ships against an enemy that costs almost nothing. That decision is where wars become long."

## Story

The minesweepers arrived on the second day: four small wooden-hulled ships from the UK and the United States, painted in colours that made them look like fishing boats. Their crews knew the work. Most had done it in the Gulf before, sweeping for Iranian mines in the Tanker War of the 1980s, when the world was younger and the explosives older.

Commander Lin watched them approach from the Truxtun's bridge. The strait had become a strange sea: traffic stopped, warships from three nations holding station like chess pieces that had forgotten the opening, and the *Panama Crown* still burning, now a black island at the channel's narrowest point.

"Message from the flagship," a signalsman said. "Mine countermeasures commence at 1400. Truxtun to provide air and surface cover."

Lin nodded. She understood the theatre of it. Four small sweepers, defended by a billion-dollar destroyer, against an enemy that could attack with a hundred-dollar fishing boat packed with explosives. That was the definition of a world out of balance.

At 1350, the first Iranian speedboats appeared.

They came in a loose swarm, twenty or thirty of them, darting between the anchored merchant ships like wasps among cattle. Their tactic was simple: overwhelm the defence by presenting too many targets at once. Against a sophisticated radar, a speedboat is almost nothing—until it is close enough to be everything.

"Vampire contacts, bearing one-four-zero, multiple inbound," the TAO announced.

Lin felt the old cold behind her ribs. "Warn them off. Helicopters airborne. CIWS manned."

Across the water, Captain Sharifi stood on the shore command post and watched the swarm through binoculars. His order to hold position had been countermanded by Tehran. Now the boats were his, but he did not control them. Each commander was a young man with adrenaline and a radio, interpreting courage as aggression.

"God help them," Sharifi said.

"Sir?" his aide asked.

"The boys in the boats. They think they are hunting a ship. They do not know they are being hunted by history."

The Truxtun's Seahawk fired warning shots. The boats scattered, regrouped, and pressed closer. Then, from a concrete bunker near Bandar Abbas, a coastal anti-ship missile rose on a column of white flame.

"Inbound! Incoming!"

Lin did not shout. She had trained for this since she was twenty-three. "Chaff and decoys. Hard turn starboard. Engage with SM-2."

The destroyer heeled. The missile, an old Chinese-designed Silkworm with a new guidance package, tracked the turn for two seconds too long and splashed into the sea two hundred metres astern. The explosion threw a column of water high enough to drench the bridge wing.

One of the speedboats, confused by the missile launch, turned toward the minesweepers. A British commander on the lead sweeper raised his loudhailer and warned in Farsi. The boat slowed, then sped away.

No one died that afternoon. But the rules had changed. The strait was no longer a shipping lane. It was a contested corridor where any hull could become a weapon and any radar blip could become a coffin.

That night, Lin sat in her cabin and wrote: *We are fighting shadows with a searchlight. The shadows multiply.*

## Discussion

"So nobody won," the Young Correspondent said.

"There are no quick wins in asymmetry. The side with the expensive ship must be patient. The side with the cheap boat must be lucky. Both run out of patience and luck eventually."

"And the mines?"

"Still in the water. Waiting for the next chapter."

The Archivist rolled up the chart.

"Tomorrow we enter the machine itself. The war has a ghost, and it is wearing a programmer's face."
