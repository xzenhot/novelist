# Behind Enemy Lines

## Workshop

The Archivist opened a waterproof notebook. Its pages were warped by salt water, and the handwriting was compact, as if written in a hurry.

"This belonged to Master Chief Miller," she said. "It is a record of a rescue. But it is also a record of a choice."

"What choice?"

"Whether to save one person when saving her might cost many."

## Story

Master Chief David Miller had been a SEAL for nineteen years. He had learned early that the body could endure far more than the mind believed, and that the mind could endure far more than the heart. He had also learned that the phrase *no man left behind* was not a slogan. It was a debt.

The call came at 0512. Lieutenant Okafor's beacon had settled in a dry valley six kilometres inland from a fishing village called Poshteh. Iranian Basij militia were searching the area. A helicopter extraction was impossible until after dark. The only way to reach her before the militia was on foot, fast, and quiet.

Miller chose four men: a medic, a linguist, a sniper, and a young petty officer who had never been in combat but who ran a sub-three-hour marathon. They boarded an MH-60 at the edge of the Nimitz's flight deck and flew nap-of-the-earth toward the coast, the rotor blades clipping the dawn.

They landed in a wadi three kilometres from the beacon. The heat was already rising. Miller checked his GPS, his weapon, and the faces of his men. He did not give a speech. Speeches wasted calories.

"We move. We find her. We bring her home. If we are compromised, we hide. If we cannot hide, we fight our way to the sea."

They moved in single file through terrain that looked empty but was not: goat herders with mobile phones, shepherds who saw everything and reported nothing, children who stared at the armed foreigners with the calm curiosity of people for whom war was weather.

Okafor was conscious when they found her, her shoulder dislocated, her helmet cracked. She had buried her parachute and was hiding in a thorn thicket.

"Took you long enough," she said.

"Traffic was bad," Miller replied.

The medic worked on her shoulder while the linguist scanned the ridgeline for movement. The sniper reported a pickup truck approaching from the village.

"Can she move?" Miller asked.

"She will have to."

They ran. Or they tried to. Okafor's arm was strapped to her side, and every step sent a jolt through her that made her bite her lip until it bled. Miller stayed beside her, matching her pace, one hand on her good arm.

The pickup truck found them at the wadi's mouth. Three militiamen, one with a rifle, two with pistols, young and frightened and shouting. Miller's team had them in a crossfire before the first word was finished.

"Hold," Miller said. "They are kids."

The militiamen saw the ambush too late. They dropped their weapons and ran back toward the village.

"We could have stopped them," the sniper said.

"Yes. And then we would have killed three boys for being scared. The debt is for one pilot, not the whole country."

They reached the extraction point at dusk, a rocky beach where a rigid-hulled inflatable boat waited with its engine burbling. Okafor collapsed into the boat. Miller counted his men twice.

As they pulled away from the shore, he made a note in his book: *Retrieved Storm. No shots fired. Three scared boys allowed to go home. I do not know if that was mercy or weakness. I know it was human.*

## Discussion

"They didn't kill them," the Young Correspondent said.

"No. And that choice will not appear in any after-action report. But it is the choice that determines what kind of war you are fighting."

"Did it matter? The boys might tell others."

"They might. Or they might remember that the enemy let them live. Either way, Miller made the war smaller for three people. That is not nothing."

The Archivist closed the notebook.

"Tomorrow we leave the battlefield and go where most wars are truly lost: among the people who did not choose it."
