# The Strait Closes

## Workshop

The Archivist spread a photograph of a supertanker's deck at sunset. Men in orange coveralls stood at the rails, looking toward a warship whose silhouette blocked the light.

"This is the Orion Pride," she said. "Two million barrels of crude. Forty-three souls. And, for three days, the centre of the world."

"A ship?"

"A hostage. The strait had closed in fact the moment the Panama Crown ran aground. This is the moment it closed in law."

## Story

Captain Thomas Vance had been at sea for thirty-two years. He had carried oil through piracy off Somalia, through typhoons in the South China Sea, through the slow, grinding loneliness of a life measured in ports he saw only from the bridge. He had never carried a cargo as dangerous as attention.

The *Orion Pride* was anchored five miles south of the burning *Panama Crown*, her engines stopped, her crew forbidden from moving by a voice on the VHF that claimed to speak for the Islamic Republic's Maritime Authority. There were thirty-one Indian, eight Filipino, two Ukrainian, and one British citizen aboard. They had enough food for ten days, enough fuel for the generators, and enough fear for a year.

Vance stood on the bridge wing and watched the Iranian patrol boat approach. It was small, but it carried a boarding party with automatic rifles and the nervous energy of men who had been told this was a great moment.

"Captain," his chief mate said. "They say we must follow them to Bandar Abbas."

"For what purpose?"

"They did not say."

Vance thought of his wife in Cornwall, his daughter at university in Manchester, the mortgage on a house he saw three months a year. He thought of the oil beneath his feet, worth more than all of them combined, and of the warships on the horizon that were pretending not to see him.

"Tell them we are a Panamanian-flagged vessel engaged in innocent passage. We will not resist, but we will not move until we receive written orders from a recognised authority."

The chief mate looked at him as if he had gone mad.

"Captain, they have guns."

"Yes. And if they use them on an unarmed tanker captain, the world will finally know what this war is about."

The boarding party came aboard anyway. They were young, poorly briefed, and frightened. Their officer, a lieutenant barely out of his teens, demanded the ship's log and the crew's passports. Vance gave them tea and the log. He kept the passports.

"You are not prisoners," the lieutenant said, contradicting the rifles in his men's hands.

"Then may we leave?"

"When the strait is reopened."

"Who will reopen it?"

The lieutenant had no answer. He was following orders from a ministry that was itself following orders from a committee that no longer met in the same room.

Meanwhile, the Truxtun held station twelve miles to the south. Commander Lin could see the *Orion Pride* on radar, a bright dot surrounded by smaller, hostile dots. She had orders not to intervene unless the crew's lives were in immediate danger. The definition of *immediate* was being argued in Washington in a language she did not speak.

"We cannot just watch," her operations officer said.

"We are not watching. We are waiting. There is a difference."

"For what?"

"For Tehran to make a mistake in front of the cameras. Or for us to make one."

In the end, the mistake came from below. A sailor on the Iranian patrol boat, smoking near a fuel drum, started a small fire. The patrol boat pulled away to deal with it. The boarding party, suddenly cut off from their ride, looked at Vance with the eyes of children who had followed a dog too far into the woods.

Vance gave them more tea and radioed the patrol boat that he would keep the lieutenant safe until they returned. The lieutenant, mortified, sat in the mess and drank.

That night, the Iranian Ministry announced that the Strait of Hormuz was closed to all traffic except humanitarian vessels under its escort. The world, already frightened, became more frightened still.

## Discussion

"He kept them safe," the Young Correspondent said. "The men who boarded him."

"Yes. That is the only victory in this chapter: not a battle won, but a cruelty avoided."

"Did it matter?"

"It mattered to the lieutenant. It mattered to Vance. In a war, small refusals are the only things people own."

The Archivist put the photograph away.

"Tomorrow, Washington acts. And action, in this story, is never as clean as decision."
