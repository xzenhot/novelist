# The Decisive Battle of the Chokepoint

## Workshop

The Archivist laid a radar printout on the table. It was a web of green arcs and hostile blips, annotated in red ink.

"This," she said, "is the battle the world watched. Two fleets, one strait, and a sky full of missiles."

"Who won?" the Young Correspondent asked.

"The side that did not lose. That is how most modern battles are won."

## Story

Commander Sarah Lin had not slept in forty hours. She had learned to think of her body as a machine that could be kept running with coffee, cold water, and the refusal to sit down. The Truxtun had become her world: steel corridors, the hum of turbines, the smell of coffee and fear.

The coalition plan was straightforward in the way that only simple plans are. The mine-clearing force would open a channel through the strait. Carrier aircraft would suppress the anti-ship missile sites. Surface ships would defend against air and small-boat attacks. Submarines would hunt Iranian diesel boats in the deeper water.

Simple. But no battle plan survives the first radar contact.

At 0600, the missile sites along the Iranian coast opened fire. They fired in volleys, trying to overwhelm the defences by sheer number. The Truxtun's combat systems came alive. Lin gave orders in a voice that sounded calm because she had trained it to sound calm.

"All stations, air defence. Engage inbound. Decoys away."

The ship shook. An SM-2 leapt from its cell, trailing fire. Another followed. The sky above the strait became a geometry of intercepts: missile meeting missile, fire meeting fire, mathematics deciding who lived.

On Qeshm Island, Captain Sharifi watched the engagement through a periscope camera feed. His own missile sites were firing on Tehran's orders, and he hated every launch. He knew the American systems. He knew the Chinese missiles. He knew the arithmetic: the Iranians would run out of missiles before the Americans ran out of interceptors.

"Sir," his aide said. "They want you to order the fast-attack boats in."

"That is suicide."

"They say it is sacrifice."

"Suicide and sacrifice share a uniform. Tell them I will not waste the boats until there is a target worth the cost."

He knew the order would be countermanded. It was. Within minutes, a second swarm of boats left the jetties, racing toward the minesweepers. The Truxtun shifted its fire to the surface threat. The air-defence umbrella thinned.

Lin felt the ship heel as the captain turned to bring all guns to bear. "CIWS engage. Helicopters to the surface contact line."

The Close-In Weapon System sounded like a chainsaw the size of a house. Tracer rounds stitched the water. One boat disintegrated. Another turned, burning. The rest pressed on.

Then the first Iranian submarine torpedo was detected. The Truxtun's sonar operators shouted coordinates. A British frigate dropped depth charges in a pattern that made Lin's teeth ache. The explosion underwater was a dull thud, not a roar.

"Subsurface contact neutralised," sonar reported.

By 1000, the anti-ship missile sites were silent. The fast-attack boats were retreating. The minesweepers had cleared a channel two hundred metres wide through the strait. The Panama Crown still burned, but ships could pass.

Lin stood on the bridge wing and looked at the sea. It was littered with debris: wood, fibreglass, the bright fragments of missiles. The water had been made into a graveyard by men who would never touch it.

Across the strait, Sharifi lowered his binoculars. He had not fired a shot himself, but he had given orders, and orders were a kind of gun. He thought of his son, of the lieutenant who had boarded the Orion Pride, of the boys in the boats who had not come back.

He wrote nothing. There was nothing to write that would not be rewritten by someone else.

## Discussion

"The strait was open," the Young Correspondent said.

"Yes. But not because anyone was defeated in the old sense. The machines ran out of missiles. That is a strange kind of victory."

"And the cost?"

"Ships, planes, lives, trust. And the knowledge that the next war will begin where this one left off."

The Archivist rolled up the radar printout.

"Tomorrow the guns stop. And the silence after the guns is the hardest chapter to write."
