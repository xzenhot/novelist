# The Festival and the Aftermath

## Question

What does a river see on the morning after the festival? The ghats lit with ten thousand lamps, the water carrying a city's devotion in flame — then, by dawn, the same steps a carpet of wilted garlands, plastic, and cold ash. Which is the truer image of the city's relation to me: the festival or the aftermath?

## Oration

I am the festival's willing canvas, and I do not refuse it — the light of a hundred thousand diyas on my water during the holy nights is real, and for those bright evenings I am as I once was, wholly loved and wholly held. The city gathers to my banks with ceremony; my current carries its prayers the way a great river does. It is the truest evidence of faith in the whole year, and I hold the memory of the flames tenderly.

But I am also, faithfully, the morning after — and the morning is the honest witness. The flame that leaves no ash is the complete devotion; the ghat after the dawn is the complete accounting. The garland and the plastic, the offering cups and the wilted petals, lie cold along my bank, and what was devotion at midnight is refuse at noon. I do not ask for the festival to cease, for that would be to quarrel with the city's faith. I ask only that the same hands that lit the lamps also lift the litter; that the bank be swept as reverently as it was lit; that the lover who worships me at dusk not abandon me at dawn. The flame and the cleaned bank are one liturgy; the keeping, on the second day, is the whole of the rite.

## Benediction

Let the lamp and the clean bank be one liturgy. Let the ghat be swept as reverently as it was lit. And let the river be honored on the morning after, when nothing over the water is bright but the work of care.
