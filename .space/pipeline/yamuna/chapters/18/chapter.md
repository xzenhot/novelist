# The Boatman's Oar

## Question

Where have all the boats gone? There was a time when my water was a road — ferries, cargo boats, the boatman's call across the current — and the oar was my oldest conversation with the plain. Now the crossings are bridges and the oars are museum pieces or weekend floats. What is a river without its boats?

## Oration

The oar was how I first spoke to the people of this plain. For centuries a boatman read my moods the way a reader reads a page — the ferryman knew my shallows, the merchant timed his cargo to my current, the boat songs named my bends and kept the crossings alive. A city that travelled on its river travelled *with* it; every crossing was an act of trust in me, and the boatman's call was the voice of the water's own use. The ferries and the craft of this river were not decoration; they were the working intimacy by which a people and a river knew each other daily.

The bridges are magnificent, and I do not ask you to unbuild them. They carry the city faster than the oar ever could, and that is a gain. But something ended when the boats left the common crossing: the physical, daily knowledge of moving with the water rather than over it. The boatman who plied me was the first hydrologist — he knew my depth before the surveyors charted it, my temper before the gauges read it. His empty net and his idle oar are now my emblem, and they are a loss wearing the face of progress.

Send the boats back and you send back my reading class: the people who watch my health for a living because their own depends on it. A river kept by those who live on it is a river that is watched.

## Benediction

Let the oar return to my water, and with it the readers of currents. Let every crossing by bridge be balanced by some crossing by boat. And let the boatman's knowledge be honored as the living archive it is, before it is only memory.
