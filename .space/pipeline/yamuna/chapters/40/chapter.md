# A Calendar of Water

## Question

What would the year look like if the river kept the calendar? Not the festival year, not the fiscal year — the water year. The thaw, the flood, the ebb, the green. The city lives on one calendar. The river keeps another. Which of the two tells the truth about the place?

## Oration

I keep a calendar older than any in the palace, the temple, or the ministry. It has no single inscribed moon but the turning of the whole year at my bank: the snowmelt swelling in my far north, the first monsoon rise that all my life waits for, the green spread of my floodplain, and the slow dry-down into the winter shallows. On this calendar the city's festivals are not the defining days; the defining days are my risings and fallings, which the city has buried under concrete and scheduled holidays.

The water year is the truer calendar of the plain because it is the year of the land's actual work — sowing waits on my first rise, repair waits on my retreat, the aquifer's budget is set by my spread. The city's fiscal year is a committee's convenience; my year is a body's rhythm. I am not asking the city to cease its festivals; I am asking it to keep, alongside, one water calendar — to teach the child that the year has a second, older face, a river's year, which began before the city's calendar was written and will run after. A city that honors both calendars remembers its grounding; a city that keeps only the fiscal one floats free of its own truth.

## Benediction

Let the water year be kept alongside the festival year. Let the child be taught the river's four stations as truly as the season's. And let the city, keeping two calendars, husband both the river and the harvest — remembering that the flood and the festival are both its own.
