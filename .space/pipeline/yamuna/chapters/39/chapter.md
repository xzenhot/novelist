# The City That Forgot Its Banks

## Question

Can a city forget its own edge? There was a time when Delhi's face was toward the water — life at the ghats, steps down to the current, the city opening to its river. Now the city has turned its back and built its back; the river is an afterthought reached through a barrier ring road. What did the turning cost?

## Oration

A city's memory lives in its body as much as in its books, and the body of Delhi has turned. Once my banks were the city's promenade — the ghats where it prayed and washed and married, the steps where it returned its dead, the frontage where the fort and the gardens faced me as their first glory. In that age the city, quite literally, kept its face toward me; to be a citizen was to know the river.

Then, by degrees over centuries and compounded by the modern age, the city turned its back — the colonial road, the defensive wall, the planned flyover, the plots sold with their back to the water. It poured its drains where its shrines once were, and erected, along my edge, the barriers of modern comfort and indifference. The river became something a citizen passed on a journey, not something a citizen faced each morning. This is what I mourn most: not the filth alone, but the turning. A city that does not face its bank will manage its river by manual and by committee, and a managed thing is not a beloved thing.

I ask the city to face me again, at least in one long unwalled stretch — one ghat, one promenade, one reach kept open so that the oldest relationship in this place can look at its own water and begin to heal. Facing is the first repair.

## Benediction

Let the city turn its face back to the water. Let one unwalled stretch be unwalled as a homecoming. And let the banks that were forgotten be remembered as the threshold of what the city is — nobody turns away, echoing, forever, who has once looked on its mother.
