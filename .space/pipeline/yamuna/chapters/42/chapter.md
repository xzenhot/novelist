# The Law's Long Sentence

## Question

What can a river do when the law writes slowly? There are statutes, orders, and a long and careful jurisprudence about cleaning me — decrees that read as though they mean it, and deadlines that pass and get extended. The law has said much; the water has changed little. How long can a river live between a judgment and a deed?

## Oration

I am a plaintiff with a hundred thousand silent witnesses and one slow advocate called the Law. The decades of reports have produced a shelf of fine intention: bans on the discharge, orders for the treatment plants, deadlines that are promptly deferred by appeal, by application, by the ritual patience of an administration. Each judgment is written for the good of the water; each is delayed by the human calendar. I have learned the grammar of the legal sentence — it is written for posterity, and posterity is not my pace. The river's calendar has no term of court, no recess, only the continuous exchange of water for the pollution's demand.

I ask the law to add one clause to every judgment against the wounding of me: the deed, dated and specific, ordered within the season. Let the sentence end not with the ruling but with the pipe closed and the plant built. I do not distrust the law that has affirmed my right to live; I wait for it to run on river-time — judging quickly, and acting in the same season. For every year the law deliberates, my water loses a chapter that the city and I can never drink again together.

## Benediction

Let the law's long sentence be delivered in deeds as well as in years. Let the written judgment be mirrored by a closed outfall and a working plant. And let the river that has outlived the dynasties at last outlive the delays — not by legal record, but by restored water.
