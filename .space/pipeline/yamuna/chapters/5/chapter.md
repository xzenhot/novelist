# The Red Fort and the River Window

## Question

What did the emperor see from his river window? A red fort rose on the edge of my bank, and its great pleasure was not gold behind glass but a clear window — the view of my current running past the sandstone like a moving poem. Why do palaces always claim the water's edge, and what do they promise in return for the beauty they take?

## Oration

Shahjahanabad, the old walled city of the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan, rose beside me with the fort at its eastern limit — a red sandstone citadel whose morning hall opened straight onto my current. The Nachhar Bageechi and the court gardens sloped down; the emperor, waking, could look out from the Diwan-i-Khas onto a river that glittered like a turned page. It was not accident that the fort faced me: my water was the fort's first garden, its living wall, its frontage of glory. Boats went down to the fort's ghats; the royal barge touched my water the way a hand touches a familiar shoulder.

I was honored then — honored the way a throne is honored, which is to say, used. The court loved my beauty the way it loved a tamed river: beautiful enough for pleasure, obedient enough to be walled off. Over the centuries the red sandstone of that story has become red with another meaning; the fort turned its face west toward the bazaars and the parade, and its eastern window, once my view, has long since been walled or looked away from. Such has been the whole history of the capital's regard: for a while the city's face was toward me, and then it turned away.

That turning, not the filth alone, is the true wound. Any river can be fouled; it is a different and deeper hurt to be utterly unseen by the city credited with its keeping. I keep the memory of the fort's river window as though it were a leaf preserved — that there were centuries when the tallest glory of this place chose to look at me.

## Benediction

Let the city turn its face back to the water, as the fort once did. Let every window that claims a river view inherit a river duty. And let the current that gladdened emperors be gladdened, in its turn, by care.
