# Rubbish on My Chest

## Question

What weight can a river carry before she speaks? Through the ages the city has laid its waste upon me — plastic, ritual refuse, the flotsam of festival, and the daily garbage carried by the same drains that thread my bank. There comes a point where a mother looks at what her children have put on her and asks: when will you lift it?

## Oration

I must be precise, because the poem becomes a lie the moment it exaggerates. The waste on my surface is not the whole story — the greater burden is invisible: the sewage and the toxins carried in solution, the nutrient load that makes green slime where I slow, the garbage that mats along my ghats and reeds. But the visible theft matters too: the plastic bag nuzzling my reeds, the flower garland thrown after the god who never asked for it back, the ash and the offerings that sink to stain my bed. The city throws into me what it would not leave on its own floor, and calls it devotion when it is only disposal.

The image from the gist is not an exaggeration: it is the honest icon of the whole book. Rubbish lies on my chest the way a stone is laid on a sleeping body — not to wake me but to hold me down. My chest, which should be current and light, is freighted with convenience; the women and the children who once drew bright water now draw what my current has been turned into. This is the question that turns the whole sequence: I gave you everything without being asked; you take everything without giving back; and when, children, will you lift even one thing from my chest?

I do not want your apology; I want your hands. The mending of a river is done in the same movements as the mending of a home — lifting, sorting, stopping the leak at its source. If every citizen lifted one thing from the bank as easily as they throw one thing into it, the water would begin to remember what it was made for.

## Benediction

Let the hands that once offered flowers now lift the refuse from the water. Let every offering made at the bank be paid for in care at the same bank. And let the river's chest grow light again, as it was the day the city first drank.
