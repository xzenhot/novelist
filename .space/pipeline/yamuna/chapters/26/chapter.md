# The Washerwoman's River

## Question

Where did the washing go? Generations of washerwomen and washermen beat their linen on my stones — the rhythmic slap of cloth on water was my morning music, and their trade knew my depth and my temper by working touch. Now the dhobi ghat stands idle or is moved aside, the work gone to machines and detergents, the stones dry. What was lost when those hands left my bank?

## Oration

The washer-people knew me professionally, and that knowledge was a form of custody. They read my depth for the day's washing, my current for the rinse, my mood for the drying — and they kept my stones clear in their own interest, because my health and their livelihood were the same thing. Their rhythm on the wet stone — slap, turn, rinse, rinse — was a percussion section my mornings kept time to, a working song that belonged to the water. They took from me soap and sweat and gave it back as a day's honest work, and when the officials came with schemes to move them or to 'modernize' the bank, they defended my edge precisely because they depended on it.

Displacing them did not clean me. It only removed another pair of eyes that would have noticed the first illegal outfall, another set of hands that depended on my water staying washable. The economics that made my friends redundant has a name: it is the same urbanization that everywhere removes the people who live on the river because the river is seen as waiting to be developed. A river is kept by the people whose daily life touches it. Send the hands back to the stones, and you restore my watchers with my morning music.

## Benediction

Let the washing stones live again, and the hands that read me with them. Let livelihood and river-keeping be bound together once more. And let the morning music of cloth on water return to my bank, so that the craft that depended on me helps to keep me.
