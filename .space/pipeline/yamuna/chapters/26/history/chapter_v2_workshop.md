# The Washerwoman's River

## Question

Where did the washing go? Generations of washerwomen beat linen on my stones — the rhythmic slap of cloth on water was my morning music. Now the dhobi ghat stands idle or displaced, the work gone to machines and detergent, the stones dry. What was lost with those hands' knowledge of me?

## Oration

The washerwomen knew me professionally. They read my depth for the day's work, my current for the rinse, my mood for the drying. Their slaps on the wet stone were a percussion section my mornings kept time to. They took from me and gave back nothing worse than soap and sweat — and they defended my stones when officials came with schemes, because their livelihood and my health were the same thing. Displacing them did not clean me; it only removed another pair of eyes that would have noticed the first illegal outfall. This poem mourns the economics that made my friends redundant. A river is kept by the people whose daily life touches her. Send the hands back to the stones, and they will keep watch better than any sensor.

## Benediction

May the washing stones live again, and the hands that read me with them. May livelihood and riverkeeping be bound together once more. And may the morning music of cloth on water return to my bank.
