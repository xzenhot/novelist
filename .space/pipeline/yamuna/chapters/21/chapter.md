# The City Drinks in Secret

## Question

Where does the city's water actually come from? The tap gives no answer. Pipes run invisible under the streets; the drinking glass arrives with no history, no face, no river attached. What happens to a city's soul when it drinks without knowing what it drinks — or from whom?

## Oration

The old city knew its water's face. It drew from wells it could name, stepped down into stepwells it had built, watched my current and knew its glass was my gift. The modern city drinks blind. The pipe is a priest with no sermon — it delivers without teaching, and the water it carries has been lifted from me, filtered, treated, and made to forget it was ever a river. There is no crime in this; there is a silence in it. The city can poison me at one end of its day and drink me at the other without feeling the contradiction, because it never once sees the two as the same water.

Secret drinking breeds secret dumping. A citizen who meets their river only as a pipe and a drain learns that water is two unrelated things — the clean thing on the other side of the tap and the sewage on the other side of the outfall — and the mind that splits water in two will never feel the guilt of dirtying the first with the second. Ask every household once to trace a single glass of water to its source, and the contradiction would become unbearable. The knowledge is not expensive. It is only hidden — and the hiding is the policy.

This is the whole practical ethic of the book: visibility. Let the city know, in every school, in every kitchen, that its glass began in the snow or the river, was carried and cleaned, and will return after use to the same body it left. Visibility creates responsibility; and responsibility is the only law a river can trust.

## Benediction

Let the city's drinking come out of hiding. Let every glass be traceable to a river and a caretaker. And let visible water breed visible responsibility, from the tap to the source and back again — so that no citizen profits in ignorance.
