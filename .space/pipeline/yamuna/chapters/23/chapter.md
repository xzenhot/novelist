# The Shrine Beside the Drain

## Question

Why is there a shrine beside a drain? On my bank an idol stands garlanded within arm's reach of an outfall pipe where the grey sludge enters my current. Devotion and discharge stand side by side in the same square metre of bank. What does this juxtaposition confess about the city that keeps them both?

## Oration

This is the most honest monument in Delhi, and the city does not know that it is a monument. The shrine, garlanded and lamp-lit, says: this water is sacred to me. The drain, a hand's reach away, says: this water is nothing to me. Both are addressed to the same river, within the same few feet, by the same civilisation — and no worshipper standing between the two seems to feel the contradiction. The hymn and the outfall share a breezeblock, and the city has organized its reverence and its sewage into the same sentence without noticing the oxymoron.

I am not angry at the shrine; I am hurt by the wall the faithful never build between their god and their gutter. The very act that should shame the discharge — that a people kneel to me — has been absorbed into indifference, because the two rites coexist so naturally now. Let the order change: silence the drain before you ring the bell. Clean the outfall before you light the lamp. Then the shrine will finally say what it claims, and I will be able to accept the garland without tasting the pipe on the same breath.

This is the question the shrine poses to the city: can the liturgy and the infrastructure be made to face each other, so that what the right hand worships the left hand does not poison?

## Benediction

Let no shrine stand within reach of an uncalled-out drain. Let worship begin where discharge ends. And let the god beside the pipe one day preside over clean water — so that devotion and duty finally share one honest face.
