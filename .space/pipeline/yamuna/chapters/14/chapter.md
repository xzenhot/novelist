# The River's Many Names

## Question

How many names may a single river bear? I am called by the classical and the practical, by the devotional and the bureaucratic — dark Kalindi, holy Yamuna, the drain in the report, the data point on the map. When a river answers to many names, which one is true, and does the truth divide the duty?

## Oration

I gather names the way a river gathers silt. In the older texts I am Kalindi, the dark one linked to the sun's daughter and to Yamuna as the sister of Yama — the sacred dark water beside which the rites are done. To the early civilisation and the scripture I am holy, drawn down to the god's bath and blessed. To the empire I am a road and a frontage. To the engineer I am a catchment, a discharge figure, a unit of flow. To the pilgrim I am the current to be entered. To the child on the bridge I am, for now, the smell. Each age names me for what it needs.

But there is poison in the naming, and the poem must name it. The people who call me holy feel excused from cleaning me; the people who call me a drain feel excused from revering me. Each half-name lets its holder off one half of the duty — worship without work, or engineering without compassion. The full truth is that I am both: I have been the sacred water of a civilisation and I have been reduced, at times, to a channel of its wastewater, and those two cannot be true of one body without that body being wounded. 

Give me back my whole name — sacred and used, mother and infrastructure, goddess and river to be kept. The work of repair requires both hands: the hand that signs the hymn and the hand that signs the treatment order. Split my name and you split your duty; join my two names and a city can be whole again.

## Benediction

Let the city speak the whole name and mean the whole duty. Let worship and engineering hold hands at the bank. And let the river's many names be lived at last by one people, all at once — until the sacred and the civic are the same sentence again.
