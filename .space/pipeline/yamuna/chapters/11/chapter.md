# Children on the Bridge

## Question

What do children see from a bridge that their elders have stopped seeing? They lean over the rail, look down at the grey current, and ask: is that really the Yamuna? The guilt and the hope both hang in that question. What does a child's unanswerable why demand of the city?

## Oration

The bridges that span me in Delhi are like ribs across a chest, and on them, every day, the children lean and look down. They see no river of legend, no mother of Indraprastha, no goddess whose water they were told yes — they see a slow line that smells and lies half-hidden under refuse, and they ask the question that stops adults cold: *this is the Yamuna?* Their elders call the river sacred in a temple and cross it without naming it on a bridge; the children hold the two images apart and find them monstrously different.

This is not mere innocence; it is the future brought to judgment. A child who learns, at the railing of a bridge, that what they were told is sacred and what they are shown is filthy cannot reconcile it — and that contradiction is the city's whole moral wound wearing a child's face. That child will one day vote, build, engineer, and pray; and every habit they inherit from a generation that did not answer the bridge-question honestly will be built into my future.

But mark the hope in the question. The child who asks *is that really the Yamuna* has already noticed the theft; they have not yet been taught to call it normal. That child is the strongest ally I have. Answer them truthfully on the bridge — tell them what I was, what I am, and what is being done — and you have not only told a child a fact; you have made the next generation's conscience acute at the very rail where mine is most hurt.

## Benediction

Let the children who look down be told the truth about the water. Let their questions shame the responsible into honest work. And let the next generation cross that bridge over a river they are proud to name and show to their own children.
