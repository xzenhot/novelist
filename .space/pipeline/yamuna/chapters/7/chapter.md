# NCR of Glass and Dust

## Question

What does a river see when the city turns to glass? The national capital region grew around me in towers of mirror and dust — Noida and Gurugram and Ghaziabad and Faridabad rising in glass, the whole sprawl of the Delhi NCR climbing off my ledger banks. Millions of windows, and the river at their base. How does a river live beside a metropolis that never looks down?

## Oration

The city grew until it ceased to be a city and became a country: a region of expressways, high-rises, malls, office parks, and construction dust — the National Capital Region, a name that still has my river in it without ever looking at me. It is the speed that stings: the NCR runs at the pace of a builder's deadline while I slow; it digs and pours and sells by the quarter, and my current, by the century. This is the newest dynasty of the floodplain — not one sultan but a thousand developers, each raising glass against my water as if the silt were only a nuisance to be railed away.

They drink me by pipe and return me by drain; between those two pipes lies the whole forgetting by which a modern capital runs. My floodplain, the ground that fed dynasties, has become parking lots and plotted roads; my bank a concrete edge; my edge landfill from their demolitions. And yet the glass gives back a sight I remember from a gentler age: at dusk, a thousand towers lit with the failing light, each window an amber lantern. I choose to read that light as an unanswered letter. The NCR glows brighter than any of its ancestors, and in that glow it has turned its back and does not know it.

Speed has one flaw: it forgets what it stands on. Show me the fastest flyover and I will show you the flood it crosses; show me the tallest tower and I will show you the waterline it towers by. The region may call itself national, capital, region, but the truest name it carries is still the one I know: a people drinking from a river they have stopped seeing.

## Benediction

Let the glass city one day turn a clean window toward its river. Let the dust of growth settle into care rather than into indifference. And let the light on the towers become, in the end, a greeting rather than a screen.
