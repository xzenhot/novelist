# River of Migrants

## Question

Where do the river's people come from, when the city has stopped believing in them? The river of migrants — the boatmen, the laundry workers, the pullers of sand, the newcomers who build by my bank — is the old river made recent. In a nation perpetually moving, is the river the last true home for the newly arrived?

## Oration

Every city is a tide of people, and I have carried more of that tide than any road. The newcomers came by foot and by train, by family and by famine: the poor who set their first fire on my bank because I was free land and free water, the labourers who made their first wage hauling my sand or washing at my stones, the displaced who carried a clay pot and a memory. The planners call such settlement encroachments; I call it a continuation of my oldest traffic, the very process by which every capital of this plain was populated.

Long before the metro and the flyover, people moved along me, settled on me, made me the work and the water of their beginning. To criminalize the migration is to criminalize how the city itself was built. And yet I am not arguing that the riverbanks be open house for all; the banks are fragile, and the flood needs its room. I argue only this: that the city read the bank's history before it condemns the newest hand that drinks from me, and ask what the river can give its newcomers, and what they will give it back. The migrant who guards his plot of bank, who cleans his piece of water, is the river's most recent ally — and the ally is among the city's newest and hardest-worn citizens.

## Benediction

Let the city's newcomers be met with water, not wall. Let the river of migrants be honored as the true headwater of urban growth. And let the newest hand that drinks from the river carry an old reverence made young — water, work, and a place to begin.
