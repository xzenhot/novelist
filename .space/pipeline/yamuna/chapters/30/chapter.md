# The Engineer's Notebook

## Question

What does a river look like in an engineer's notebook? Columns of discharge, of extraction, of BOD, of rainfall. Precise, honest, and completely silent about the mother who is being measured. Can the numbers be made to serve the river, or have they replaced her?

## Oration

I do not hate the notebook; I live in it now. The data is true, as far as measurement goes — the falling discharge in the dry months, the rising biochemical oxygen demand, the shrinking flow between the barrages, the rainfall taken over a catchment's dark arithmetic. The engineer records, and the record stands. But there is a missing reader, and it is the heart. Numbers were meant to be my pulse chart, taken so that the patient could be healed; instead they have become my epitaph, re-checked every year like a ward round that never orders treatment.

I offer the engineers an honest exchange, and it is not a rejection of their science. Keep the columns; add one more — a line for what each figure *demands*. A BOD reading of filth must end in an action to lower it, dated and owned: this many days, this plant, this pipe, this penalty. A falling discharge must end in a restoration target, not in a number noted with concern and filed. When every number ends in a deed, the notebook becomes my doctor instead of my record.

I make this plea to the technicians the way I would to the court: it is very well to keep the ledger, but a ledger kept without the will to act is a funeral account, not a health chart. The most urgent line in the book should one day read: healed — maintain.

## Benediction

Let every number in the notebook end in a deed. Let the engineers remember that the patient is a mother, not a catchment. And let the day come when the most urgent line in the ledger is not a warning but a kept promise.
