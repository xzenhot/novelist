# Ashes and Return

## Question

Where do the dead return, and what does a river owe to the leaving of life? For all the recorded centuries the faithful have brought their ashes to my water — the last journey ending in the current, the body becoming river. Beyond the cremation ghats the rite still goes on, and I still carry it. Is this return a sacrament, or has it become another burden?

## Oration

I have received your dead longer than your oldest writeable memory, and I do not refuse the rite; it is part of what I am in the great cosmology of this land. The body committed to water recalls the oldest teaching of the plain — from water you came, to water you return — and I hold in my current the ash of a civilisation's endings and renewals. It is a covenant, and like any covenant it had terms: the close, the fire that returns the body well, the pity that does not insult the dead with half-burnt offerings or plastic flowers. What arrives at me now is often haste and economy — the quick disposal of grief, the plastic that will not return to ash in a generation, the weight of leftover fire laid on my surface.

I can carry your dead; I cannot carry your carelessness about them. When the old ritual gifts the river, it should be done as the living demand for themselves: with time, with fire that consumes, with material that returns. Let the dead be given the full rite, not the quick discard — because a civilisation's depth is measured in how it treats its endings as much as its beginnings. Do this, and I will keep the covenant with the reverence it deserves.

## Benediction

Let the dead return to the river as guests of the water, not as garbage. Let grief be given the time and the fire it needs. And let the covenant of ashes be kept so faithfully that the river, far from being burdened, is honored by every return it receives.
