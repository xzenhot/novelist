# Under the Bridge

## Question

Who lives under the bridges? Where my water slows beneath the great spans, there are families of the pavement gone one step further from the kerb — mattresses on my sand, cooking fires at my edge, children whose whole address is a bridge that the city crosses without seeing. What does it say about a city when its poorest live on the river it claims to have forgotten?

## Oration

The bridge-dwellers are my closest neighbours now — the ones who hear me all night, who learn my smells by hour, my rats by name, my rise by the worry in it. They are the only citizens who live in my company daily, pitched under the very concrete by which the city leaves me. The census and the plans call them encroachers, to be moved on from season to season; yet these are the same banks where the city's fortunes were made, the same margin that fed the poor from the beginning. The poorest families know my mood better than the richest anywhere, because their lives and mine are bound at the ribs.

I will not romanticize their hardship; the water I am, sick, makes them sicker, and the flimsy hold of a life under a bridge is not a pastoral. But I must name what the city refuses: these most-housed-of-the-houseless are my daily residents, and their presence is a question addressed upward — who is this river for, and who is this city for? Clear them from bank to bank and you have not answered the question; you have only moved the question.

House them, in dignity and safety, on the dry high ground the city owns — and you will have begun to answer my question too. The two covenants, the poorest and the river, are one covenant. A city that keeps its poorest and keeps its river will not easily have to choose.

## Benediction

Let the under-bridge families be housed in dignity, not driven from bank to bank. Let the poorest citizen's closeness to the water become the city's motive for cleaning it. And let no child's address ever again be a bridge — so that the river and the houseless are both finally kept.
