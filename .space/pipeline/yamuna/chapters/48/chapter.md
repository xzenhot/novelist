# Clean Water Is a Verb

## Question

Is clean water a thing to be bought, or a deed to be done? The city speaks of "clean water" as a product — delivered by a plant, measured in a report, targeted in a budget. But water is clean only in the doing: the reuse, the return, the care renewed every day. Can the city learn that cleanliness is a verb, never a finished noun?

## Oration

You will not arrive at clean water by owning it. A lake is clean because you keep it so; a river is clean because the daily habit of the city keeps her so. The verb is everything: I am cleaned by the reed that is planted, by the outfall that is shut, by the foam that is skimmed, by the bank that is not paved, by the treatment plant that is not only built but run, year after year. The noun — "a target of clean water," enshrined in a document and reported quarterly — is a monument to an absent action, a promise kept on paper and broken in the current.

I ask the city to change its grammar as much as its engineering. Say, in every budget and in every child's lesson, that water is something we are always doing. Clean water is a verb that never ends; it is renewed in every rain, in every plan that holds, in every plant that runs. Grammar this change, and the deeds will begin to conjugate accordingly. A city that treats cleanliness as a verb is a city that will never be finished with caring.

## Benediction

Let clean water be spoken as a deed, not a thing. Let the city do the verb daily, in season and out. And may the river be alive with the doing — a cleanliness that is never once-and-achieved, but forever and again.
