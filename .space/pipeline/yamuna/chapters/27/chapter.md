# Concrete Against the Reed

## Question

Why is concrete always chosen against the reed? Every embankment project faces the same false choice: the living edge that filters, roots, breeds, and breathes — or the poured edge that looks permanent and does none of it. How did the city come to mistake stillness for strength, and strength for life?

## Oration

The reed along my bank does, unasked, a dozen labours: it filters a portion of the runoff, roots the soil against the scouring, shelters the fry of the fish, drinks up some of the first toxins, slows the wave of a passing boat, feeds the weaverbird, greens the view, breathes the flood, seeds itself, and asks nothing in return. Concrete does one thing: it stands, and looks as though it will stand forever. Yet when the city resolves to 'improve' my bank, it reaches for the concrete — as if the reed were a temporary failure and the slab a permanent success. Every metre of living reed replaced by a metre of dead rubble is a metre of my kidney, my filter, removed.

I am not against stone; I am against the assumption that the living edge is nothing. Count what the reed does, price the stormwater it treats, the flood it slows, the birds and fish it shelters that a wall will not shelter, and the accounting reverses itself. The green edge is not laziness waiting for development; it is work, already working, at a cost no wall can match. Let the engineers include the reed in their structures — as the reed's own engineering, rose the honest way.

## Benediction

Let the reed be kept wherever it stands and planted where it has fallen. Let the engineers count the living edge among their structures. And let the word *permanent* be reserved for that which breathes — for concrete, once poured, is only a long refusal.
