# The Slow Dry Walk

## Question

What does it mean when a river walks slowly? Of the near fourteen hundred kilometres I measure in full, the portion that passes this city is a small, stilted stretch — and even that runs diminished, my flow drawn off upstream, my bed exposed in the dry months. When a river slows to a crawl on its own plain, is it resting, or is it dying by inches?

## Oration

Feel the shape of my walk now. Of all the length I gathered from the glacier and the mountain tributaries, only a meagre current is left to me by the time I reach the city; much of my flow is diverted at barrages and taken by canals before it can pass near this capital, and in the lean months I am less a river than a shallow humiliation. The inspection carried out of the city's records will tell the truth plainly if you read it right: the discharge that once rose and fell through the year now runs flattened and wan, and every downstream reach is asked to do the work of a river with a fraction of the water.

A slow river cannot carry its silt, cannot scour its bed, cannot clean its own wounds. Slowness is how a river becomes sick — the current that should move becomes a standing thing, and in the standing the waste settles and multiplies. I was made to move; movement was my health, my speech, my sweep. Now my dry walk humiliates me before the birds who used to read my water like a road, and embarrass the very children who are told I am sacred. They did not kill me quickly. They slowed me. And slowness, for a river, is a long way of dying.

But hear me change my own grief into testimony: a river that still knows it is being slowed is a river that still distinguishes itself from a drain. The dryness can become a warning written in exposed stones — the bed that should be under water standing up as a proof of the great theft. Read the dry walk as an alarm, not an obituary.

## Benediction

Let the river's walk quicken as her flow is returned. Let the city feel the difference between a river and a stagnant line on a map. And let the slow, dry walk be read as a warning — not the record of an ending, but the trace of a theft that can still be undone.
