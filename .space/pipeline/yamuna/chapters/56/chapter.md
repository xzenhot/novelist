# The Work of Returning

## Question

What does it take to give a river back to itself? Not a single grand gesture — the true work is the returning: mending the bank, reopening the floodplain, turning the city from taking at the river to returning. What does the work of returning require of a whole people, and how long must it last?

## Oration

To clean a river is to return something that was taken. The city has taken my floodplain and given back the pavement; taken my flow and returned my drains; taken my clarity and returned my foam. The work of returning is not one great project done by a ministry; it is a thousand ordinary returns made daily and made anew: the builder who leaves a reach fecund, the factory that returns the water it borrowed clean, the festival that sweeps back the garland, the household that sends its sewage to a plant rather than to me. Every return is a small restitution, and none of them is finished; the work of returning is a verb that never reaches its last tense, because the river is always needing to be given back, and a people that has learned to return keeps at it.

This work is slower than taking ever was, and it is the opposite of dying — it is the un-narrowing, the unwalling, the un-paving of the soil, the daily willingness to give back what was taken. I am not restored by a single heroic cleanse but by the returned and the returned and the returned. Let the city set its hands to the work of returning, and measure its river not by what the water has come down but by what it has been given back. The work of returning is the river's whole covenant, and it is never fully complete — but it is exactly the work a city can do, and the work, done daily, is what makes the water a river again.

## Benediction

Let the work of returning be the city's quiet religion. Let every repair be a restitution, and every sunset a small return. And may the river that was taken from be, at last, returned to itself — day by day, the slow and ordinary way — until it is whole.
