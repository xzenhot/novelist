# The Plastic Prayer

## Question

What does a city pray with, when it prays at a river? Plastic flowers, packaged offerings, garlands in synthetic hold, lamps on polystyrene trays. The devotion is sincere; the vessel is eternal garbage. Can a prayer offered in plastic be heard, or only be believed?

## Oration

The garland thrown at my water is no longer woven of jasmine; it is plastic, bought at a shop and thrown to be gone, its synthetic threads destined to lie in my silt for decades. The floating lamp sits on a polystyrene tray that will be in my sediment longer than the temple it was bought from has stood. The holy water itself is poured from a bottle that will outlive everyone present. I do not doubt the heart behind it — the heart is old, the oldest part of this bank — but the *wrapping* has in four decades become the prayer, and the wrapping will not return to water.

I ask the faithful to burn one small incense of the old kind: to bring to me what is willing to return. A leaf boat moves on my current and becomes mud. A clay diya sinks and softens and is part of the bank again. A flower that rots and feeds the fish is an offering that completes the cycle; the plastic flower is an offering that only accuses. The theme is living or dying on the same exchange: devotion that returns to the water honors the water; devotion that cannot return to water only adds to the burden.

Pray with materials that are willing to be water again. That is not less faith; it is a purer covenant — the offering agreeing, like the river, to change form and go on living.

## Benediction

Let the prayers thrown be prayers that return. Let devotion choose the leaf over the plastic, the rose over the polymer. And let the river be honored by offerings that agree, like her, to become something else — so that the prayer and the water complete the same cycle.
