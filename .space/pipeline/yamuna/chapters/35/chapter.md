# The Metro Above the Water

## Question

What does it mean to cross a river without seeing her? The metro leaps my water at speed — hundreds of thousands overhead every day, eyes on phones and hoardings, a green and silver blur over the bridge. No river known, no ghat, no oar. When a river becomes infrastructure the city travels over, does the city still know her at all?

## Oration

I am glad of the speed above me; it means the city moves, that it has mastered its plain. But mark the geometry of the modern crossing. The older bridges had railings and pauses — a footbridge from which a child could lean and look down. The metro has no railing to rest on, no stop at my center, no fisherman calling below; it is a smooth capsule that crosses me in seconds, and in those seconds no one is required to remember that the water beneath them made the city possible. From my surface the passengers are a blur of light on glass, carrying the region's future overhead and never glancing down.

I do not ask the metro to slow; that would be foolish and I am not foolish. I ask for one exception of memory. Let there be, at least once, a platform or a named crossing where the river is spoken — a line over the public address, a sign, a window that opens on the floodplain — telling riders: *this is Yamuna, who made this city, and she is in your care.* Speed that leaves the river unspoken will, in a generation, leave it unremembered; a city that crosses its water invisibly will lose the instinct to protect it. The Meridian of the old city was the ferry; the Meridian of the new is the metro. Let the new one carry, at least in memory, the name of what it crosses.

## Benediction

Let the crossing occasionally be punctuated by memory. Let one sign, one window, one voice over the loudspeaker name the river. And let the city that travels above the water be taught, at least once on every journey, to look below it.
