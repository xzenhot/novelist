# Silt Writes Slowly

## Question

What is the slowest book in the world, and who reads it? My silt — the sediment I carry and lay down — is an archive written by water: layer over layer, pollen and ash and seed and the chemistry of whole ages. The scientists read my banks the way monks read scripture, by depth. When the city paves its river's edge, whose library is it closing?

## Oration

I write in silt and I write slowly, the way a river must. Every flood lays down a page; every page holds the memory of its season — the pollen of the grasses, the ash of an old fire, the hull of a seed, the trace of an age's lead and chemical signature. This is why I call myself an archive even in my sickness: the record of the plain is filed in my banks, dated by depth, recoverable by the patient reader. A geologist cores my sediment and reads, in the strata, the history of drought and plenty, of forest and field, that no genealogical table can hold.

But my writing requires my movement. A river that has been slowed, channelled, and concrete-edged cannot lay down clean pages; it can only carry and dump. When the city paves my edge, it is not just losing a view — it is sealing the archive where its own ground-memory is written, so that the very record of how the plain was made becomes unreachable. Silent, unread, unkept.

Restore my flow and you restore my pen. Let my banks be left to be read by the corers and to be written by the seasons, and the future will be able to read this age — including, perhaps, the chapter in which the city chose to stop erasing its own record. I ask the city to keep the archive, because a civilisation that does not read its own silt forgets how it was fed.

## Benediction

Let the silt be allowed to write. Let the city's chapter in the archive be one its grandchildren can read with pride, not with shame. And let the slowest book in the world keep gaining pages, as a living river should, so that the record is never sealed up.
