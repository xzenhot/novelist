# A Blessing At The Riverbank

## Question

What changes in the poet when A Blessing At The Riverbank reveals Dolly not as an object of possession, but as a living threshold between New Delhi and the remembered Yamuna?

## Oration

Beloved Dolly, the city does not know how softly it changes when your name passes through it. New Delhi continues with its vendors calling, its balconies holding little rectangles of evening, its buses breathing heat, its wires carrying the impatient speech of many houses, yet within that ordinary unrest the poet sees a second river move. It is not the Yamuna as a map can name it, not only the old water beside ghats and fields, but a quieter current inside attention, where beauty arrives and asks to be honored without being captured. The topic of this poem, A Blessing At The Riverbank, becomes such a bank of seeing, and the poet stands there with dust on his shoes and restraint beginning like cool water in his palms.

He remembers the image that first wounded and healed him: a woman of New Delhi shining through the mind like Radha near the Yamuna, a pitcher resting in her lap, clay against cloth, silence against the trembling of praise. Yet he must be careful, for every sacred comparison can become a theft if it forgets the living person before it. Dolly is not made smaller by his devotion; she is not a painted figure borrowed for his hunger. She has her own morning, her own errands, her own laughter, her own refusals, her own inward weather. Therefore the poet lowers his voice and lets the metaphor kneel. Radha is a lamp behind the seeing, but Dolly is the flame that cannot be owned.

The theme that moves beneath this chapter is The Beloved Necessity, the lesson of accepting distance as part of devotion. It enters through thread, wind, seed, yoke, not as doctrine but as image. If the theme is a chamber, then the poet must enter it alone and leave the beloved unbound outside its door. If it is a river, then he must learn from water, which touches the bank and moves on, giving shape without demand. If it is a lamp, then he must understand that light does not keep what it reveals. So the poem asks him to become less noisy inside himself, to let desire lose its sharpness, to let praise become a clear vessel rather than a net.

In the imagined Yamuna the pitcher is full, but not overflowing; it teaches measure. Clay was once earth without form, then pressure, wheel, hand, heat, and patience gave it a hollow strong enough to carry water. So too the poet must be shaped. His first feeling is only wet earth, bright and unstable; his song must pass through discipline before it can be worthy of Dolly's name. He cannot spill adjectives over her and call that reverence. He cannot mistake astonishment for love. He must learn the grammar of distance, the courtesy of silence, the brave poverty of a gaze that asks for nothing in return.

Around him, Delhi keeps speaking. A tea stall lifts steam like a small offering; a metro window catches a face and loses it; a temple bell is swallowed by traffic and returns as memory; rain darkens the pavement until every headlight trembles like a broken star. These things do not distract from Dolly, they teach him how to see her rightly. The city says, everything passes. The river says, everything returns changed. The pitcher says, what you carry must not be wasted. Her silence says, do not write me into a room where I did not choose to stand. Her beauty says, be awake, but do not be greedy with your waking.

Therefore the poet makes of A Blessing At The Riverbank not a monument but a prayer in motion. He lets the lines breathe as a song might breathe, with refrain and return, with wonder moving toward humility. Again he sees the lap, the pitcher, the river-light; again he sees New Delhi, modern and restless, refusing to become a painted village for his convenience. Between the two, Dolly walks as herself. The poet's task is not to solve this radiance, but to remain honest before it. He may love the image, but he must bless the person. He may remember the myth, but he must protect the human face from the violence of comparison.

So let the oration turn inward. What is beauty for, if not to cleanse the one who witnesses it? What is longing for, if not to discover the boundaries it must not cross? What is a poem for, if not to return the beloved to freedom more luminous than before the poem began? The poet answers by giving up the louder claim. He places the word Dolly beside Yamuna, beside Radha, beside pitcher, beside Delhi dust, and he does not close his fist. He lets the words remain open. He lets them carry water only as long as water wishes to be carried.

When this poem ends, it must not end as possession. It must become a small clearing through which the next poem, The Last Question Of Desire, can be approached with a cleaner heart. Let the poet leave behind one excess, one vanity, one secret demand. Let Dolly remain beyond the page, alive in her own day. Let the Yamuna continue inside him as conscience. Let the pitcher in her lap become the measure of his speech: full enough to nourish, humble enough to hold, quiet enough not to boast of its own holiness. In that measure, the poem finds its blessing, and the poet learns to love as one who guards the river he cannot own.

## Benediction

Let the closing blessing leave Dolly free, the poet humbled, and the reader carried toward 'The Last Question Of Desire' with a quieter, cleaner gaze.
