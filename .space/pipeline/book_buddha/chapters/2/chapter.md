# Chapter 2 — The Great Renunciation

## Section 1 — Workshop

Maya and Arjun sat again at the low table. Outside, the afternoon had turned grey, and a soft rain tapped against the window.

"You said he left," Arjun said. "But I keep thinking about what he left behind. A wife. A child. A kingdom. How does a man walk away from all of that?"

"That is the question the story asks," Maya said. "And the answer is not that he did not love them. It is that the question had become larger than everything else. He could not stay, because to stay was to turn away from the truth he had seen."

Arjun shook his head. "It still seems cruel."

"Perhaps," Maya said. "But the story does not ask you to approve. It asks you to understand. Listen."

## Section 2 — Story

In the dead of night, the prince rose from his bed and went to the door of the chamber where his wife and his newborn son lay sleeping. He stood there a long time, looking at them, and he did not wake them, for he knew that if he looked into their eyes he would not be able to leave.

He called his charioteer, and together they rode out of the city, through the sleeping streets, to the edge of the forest. There the prince dismounted, and he cut off his long hair with his sword, and he put on the simple robes of a mendicant, and he sent the charioteer back with his jewels and his horse.

"Go," he said, "and tell my father that I have not left in anger, but in search of the truth. If I find it, I will return."

And so the prince went out into the world as a seeker, with nothing but the robes on his back and the question in his heart.

He studied with the great teachers of his time, and he mastered their doctrines, but none of them answered his question. He practiced the most severe austerities, starving his body until he was near death, until his ribs stood out like the rafters of a ruined house, and still the answer eluded him.

At last, near death from his austerities, the prince understood something that changed everything: neither indulgence nor self-mortification leads to awakening. He accepted a bowl of rice-milk from a village girl, and in that simple act he found the **Middle Way** — the path between the extremes of pleasure and pain.

He sat down beneath a great tree, the Bodhi tree, and resolved not to rise until he had found the truth.

## Section 3 — Discussion

Arjun was quiet. "The middle way," he said. "He had to nearly kill himself to find it."

"Yes," Maya said. "The middle way is not the easy way. It is the way found only after you have tried both extremes and seen that neither works. The prince had to exhaust both pleasure and pain before he could find the path between them."

"And the bowl of rice-milk?" Arjun asked. "That small thing. It seems so ordinary."

"It was ordinary," Maya said. "That is the point. The truth is not found in the extraordinary. It is found in the ordinary, in the simple act of accepting what is given. The bowl of rice-milk was the prince's first step back to life."

Arjun nodded slowly. "And then he sat under the tree."

"And then he sat under the tree," Maya said. "And that is where the story reaches its turning. But that is for tomorrow."

"Tell me," Arjun said.

"Tomorrow," Maya said. "For now, sit with the middle way. Let it be uncomfortable. The discomfort is the beginning of the awakening."

Arjun nodded. "I'll be here."

Maya smiled. "I know you will."
