# Introduction

## Section 1 — Workshop

The room was quiet, lit by a single lamp, and the two of them sat across a low table as the evening gathered outside. The older of the two, a woman named **Maya**, had the calm, unhurried manner of someone who had spent many years listening. The younger, a man named **Arjun**, sat with his hands folded, restless, as if he had come a long way to ask something he could not yet put into words.

"You came," Maya said.

"I did," Arjun said. "I've been reading about the Buddha. Everyone knows the name, but I don't think I know the man. I want to understand what actually happened — what he did, and why it still matters."

Maya nodded slowly. "Then let us begin at the beginning. Not with the teaching, but with the man — a prince who had everything, and who walked away from all of it."

"Everything?" Arjun said. "Why would anyone do that?"

"That," Maya said, "is the whole of the story. Now — let me tell it to you."

## Section 2 — Story

In the kingdom of the Shakyas, at the foot of the Himalayas, a prince was born, and the wise men said he would be either a great king or a great sage. His father, the king, fearing the prophecy, built a palace of pleasure around the boy, so that he would never see the sorrow of the world and would choose the throne.

The prince grew in luxury, surrounded by music and beauty, shielded from age, sickness, and death. He married, and a son was born to him, and for twenty-nine years he knew nothing of the world's suffering. But a restlessness grew in him — a question the palace could not answer, a longing for something the pleasures could not give.

One day the prince rode out beyond the palace walls, and he saw four things that shattered his world: an old man, bent and broken by age; a sick man, racked by disease; a corpse, being carried to the pyre; and a wandering ascetic, serene and at peace. For the first time, the prince saw that all beings are subject to old age, sickness, and death — and that there might be a way beyond them.

The question took hold of him and would not let go: *Why do we suffer, and can suffering be ended?*

And this is where the story truly begins — not with an answer, but with a question that would not let the prince go.

## Section 3 — Discussion

Arjun was still. "The four sights," he said. "I've read about them. But I never felt them until now. He had everything, and he still saw that it wasn't enough."

"Most people see the same four sights every day," Maya said. "They just look away. The prince did not look away."

"And the question?" Arjun asked. "Why do we suffer — does it ever get answered?"

Maya looked at him with something like tenderness. "That is the whole of the story. But the answer is not what you think. It is not a word you can hold. It is a thing you must become."

"Then tell me the rest," Arjun said.

"Tomorrow," Maya said. "For now, sit with the question. Let it be uncomfortable. The discomfort is the beginning."

Arjun nodded slowly. "I'll be here."

Maya smiled. "I know you will."
# Chapter 1 — The Sheltered Prince

## Section 1 — Workshop

Maya and Arjun sat again at the low table. The morning light came through the window in a long, pale slant, and the dust moved slowly in it.

"Did you sit with the question?" Maya asked.

"I tried," Arjun said. "But I kept thinking about the palace. About how a man could have everything and still feel that something was missing."

"That is the first thing the story teaches," Maya said. "The palace was not a place. It was a way of not seeing. And the prince, for all his luxury, was a prisoner of it."

Arjun frowned. "A prisoner? He had every pleasure."

"No," Maya said. "He had every pleasure except the truth. Listen."

## Section 2 — Story

The prince grew in the palace of pleasure, and the king saw to it that no shadow of suffering ever fell across his son's path. The gardens were always in bloom, the music never ceased, and the servants were chosen for their youth and beauty, so that the prince would never see a face marked by age or grief.

But the prince was not content. A restlessness grew in him, a question the palace could not answer, a longing for something the pleasures could not give. He would sit at the window and watch the birds fly beyond the walls, and he would wonder what lay outside.

And so, one day, he asked his father to let him ride out into the city. The king, reluctant but unable to refuse, ordered the streets to be swept and the sick and the old to be kept from sight. But the prince saw what the king could not hide.

He saw an old man, bent and broken by age, and he asked his charioteer, "What is this?" And the charioteer answered, "That is old age, which comes to all."

He saw a sick man, racked by disease, and he asked, "What is this?" And the charioteer answered, "That is sickness, which spares no one."

He saw a corpse, being carried to the pyre, and he asked, "What is this?" And the charioteer answered, "That is death, which is the end of all."

And then he saw a wandering ascetic, serene and at peace, and he asked, "What is this?" And the charioteer answered, "That is one who has left the world to seek the truth."

The prince returned to the palace, but he was not the same. The four sights had shattered his world, and a question had taken hold of him that would not let go: *Why do we suffer, and can suffering be ended?*

## Section 3 — Discussion

Arjun was quiet for a long moment. "He saw what everyone sees," he said. "But he didn't look away."

"No," Maya said. "He did not look away. And that is the difference between a prince and a seeker. The prince saw the four sights and could not unsee them."

"And the ascetic?" Arjun asked. "The one who was at peace. That was the seed, wasn't it? The hint that there might be a way out."

"Yes," Maya said. "The ascetic was the fourth sight, and the most important. The first three showed the prince the problem. The fourth showed him that there might be a solution."

Arjun leaned forward. "And so he left?"

"So he left," Maya said. "But not yet. First, he had to understand what he was leaving, and why. That is the next part of the story."

"Tell me," Arjun said.

"Tomorrow," Maya said. "For now, sit with the four sights. Let them be uncomfortable. The discomfort is the beginning of the turning."

Arjun nodded. "I'll be here."

Maya smiled. "I know you will."
