# Chapter 5 — The Echo

## Section 1 — Workshop

Maya and Arjun sat again at the low table. It was the last evening, and the lamp burned steady between them.

"The echo," Arjun said. "What remains after the teacher is gone."

"Yes," Maya said. "Every story has an ending, and this is the ending of the Buddha's. But it is also a beginning. Listen."

## Section 2 — Story

At the age of eighty, the Buddha lay down between two sal trees at Kushinagar, and his disciples gathered around him, weeping. And the Buddha said to them, "Do not grieve. All compounded things are subject to decay. Strive on with diligence."

And then he passed into **parinirvana** — the final, complete peace.

But the teaching did not die. It lived on in the Sangha, in the millions who walked the path, in the light that one man's inward journey had kindled for all mankind.

The teaching spread across India and, in time, across the world — east to China and Japan, south to Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia, west to the lands beyond. It transformed the way human beings live and think, a reinvention of philosophy itself, from speculation about the cosmos to the practical discipline of ending suffering.

And the question that had haunted the prince — *Why do we suffer, and can suffering be ended?* — was answered, not with a word, but with a way.

What remains is not the man, but the awakening — the truth that suffering can be ended, and that the way is found within. And this story is told so that the reader may remember: that one of the most wonderful things that ever happened to mankind was a prince who turned inward, and in turning inward, transformed the world.

## Section 3 — Discussion

Arjun was silent for a long time. Then he said, softly, "Strive on with diligence. Those were his last words."

"Yes," Maya said. "Not a grand pronouncement. Not a mystery. A simple instruction. Strive on. The path is not finished. It is walked, every day, until the end."

"And the echo?" Arjun asked. "What remains?"

"The echo is the teaching," Maya said. "The light that one man kindled, and that millions have carried. The Buddha is gone, but the awakening is not. It lives in every person who turns inward, who asks the question, who walks the path."

Arjun looked at the lamp, and then at Maya. "And the question? Why do we suffer?"

Maya smiled. "You know the answer now. Suffering can be ended. And the way is found within."

Arjun was quiet. Then he said, "Thank you. For the story."

"Thank you," Maya said, "for listening. The story is not finished. It is yours now. Carry it well."

Arjun nodded, and rose, and went out into the night — carrying the silence within, as the prince had carried it, as all who walk the path have carried it, from that first dawn under the Bodhi tree to this very evening.

And the echo went on.
